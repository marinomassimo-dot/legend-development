## Decision

1. DATO — In children given vigabatrin for infantile spasms the brain-MRI abnormality is
   common and reproducible: 22.1 percent of 172, 32.5 percent of 77, 28.1 percent of 64 and
   44.7 percent of 76 imaged children across four independent cohorts (rows B1-01, B1-13,
   B1-11, B1-08).
2. DATO — It is mostly clinically silent and mostly resolves after stopping: 65.8 percent
   asymptomatic in one cohort, 25 of 44 in another, resolution in all 13 children with a
   follow-up examination in a third, and a quantitative white-matter metric that fell during
   exposure and rose after it stopped (B1-06, B1-16, B1-35, B1-18).
3. DATO — "Start age decides" does not survive a full-text human reading. The two human
   cohorts read here at full text found no association with age at first exposure, duration or
   cumulative exposure (B1-02, B1-03, B1-18); age under two years and younger initiation are
   risk factors in one abstract-level cohort and in a pooled review (B1-08). In animals the
   window dependence rests on abstract-level rat and dog studies only.
4. DATO — Reversibility is endpoint-specific, not global: in the immature rat, imaging and
   vacuolation resolved after a drug-free period while myelin content recovered only partially
   and behaviour stayed abnormal; in the adult rat microvacuolation had not fully resolved
   after twelve recovery weeks (B1-23, B1-25).
5. DATO — The one human test of the transfer question is negative: in a cohort of 76, a
   delayed-myelination baseline did not appear to predispose to the abnormality, alongside
   aetiology, microcephaly and oral steroids (B1-09).
6. DATO — The hormonal confounder is not resolved and is not symmetrical: hormonal co-therapy
   travels with the symptomatic form (all 11 symptomatic cases in one cohort; p = 0.039 in
   another) but not with the imaging finding itself (B1-36, B1-12, B1-35, B1-09).
7. DATO — The siblings have no white-matter measurement at all: the CPP-115 package states
   that no histological data existed, its only juvenile study scored cortical and hippocampal
   injury, and neither OV329 full text contains the myelin vocabulary (B1-32, B1-33, B1-34).
8. INFERENZA — The liability is better read as a property of raised GABA in a myelinating
   brain than of the vigabatrin molecule: GABA alone reproduced the mild fibre toxicity in
   culture, cerebrospinal GABA was higher in the children with the abnormality, and two
   genetic conditions that raise GABA without any drug show reduced myelination, one of them
   in the same regions (B1-28, B1-17, B1-30, B1-31).
9. INFERENZA — The WWOX unit therefore needs a harm tag for vigabatrin and a separate
   `undetermined` for the siblings. An unmeasured endpoint is not a clean one, and a
   retina-sparing sibling remains unassessed on brain myelin.
10. IPOTESI — A hypomyelinating baseline could cut either way, and the discriminating
    experiment is a start-window series in a surviving juvenile model with three separate
    readouts (imaging, vacuolation, myelin content), because the human literature shows those
    three do not recover together; the detection-bias arm matters as much, since an abnormal
    baseline can hide the finding (B1-07, B1-10).

## Evidence by theme

**Incidence and who gets it.** Four exposed cohorts and one case series. Rates 22.1 percent
(172 children, one centre), 32.5 percent (77), 28.1 percent (64, two examinations required),
44.7 percent (76 imaged). Aetiology modifies the rate: 10 percent with tuberous sclerosis
against 32.6 percent with other causes in the largest cohort, and genetic aetiology in half
the cases against structural predominance among the non-cases in a 2026 cohort whose authors
call the signal hypothesis-generating. The earliest finding reported is 30 days after start;
the median age at diagnosis in a 44-case series is under seven months, range two weeks to
eleven months. Rows B1-01, B1-04, B1-08, B1-11, B1-13, B1-16.

**Exposure dependence.** Split. Peak exposure, not cumulative, was associated with the
asymptomatic form in one cohort (p = 0.0028) and was the only risk factor in another; peak
exposure was not associated in the largest cohort, nor in the same group's later cohort, nor
between the two exposed arms of the diffusion study, and in the largest cohort 37 of 38 cases
occurred inside the conventional range the authors name. Duration and cumulative exposure were
unassociated wherever they were tested, and in the one cohort where the lower-rate group had
the longer exposure the ordering runs backwards. Rows B1-02, B1-03, B1-12, B1-13, B1-18, B1-36.

**Age and window.** In animals: rat lesion location follows the myelination timetable and the
dog lesion appears when dosing starts earlier and not later (both abstract level, already on
card H02). In the immature rat, exposure during the myelination window reduced myelin staining,
myelin and oligodendrocyte enzyme activity and myelin basic protein, and at low exposure
produced myelin loss, axonal degeneration, glial death and astrogliosis without any
vacuolation — two distinct lesions, not one. In humans: one cohort supports younger
initiation and age under two years; two full-text cohorts do not. Rows B1-02, B1-08, B1-18,
B1-23, B1-24.

**Reversibility.** Imaging resolves — completely in all 13 children with a follow-up
examination, in several regions across 12 post-discontinuation examinations, in a case eight
months after gradual withdrawal, and as a rise in optic-nerve fractional anisotropy within
about three months. Tissue does not resolve as cleanly: microvacuolation persisted after a
twelve-week recovery in the adult rat, and myelin content recovered only partially in the
immature rat while behaviour stayed abnormal. Rows B1-14, B1-18, B1-19, B1-23, B1-25, B1-35.

**Clinical correlates and their absence.** Asymptomatic in the majority (65.8 percent of 38;
25 of 44). Where symptoms occur they are movement disorder, lethargy, encephalopathy and, once,
respiratory distress or arrest. A multicentre trial cohort found the imaging changes in two of
eight infants with a movement disorder and in three of ten comparison infants and concluded the
changes are not specifically related to the movement disorder. Rows B1-06, B1-15, B1-16, B1-20.

**Mechanism, from the adjacent literature.** A myelinating explant culture showed mild
myelinated-fibre toxicity from vigabatrin and from GABA alike, with no intramyelinic oedema
in either. Cerebrospinal free GABA was higher in the two children with the abnormality than
in those without. A genetic block of GABA catabolism gives dysmyelination greater than in
healthy controls, worse with higher plasma GABA and in younger people, in the dentate nucleus,
basal ganglia and periatrial white matter — the regions of the drug finding — with myelin and
oligodendrocyte transcripts reduced in its mouse model; and biallelic loss of the drug's own
target was associated with decreased myelination in one infant. Rows B1-17, B1-28, B1-30,
B1-31.

**The siblings.** The CPP-115 medicinal-chemistry package states in its own words that no
histological data existed for the intramyelinic oedema endpoint. Its only juvenile exposure
study dosed rat pups from postnatal day 4 and perfused them at postnatal day 20, but scored
cortical and hippocampal injury only, and its highest of three levels was uniformly lethal by
postnatal day 8. Neither OV329 full text contains the words myelin, vacuolation, white matter
or intramyelinic, both used adult animals, and the published peer-review record of one carries
reviewer doubt about the claimed safety advantage. Rows B1-32, B1-33, B1-34.

## Counter-evidence (rows with `role = counter`)

* Human neuropathology against the animal lesion: 62 people examined after long-term exposure
  with no microvacuolation or sheath splitting attributable to the drug and no demyelination;
  five earlier human specimens likewise negative, with the animal lesion absent in primates;
  and a negative autopsy in a term infant exposed from the neonatal period with polymicrogyria
  (B1-21, B1-22, B1-27). None of these is an exposed infant in the myelinating window except
  the last, which is a single autopsy.
* The transfer question's one human test is negative (B1-09).
* Exposure intensity and duration are unassociated in several cohorts (B1-02, B1-03).
* The clinical sign and the imaging finding dissociate (B1-15).
* In the one model with pre-existing myelin pathology in which the drug was given — an adult
  autoimmune demyelination model — GABA injections worsened disease while the drug did not
  (B1-29).
* Detection works against every count in both directions: an abnormal baseline may hide the
  finding (the authors' own speculation, B1-07) and 14 of 34 case examinations had been
  reported as normal before review (B1-10).
* Both bounded nulls are counter rows: nothing has been given to a myelin-deficient model
  (B1-37) and nothing to a WWOX system (B1-38).

## Transfer conditions, and what breaks the transfer

Transfers to a WWOX-DEE infant:

1. Age band. Every cohort's exposed children sit inside the first two years, which is the
   WWOX-DEE treatment window; the finding is diagnosed at a median under seven months.
2. The exposure pattern that matters is intensity rather than accumulated amount, and the
   finding can appear within the first month.
3. The harm axis most consistent with the whole record is raised GABA in a brain that is
   still myelinating, which makes it a class question and not a vigabatrin-only one.
4. Reversal must be claimed per endpoint: imaging yes, tissue incompletely, myelin content
   partially in the immature animal.

Breaks the transfer:

1. A baseline abnormality is a detection problem before it is a biology problem. In a brain
   with periventricular white-matter loss and callosal atrophy, a positive finding may be
   misread and a negative may be a miss; no cohort has a reader-blinded re-review in such
   children.
2. The one human test of the hypomyelinating baseline is negative, at abstract level, in 76
   children, with myelination graded from routine reports — too weak to close the question and
   too direct to ignore.
3. Species. The rodent and dog lesions are the sheath-splitting kind; the human infant
   evidence is imaging, with intramyelinic oedema at post-mortem in very few cases; the animal
   lesion was reported absent in primates.
4. Aetiology and co-therapy are entangled in every cohort: the group with the lowest rate also
   had a protective co-medication, and the symptomatic form travels with hormonal therapy.
5. No denominator exists for WWOX-DEE children exposed, imaged and followed, so no WWOX rate
   can be compared with any of the rates above.
6. The siblings are unmeasured on this endpoint, so neither a benefit nor a safety transfer
   can be stated for them in either direction.

## What is not established

* Whether a WWOX-deficient juvenile brain is more, equally or less susceptible. Nothing has
  measured it.
* Whether the liability is a class effect of GABA-aminotransferase inactivation or specific to
  vigabatrin. The GABA-axis evidence is suggestive and indirect; no matched-engagement
  comparison exists.
* Whether hormonal co-therapy causes the imaging finding, modifies its symptoms, or marks
  disease severity. No primary report was found that attributes the deep-grey and brainstem
  pattern to hormonal therapy given alone (query Q11).
* Whether the myelin-content deficit that recovers only partially in the immature rat has any
  human counterpart. No human study measures myelin content under exposure; the nearest is a
  quantitative optic-nerve metric that recovers.
* Whether the aetiology signal in the 2026 cohort reflects genes, referral or imaging practice.
  Its authors say it is hypothesis-generating.
