# Hydroxocobalamin and functional cofactor availability

**Opening decision.** Examine hydroxocobalamin across two intracellular cobalamin-dependent reactions. WWOX white-matter defects provide a phenotype for a competing secondary-cofactor hypothesis. The folinic acid dossier found one qualitative human metabolic work-up. It did not establish a cobalamin-specific lesion. This is a new extension to the roadmap motivated by the ability to distinguish cofactor limitation from a broad change in myelination. The molecule is hydroxocobalamin; sorting or downstream enzyme defects could make precursor delivery ineffective despite cellular entry.

**Prespecified before calculating.** For transport and processing use TCN2, CD320, LMBRD1, ABCD4, MMACHC and MMADHC. For the cytosolic branch use MTR and MTRR, and for the mitochondrial branch use MMAA, MMAB and MMUT (allow historical MUT). Compare untreated human organoids with both mouse brain datasets, with baseline abundance and single-library omission signs. A coherent change within the same branch would motivate a flux comparison. Mixed signs across or within models lower the value of RNA for selecting this molecule. Assess historical symbol mapping before treating an apparent absence as biological.

**Discriminating prediction.** An intracellular cofactor shortage affecting both branches should be accompanied by impaired homocysteine remethylation and methylmalonyl-CoA conversion. At matched substrate availability, restoring both fluxes and improving axonal or myelin function preferentially in WWOX cells would support repurposing. A methylmalonic acid or homocysteine abnormality in isolation can have other explanations, including renal or folate status. A rise in total B12 or nonspecific growth has little discriminatory value. Next check existing WWOX metabolite values for actual analyte, matrix, age, nutritional status and reference range, and separate pediatric exposure precedents from efficacy in WWOX.

**Initial WWOX comparison.** MTR, MTRR, MMAA and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (source symbol MUT, now MMUT) had lower mean expression in knockout mouse cortex, mouse hippocampus and human organoids. Omitting any single library preserved each contrast's sign; organoid MTR was nearly zero in one omission (−0.00047 on the log scale). Mouse tissue uses TPM and human organoids library-normalized CPM. For example, MMAA moved from 11.92 to 10.72, 6.24 to 3.96 and 5.26 to 4.42 in cortex, hippocampus and organoid respectively. TCN2 went the opposite way in human organoids relative to both mouse tissues. Reduced enzyme capacity, neuronal maturation or cell composition could all give the observed transcriptional pattern without a cobalamin shortage. The two mouse tissues are from the same study context. Library omissions are sensitivity checks, not independent replications. [All 33 comparisons with sample counts](bulk.tsv)

The clinical question is whether *both* metabolites and substrate conversion support the same mechanism before testing a developmental phenotype. Parenteral hydroxocobalamin has a pediatric precedent in cblC, a different condition; formulation, route, actual central exposure and clinical benefit require separate assessment. **Direct human WWOX counterexample.** A bounded screen of 579 local JATS XML files identified 94 distinct eligible WWOX-titled articles. Two body texts matched B12 terms. Neither yielded an explicit methylmalonic acid or homocysteine term in the searched body paragraphs. The screen omits some synonyms, tables and supplements and cannot establish absence from all WWOX literature. In Sukkar et al., case presentation and Table 1, vitamin B12 was classified as high. No B12 concentration, unit or reference interval accompanies that entry. Urinary organic acids were described collectively as normal. The report does not quantify methylmalonic acid or plasma total homocysteine; one cannot infer either result from a generic panel. In Feng et al., B12 deficiency appears in the differential diagnosis. It supplies no B12 measurement in that paragraph. A general systemic deficiency is a poor starting assumption for the reported Sukkar child. Functional intracellular availability is still unmeasured. Next establish how strongly dual biochemical correction tracks neurological function in a pediatric comparator with a confirmed cobalamin-processing lesion and check for nutritional or renal explanations in WWOX biomarker records. Exposure–biomarker associations alone cannot answer whether correction predicts development.

**Cell-class cross-check, prespecified.** Examine Mtr, Mtrr, Mmaa and the source symbol Mut in both excitatory and inhibitory hippocampal nuclei from GSE158684. A reduction that also appears within both classes is less readily explained by their changing proportions. Low counts, one pool per genotype and nuclear sampling limit interpretation.

**Outcome of the cell-class check.** Only Mmaa declined in both classes (excitatory 42.5 to 22.9 CPM, inhibitory 41.2 to 22.4). Mtr and Mtrr declined in excitatory nuclei and rose in inhibitory nuclei. Inhibitory Mtrr had just five control and eight knockout counts. Mut rose in excitatory nuclei and fell in inhibitory nuclei. Across all assigned nuclei, the Mmaa difference was −17.31 CPM, with −17.65 within cell classes and +0.34 from changed class weights. This leaves an MMAA-related mitochondrial bottleneck as a narrower hypothesis; enzymatic impairment could itself limit the response to vitamin. The pooled nuclear data do not establish dual-branch cofactor deficiency. Reproduce with `python3 nuclei.py /path/to/files`.
