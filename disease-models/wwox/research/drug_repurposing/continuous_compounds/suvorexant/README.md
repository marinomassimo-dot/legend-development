# Suvorexant in WWOX loss of function

[Printable research brief](../../view/research_reports/suvorexant.pdf) · [Pretest](pretest.md)

**Disposition.** Retain suvorexant as a conditional comparator for the effect of sleep-state redistribution on seizure counting. Defer antiseizure and disease-modifying priority in pediatric WWOX. The decision turns on the same-compound genetic-mouse study, a replay of sleep-time denominators, and the lack of measured neural exposure in that experiment. Context policy was SYNTHESIS.

**State-time replay.** The Rensing natural-history model found more seizures in NREM by count and a higher rate in REM after adjusting for time, age and light cycle. Supplemental Table S1 gives REM versus wake RR **3.708** (1.8878–7.2830), NREM versus wake **1.7769** (0.8414–3.7525), and dark versus light **1.6879** (1.0361–2.7497). The article text prints 7.83 as the upper REM interval limit. The replay uses the supplemental table, where the upper bound also agrees with the reported log-rate SE. Methods describe 10-second manually classified video-EEG/EMG epochs and omit seizures, postictal suppression and unclassifiable background from state time. This is a denominator in the analyzed animals and conditions, not a WWOX sleep-risk coefficient.

We applied those three ratios as fixed, shared hazards to the independently read-back fractions of wake, NREM and REM in Fig. 5A–C, renormalized within each group and phase, then summed two nominal equal 12-hour phases. The composition-only treated-to-vehicle ratio is **1.025**. The separately read 24-hour bars without a light adjustment give **1.034**. Varying each marginal ratio independently to its printed limits and each bar edge by ±2 pixels gives **0.945–1.177** and **0.960–1.175**, respectively. The ranges are scenario envelopes. They have no joint 95% coverage. No state-by-phase interaction, absolute classifiable watch time, individual covariance or treatment-specific state hazard can be recovered from the marginals. In particular, the weekly Fig. 6 count cannot be divided by these 24-hour group mean state fractions to infer a causal direct effect. More time in higher-risk REM could mask within-state benefit, and the observed aggregate comparison leaves that possibility open. The exact registered inputs and calculation are in [the replay](replay.py). Searches of exact DOI-related and author/title metadata on DataCite did not identify a matching deposit. The WashU discovery request returned no usable records in this environment. Public raw joint animal-by-state data were not available for this increment.

## Does exposure support transfer?

**Adult plasma and brain-response calibration.** The FDA composite fasted 20-mg arm (52 unique adults) reported total plasma geometric-mean peak **0.68 µM** (95% interval for the mean 0.62–0.74), with median time to peak **1 hour** (range 0.5–4). The same approval file describes human plasma binding of at least 99.5% in one in vitro assay and approximately 1% free in other spiked-plasma studies, with 0.9–1.5% among healthy matched samples in a secondary comparison. With hypothetical fractions of 0.5%, 1.0% and 1.5%, that Cmax gives **3.4, 6.8 and 10.2 nM** free plasma. These are separate laboratory-supported scenarios and do not bracket every person. [FDA clinical pharmacology, printed pp. 29, 43 and 167–169 in the respective review sections](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2014/204569Orig1s000ClinPharmR.pdf)

Gotter Fig. 7 estimated 65% OX2R occupancy at a free-plasma equivalent of **3.3 nM** by transferring a humanized-rat binding relationship with an assumed human free fraction of 1%. This was a calibration for *sleep promotion*, and human occupancy was not measured. Fig. 4 shows dog sleep and satellite-animal plasma measurements, with dog occupancy calculated from the rat relationship. The central peak scenarios are roughly 1.0–3.1 times the derived threshold at that time. This comparison gives neither occupancy percentage in humans nor engagement at seizure onset. [Gotter, Methods and Figs. 4 and 7](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2202-14-90) · [FDA measured adult plasma](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2014/204569Orig1s000ClinPharmR.pdf)

**Kinetics countercheck.** In the FDA dossier the human recombinant competition Kᵢ values were **0.55 and 0.35 nM** for OX1R and OX2R, while the same summary lists functional Kᵦ **65 and 41 nM**. Mouse values in that functional assay were **53 and 38 nM**. Callander Tables 1–2 use orexin-evoked calcium responses and another protocol. The replay assigns Table 2 to OX2R by its caption and the Results; its internal column label repeats OX1R. Transforming their apparent pKᵢ gives human OX1R **4.07 to 1.86 nM** and OX2R **1.00 to 0.30 nM** for 30-minute versus four-hour preincubation. Mouse OX2R shifts from **10.23 to 4.47 nM**. These values are time-, agonist- and preparation-dependent. Rensing did not measure free local drug, OX1R or OX2R function over the EEG record, so the early REM shift supports a behavioral effect in that window. It leaves regional engagement and the duration of any antiseizure-relevant engagement unknown. [FDA nonclinical review, PDF p. 29](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2014/204569Orig1s000PharmR.pdf) · [Callander, Methods and Tables 1–2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2013.00230)

## Testable WWOX hypothesis

**Observation.** Spontaneous seizures have been observed after postnatal day 12 in Wwox-null mice. The [replayed bulk RNA](native_bulk.tsv) finds HCRTR1 **1.67→1.17 TPM** in mouse cortex (4 controls, 3 mutant libraries) and **3.07→1.84 TPM** in mouse hippocampus (2 and 3). Week-15 human cerebral organoids give **0.59→0.48 CPM** (2 and 4). HCRTR2 stays below 1 TPM or CPM in those three comparisons with differing directions. HCRT is below 0.4 in each bulk condition, and its direction differs across mouse regions. The ligand-producing lateral hypothalamus is unrepresented. Low bulk abundance and library-omission sensitivity leave cell-specific receptor function, orexin tone and WWOX state-dependent seizure rates unverified. [Cheng, seizure phenotype](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40478-020-0883-3) · [Cortex](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE158682) · [Hippocampus](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE158681) · [Human organoid](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE156243)

**Inference and hypothesis.** A circuit source outside the cortex could affect its excitability through orexin-dependent arousal despite low regional RNA. If WWOX recurrence depends on that input, a selective benefit should survive recalculation at common state and circadian weights. The Tsc1 natural-history estimate makes the rival hypothesis concrete. Changing REM duration can shift an aggregate seizure count even if the per-hour hazard in all states stays fixed. WWOX may differ in the direction or size of that effect.

**One discriminating comparison.** Use stage-matched Wwox-deficient and control animals to compare vehicle and suvorexant at a verified local exposure and with an orexin-responsive functional readout. Record continuous EEG/EMG and keep the animal as the unit. Label each pre-onset epoch, separate transitions, and define the seizure, postictal and unclassifiable windows before scoring. Estimate events per at-risk hour within matched state, circadian and exposure bins. Compare observed marginal counts to the result expected from vehicle hazards and treated durations. A benefit confined to redistributed time supports use as a sleep-state comparator. A recurrent EEG benefit at common weights, tied to orexin engagement and preserved initial recruitment, supports testing the functional hypothesis in WWOX. State is itself treatment- and seizure-dependent, so retrospective conditioning alone cannot prove a direct causal effect. If this conditional result emerges, an independent review could change the decision by testing whether carryover, postictal selection, exposure or receptor specificity accounts for it.

## Pediatric feasibility and reproducibility

The U.S. label covers an oral tablet for insomnia and states that pediatric safety and efficacy have not been established. It lists next-day impairment and somnolence, compromised respiratory function, additive CNS depression, and loss of exposure with CYP3A induction such as carbamazepine or phenytoin. These factors would confound alertness and respiratory outcomes in a medically complex epilepsy study. A published uncontrolled insomnia cohort enrolled 30 participants aged 10–20 (abstract only). In the public NCT05546554 snapshot, the randomized crossover study of sleep in autism aged 13–17 planned 26 participants and had no posted results. Its entry criteria restrict seizure disorders, antiseizure medications used for epilepsy and respiratory comorbidity, limiting transfer to WWOX-DEE. Pediatric free brain exposure and a validated within-state seizure endpoint remain the principal feasibility gaps.
