# Sulforaphane and the cost of mounting a glutathione response

**Why this compound now.** The earlier KEAP1–NRF2 package examined a noncovalent probe. A subsequent laboratory audit already identified sulforaphane-specific loss-of-function studies. The compound-specific question here concerns the timing and cellular source of glutathione recovery in WWOX. Earlier cysteine studies considered precursor delivery. This dossier asks whether the electrophile sulforaphane can increase *net neuronal* glutathione reserve after its initial conjugation and export, given the WWOX changes in substrate transport. Treat sulforaphane as the active chemical species; glucoraphanin plus myrosinase is a delivery formulation with variable conversion. [Prior KEAP1 evidence and reading gap](../../keap1_nrf2_sci46_20261111/output/answers.md) · [WWOX precursor comparison](../acetylcysteine/mechanism.md)

**Prediction before tissue extraction.** An adaptive response should allow an early decline or redistribution of GSH followed by higher de novo GSH flux, preserved neuronal redox reserve during a second challenge, and improved evoked recovery beyond any reduction in firing. A transient NRF2 reporter signal alongside ongoing GSH depletion, or improvement confined to glia with unchanged neuronal function, lowers priority. Compare vehicle and sulforaphane across genotype at matched intracellular parent/metabolite exposure, including unstressed cells. Track free GSH, GSSG, drug–thiol conjugates, cell number and de novo synthesis separately over time. Glial conditioning and direct neuronal drug exposure should be distinguishable.

**Initial data test.** Inspect NFE2L2, KEAP1, GCLC, GCLM, GSS, NQO1 and SLC7A11 in the three untreated WWOX bulk preparations. Replicated reductions in the two glutamate–cysteine ligase subunits would support direct testing of a synthesis bottleneck. Opposing directions or strong cell-composition dependence would weaken a claim of a general deficit. Compare neuronal and astrocytic nuclei in the independent hippocampal pools. RNA measures baseline context only. Nuclear NRF2 stabilization, enzyme activity and sulfur flux require separate evidence.

**Untreated WWOX result.** The hippocampal bulk preparation has lower GCLC, GCLM and GSS (−0.29, −0.74, −0.52 in mean log2[1 + normalized RNA]). Mouse cortex has approximately unchanged GCLC and higher GCLM; in the week-15 human organoid GCLC rises and GCLM falls. The predicted common two-subunit deficit fails. The previously noted SLC7A11 decline recurs in all three tissues and survives omission of each library (cortex −0.37, hippocampus −0.73, organoid −0.45). In the independent hippocampal astrocyte-enriched nuclear classes, Gclc and Gclm rise from 60 to 64 and 38 to 49 CPM, respectively. Slc7a11 falls from 352 to 83 CPM; detection falls from 188/417 to 79/479 nuclei, one pool per genotype. This favors a question about substrate availability despite ligase expression. It does not show impaired cystine uptake, establish astrocyte NRF2 activation, or predict the net effect of glutamate exchange. **Source correction at the compound level.** In Kraft et al., mixed cortical cultures exposed to sulforaphane for 48 h activated an antioxidant-response reporter, with predominantly GFAP-associated staining at 2.5 µM (Fig. 1B,E). The total glutathione measurement in Fig. 1D and the neuronal survival test with Nrf2 knockout cultures in Fig. 4A–B used tBHQ. Their caption and results support the astrocyte reporter observation for sulforaphane. They cannot establish that sulforaphane itself restored neuronal glutathione through astrocytic NRF2. The two compounds therefore require separate treatment arms in the evidential comparison. [Kraft et al., Figs. 1 and 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6793572/)

**Direct sulforaphane loss comparison already held by the laboratory.** Mao et al. exposed WT and global Nrf2-knockout cortical cultures to 1.5 µM sulforaphane starting 16 h before the hypoxic challenge and continuing through reoxygenation. The benefit in MTT and LDH endpoints was reported in WT cultures and was lost in KO cultures (Fig. 6C–D, six independent experiments). This supports an NRF2 contribution under sustained exposure. Constitutive loss and different baseline injury susceptibility complicate interpretation. Neither assay measures neuronal GSH flux, and sustained exposure cannot determine whether a brief pulse is sufficient. The new comparison places this held result alongside the WWOX substrate-transport signal and the pediatric chemistry. [Later laboratory audit](../../nrf2_comparator_sci48_20261112/verify/audit.md) · [Mao et al., ischemia methods and Fig. 6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12335009/)

**Pharmacological comparator.** In a separate neuron–glia culture under a low-magnesium seizure-like challenge, the authors reported 20% neuronal death in control medium, 38% with the challenge and 23% with sulforaphane pretreatment (5 µM, 24 h). The observed 15-percentage-point difference concerns death. It cannot say whether the initial seizure burden or network recovery changed, or whether the same drug response requires NRF2 in WWOX-deficient astrocytes. Keep a neuronal activity measure and survival alongside chemistry. [Primary study, Results on Fig. 1C](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8615008/)

**Pediatric exposure and counterevidence.** In the randomized phase of an ASD trial in children 3–12 years old, the administered preparation supplied glucoraphanin with myrosinase. Week-15 plasma cyclocondensation product means were 0.329 nmol DTC/mL (SD 0.350, n=21) versus 0.005 (SD 0.008, n=24). This measures pooled dithiocarbamate equivalents; the concentration of free sulforaphane at a developing brain cell is unresolved. The authors report *lower* free and total GSH/GSSG ratios under exposure. Table 6 displays separate arm statistics; its P values should not be read as a direct estimate of the difference in change. Absolute GSH measures did not show a significant rise in that table. The primary clinical outcome did not significantly differ at weeks 7 or 15. A seizure in the prior year was an exclusion; insomnia, irritability and taste intolerance were reported. Concomitant antiseizure medication interactions and exposure in WWOX infancy remain uncharacterized here. [Pediatric trial, eligibility, Table 5, Table 6 and clinical outcome](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8146218/)

**Decision and next comparison.** Retain as a mechanistic probe. The clearest WWOX-specific liability is that an initial thiol demand could exceed cystine supply even if an astrocyte induces its reporter. Look next for measured parent versus conjugates and the time course of depletion, washout and replenishment in neural preparations, retaining the already documented sulforaphane-specific NRF2-loss comparison as a control for sustained exposure. Prioritize a reduction in oxidant damage paired with recovery of evoked neuronal function at matched activity. An increase in reductive reserve only after abundant cystine supplementation, or failure of neuronal recovery despite glial reporter activation, would lower trial priority.
