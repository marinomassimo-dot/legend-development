# D-pantothenate and residual kinase capacity

**Disposition.** Park D-pantothenate as a candidate for an unselected pediatric WWOX disease-modification trial. Retain the compartment-specific CoA and oxidative-flux hypothesis. In the high-quality transfer test the question is whether adding substrate corrects the limiting step in WWOX. The available data establish a response in selected PANK2-deficient models and provide no exposure-response in WWOX.

**Residual function changes the candidate population.** In the 2022 Alvarez-Cordoba study (Results for Figs. 1–3 and 8), 5 µM added pantothenate for 20 days improved iron staining and protein markers in responsive PKAN fibroblasts. Four patient lines with residual PANK2 protein responded on the protein readouts, whereas the truncated P5 line did not. The authors describe detectable changes from 1–5 µM and maximal changes near 50–100 µM in the selected P1 concentration series. All six compounds selected by the authors improved PANK2 and mitochondrial acyl carrier protein (mtACP) abundance, including agents outside the pantothenate pathway. A change in cellular state or gene expression could therefore mimic a substrate-specific response. This comparison defines neither a minimum percentage of catalytic activity nor a probability of response among unselected genotypes. DMEM and serum were used; the concentration added to the medium does not determine basal-plus-added free intracellular vitamin. [Primary fibroblast study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9364590/)

**Neural endpoint and selection.** The same group's 2021 study selected an already-responsive P1 fibroblast line for direct neuronal conversion. In Figs. 6–7, 500 µM added pantothenate for 20 days reduced iron staining and restored PANK2/mtACP expression. Conversion efficiency was estimated at 50% in controls and 20% in patient cultures; final neuronal purity was approximately 35% and 55%, respectively. Single-cell Tau-associated staining and a bulk protein readout consequently have different denominators. These selected findings do not establish that neurons require 500 µM, that 5 µM would work in neurons, or that evoked neural function and mitochondrial free CoA recover at either concentration. The two papers share investigators and model provenance. [Patient-cell study, Results, Figs. 6–7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8101147/)

**Human exposure changes the comparison.** Rao et al. measured plasma pantothenate following oral calcium pantothenate in healthy adults. In the eight-person repeated-exposure group, the reported peak was 11.05 µM on day 1 and 27.24 µM on day 14. The respective 24-hour time averages derived from AUC were 1.75 and 5.16 µM. Thus the fibroblast addition of 5 µM overlaps the scale of total measured adult plasma exposure. The neuronal addition of 500 µM is about 18 times the reported day-14 peak and 97 times the mean. These comparisons span plasma, cell-culture medium and different exposure durations. The study did not adjust for basal pantothenate; dietary vitamin and small groups limit attribution. Increasing the single fasted oral amount above the 2000 mg group did not increase the observed group peak. This adult formulation study provides no pediatric brain PK or treatment recommendation. [Primary PK report, Tables 3 and 5](https://www.gavinpublishers.com/assets/articles_pdf/1610945487article_pdf1309862097.pdf) · [Unit conversions and denominators](exposure.tsv) · [Calculation](exposure.py)

**Human CSF and a transport control.** Dastur et al., printed p. 148, Table 1, report pantothenic acid at 228 ± 15 ng/mL (mean ± SE, five control CSF determinations), corresponding to 1.040 ± 0.068 µM. Blood used 64 determinations. The investigators used a microbiological assay; these data supply neither paired post-supplement kinetics nor intracellular free pantothenate. In a separate adult rabbit tracer study, excess unlabeled pantothenate reduced tracer penetration into brain and CSF by about 70%. Eighteen hours after ventricular injection, about 40% of the radioactivity *remaining* in forebrain was in CoA. The latter is not 40% of the administered amount, and a fall in tracer penetration under competition does not imply falling total substrate uptake. Another rabbit slice study found that medium-chain fatty acids suppressed uptake while sparing intracellular phosphorylation and conversion in that assay. These identify specific ways in which extracellular vitamin and synthesis can diverge; neither rabbit study predicts concentrations in a child with WWOX. [Original adult CSF table](https://doi.org/10.1079/BJN19760068) · [Rabbit transport and conversion](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3080905/) · [Slice uptake](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3734794/)

**Pediatric feasibility.** Five children or adolescents with a different transporter disease, SLC5A6 deficiency, entered a clinical series at 7 years 10 months to 15 years 1 month. Only one pretreatment serum B5 value was reported in Table 3 (44.79 µg/L, about 0.204 µM). The children received different combinations or sequences including biotin, lipoate and pantothenic acid. Improvement cannot be assigned to B5 alone, and neither the serum measurement nor this combination establishes pediatric neural exposure or safety for WWOX. Background antiseizure medication and nutrition would require matching. The 0.1 mM medium-chain fatty-acid challenge in rabbit slices could motivate an exposure-matched dietary control if such a model is studied; it does not demonstrate a clinical interaction. [Primary clinical series, Table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8747999/)

**Prediction that could reopen the dossier.** At matched neural maturation and workload, WWOX-deficient cells must show an initial deficit in mitochondrial free CoA and oxidative function relative to corrected cells. At a measured intracellular exposure, a pulse of labeled D-pantothenate should then increase *absolute* mitochondrial free CoA, incorporation into CoA and substrate oxidation preferentially in the deficient cells. Label accumulation upstream of CoA would favor restricted synthesis. Recovery of whole-cell CoA with persistent mitochondrial depletion would favor an import or partition problem. Correction of iron staining or mtACP abundance alone leaves these explanations unresolved. The held K562 analysis provides a measured example of this distinction. Neither evidence of a primary WWOX vitamin deficit nor a basis for a pediatric trial emerged. A further interpretation-only review is unlikely to replace the missing genotype-by-exposure measurement.
