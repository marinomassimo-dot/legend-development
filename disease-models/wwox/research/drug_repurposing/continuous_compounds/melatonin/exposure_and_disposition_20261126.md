# Melatonin in WWOX loss of function: pediatric outcomes, exposure and mitochondrial response

**Data scope.** Sleep minutes, sleep-stage shares and overnight scalp spikes per hour in a pediatric epilepsy crossover; response counts, post-treatment EEG and serum concentration in a pediatric infantile-spasm trial; saliva and plasma exposure for an EU prolonged-release formulation; vehicle-normalized oxygen consumption in HEK293 and mouse cerebellar mitochondrial preparations and LDH outcome in stressed mouse primary cortical neurons.

**Countercheck on EEG composition.** With a common nonnegative spike rate for each sleep stage and the published mean stage shares, the overall melatonin/placebo spike rate ratio must lie between the smallest and largest ratios of the stage shares. The observed mean ratio is 31.7/71.9 = 0.441, below the composition-only interval 0.770–1.190. Even allocating all placebo spikes to the stage with the largest relative decrease gives an expected 55.4 per hour, above 31.7. Combining N1 and N2 via the complement of N3 and REM gives the same bounds under the additional assumption of a common rate in this combined group. The first-period-only analysis footnote for N1 and N2 and possible participant-level covariance of stage share with spike rate prevent interpreting these group bounds as a subject-level adjustment. The bound exposes the assumption behind attributing the entire mean shift to stage shares. It does not turn the reported p=0.13 into an antiseizure effect. ## Mitochondrial mechanism and exposure

- In Somalo-Barranco et al., nominal 1 µM parent reduced ADP-stimulated oxygen consumption by about 20% in mitochondrial fractions from MT1-expressing HEK293 relative to vehicle. The comparison to GFP is marked significant, and GFP versus MT2 is marked ns. In the archived manuscript's mouse cerebellar panel, WT decreases and MT1 knockout is near zero on the graph. The direct WT–MT1-KO bracket is **ns**. The starred bracket compares MT1-KO with MT2-KO. The two patterns support an MT1-dependent hypothesis but the direct mouse genotype contrast is less conclusive than the narrative. The caption describes three independent experiments, and the relation of plotted points to those experiments is unverified. The preparation measures substrate-supported oxygen flux, with no combined ATP or neuronal functional endpoint. [Somalo-Barranco et al., archived manuscript Fig. 8A/B on printed p. 19, methods pp. 35–36](https://hal.science/hal-04273174/document) · [Article DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chembiol.2023.07.009)
- Suofu et al. provide a distinct endpoint under cell injury. At 10 µM in primary mouse cortical neurons, added melatonin lowered the oxygen-glucose-deprivation death readout in the WT/MT1-overexpression comparison. In a separate preparation, 10 µM melatonin reduced calcium-triggered cytochrome-c release in isolated mitochondria, with antagonism and compartment controls. In a separate N2a assay, the cell-impermeant MT1 agonist did not reproduce the intact-cell survival effect when compared at nominal 40 µM. The systems partly share investigators and differ in injury, preparation and endpoints. Cytoprotection can coexist with reduced acute respiration. Their results motivate simultaneous function and injury readouts instead of assuming that either is a proxy for ATP preservation. [Suofu et al., Figs. 2F–H and 5A](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1705768114)

**Exact formulation and exposure.** The EMA product information gives 410 pg/mL (~1.77 nM) as the pediatric saliva peak after the prolonged-release mini-tablet in a PK study described as 16 children with ASD aged 7–15. The assessment distinguishes 14 children included in its evaluable discussion. The adult fed prolonged-release **plasma** peak, 3.57 ng/mL (~15.4 nM), lies about 65-fold below the nominal 1 µM mitochondrial bath and 650-fold below the 10 µM primary-neuron assay. Sun's on-treatment **06:00 serum** medians were 84.8 versus 17.5 pg/mL (~0.365 versus ~0.075 nM), excluding the four withdrawals. Times, formulations, compartments and free fractions differ. These scale comparisons provide no central exposure margin and cannot rule out lower-concentration receptor signaling. [EMA product information §5.2](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/product-information/slenyto-epar-product-information_en.pdf) · [EMA assessment, pp. 28–29 and clinical pharmacology discussion](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/assessment-report/slenyto-epar-public-assessment-report_en.pdf) · [Sun et al., Table 3](https://doi.org/10.1111/jpi.12922)

**Pediatric feasibility.** This specific European prolonged-release drug has pediatric insomnia precedent under defined conditions. The label distinguishes qualifying age and diagnoses, recommends preservation of the mini-tablet release form and identifies somnolence and fatigue. This matters in a WWOX study with a daytime function endpoint, swallowing limitations or concurrent sedating treatments. Its labeling identifies reduced melatonin exposure with CYP1A2 inducers such as carbamazepine and important potentiation with hypnotic co-exposure. Infantile ACTH co-treatment and short follow-up limit safety transfer to chronic evaluation in severe WWOX. Choose a defined formulation and sample schedule if there is a mechanistically informative reason to proceed. Use timed sleep, stage-specific EEG, daytime alertness and development as distinct endpoints. [Current EMA product information §§4.1–4.5, 4.8 and 5.1](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/product-information/slenyto-epar-product-information_en.pdf)

## Inference, hypothesis and decision

**Inference.** Lower sleep latency is plausible in a symptomatic subgroup despite sparse local receptor transcripts in the held WWOX material. The strongest controlled infantile evidence separates a sleep signal from uncertain spasm and EEG effects. Changing clock transcripts across samples can reflect stage, circadian timing or cell composition. The combination of higher and lower transcript values across models leaves direction of a shared clock defect unresolved. In developmental neurons with limited reserve, a drop in peroxide or injury markers could accompany lower ATP and evoked function during exposure.

**Discriminating observation.** In matched-stage WWOX and isogenic neurons, use verified low and higher relevant exposure, phase-matched treatment and initial evoked workload. Record extracellular concentration and active species where possible and compare an MT1 perturbation with a matched control and a timed vehicle. Pair acute OCR and absolute ATP recovery with matched evoked inhibitory and excitatory output after washout. In a separate timed EEG comparison, calculate epileptiform events within each vigilance state and clock window, with hours at risk and alertness recorded. Improvement in sleep with a state-neutral spike rate supports the sleep route. A same-phase and same-workload benefit in ATP and evoked response that diminishes with confirmed MT1 perturbation supports the local route. Lower oxygen consumption or lower oxidative injury without net ATP recovery would lower the metabolic-rescue hypothesis. A controlled phenotypic benefit surviving verified receptor perturbation would motivate a redox or metabolite mechanism.

**Disposition.** Retain a testable symptom-specific sleep hypothesis for children with documented sleep disturbance. Park for unselected pediatric WWOX antiseizure or metabolic-rescue priority. Reopen when measured exposure and genotype-selective neural recovery persist at matched phase/arousal, or for a sleep-specific question with a documented baseline and separate EEG/alertness outcomes. The ambiguous direct genetic contrast leaves the mitochondrial hypothesis open. Reviewing it alone would not change the disposition in the absence of relevant WWOX exposure and function data.
