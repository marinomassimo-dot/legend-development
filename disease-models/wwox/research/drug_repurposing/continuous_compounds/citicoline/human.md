# What oral and intravenous human evidence can establish

**Circulating species.** In 12 fasting, mildly hypertensive adults studied in a randomized crossover, oral citicoline increased plasma choline and uridine. At the two highest exposures, doubling the oral amount produced no further increment in uridine. Cytidine was below the authors' reliable detection threshold (100 nM). The human blood assay measured choline, cytidine and uridine; the accompanying evidence for conversion of uridine to CTP came from PC-12 cells. The study cannot give a human brain concentration of intact citicoline. In WWOX neural systems, compare intact substrate with choline alone, uridine alone and the two together at measured exposures. Retain the intracellular precursor pools and independent uridine effects in interpretation. [Wurtman et al., primary study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10974208/)

**Central measurement.** After six weeks of oral citicoline in healthy older adults, Babb et al. reported a 7.3% increase in the brain phosphodiester resonance from baseline. In the first six weeks all participants received citicoline. Glycerophosphoethanolamine increased 11.6% (p=.002); glycerophosphocholine increased 5.1% (p=.137). These metabolite pools can change with membrane turnover. The observation does not measure new phosphatidylcholine formation, a myelin-specific pool, or conduction. The larger component change was on the ethanolamine side of the spectrum. [Babb et al., primary study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12021827/)

**Pediatric route.** McGlade et al. randomized 75 healthy adolescent males in a 28-day supplementation study with attention and motor tasks. The HIE trial enrolled 80 neonates after acute hypoxic-ischemic injury who were ineligible for cooling. They received placebo or intravenous citicoline in addition to supportive care. Four treated infants had mild diarrhea. The two populations and routes leave a gap for chronic enteral exposure in young children with a developmental encephalopathy. Neither study reports oligodendroglial exposure or structural myelin rescue in WWOX. [Adolescent trial abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26179181/) · [Neonatal trial, clinical results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10182621/)

**Registry and arithmetic audit.** The cited trial record, last updated May 2019, estimated 40 participants and listed the number of seizures at six months as its primary outcome. The paper enrolled 80, calls Griffiths developmental assessment its primary outcome and reports several follow-up times. The published post-discharge seizure measure counts patients with an event; it does not report seizure counts per infant at six months. The report gives 12-month developmental quotients of 98 vs 83, but records two losses in the treatment arm (including one death) and three deaths in controls. Table 3 gives no time-specific denominators. A change to the protocol could be legitimate; the public registration and publication do not establish when the primary outcome changed. [Registry and update history](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03949049) · [Publication, Methods and Tables 3–4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10182621/)

During treatment, Table 4 reports seizures in 8/40 versus 15/40, a difference of −17.5 percentage points. The stated uncorrected Pearson chi-square procedure gives two-sided p=.084 (two-sided Fisher p=.137); the article prints .04. After discharge the corresponding values are 2/40 versus 11/40, −22.5 points, Pearson p=.0064 (printed .006), Fisher p=.0129. The calculation uses the printed denominators; it does not impute seizure exposure time after deaths or loss to follow-up.
