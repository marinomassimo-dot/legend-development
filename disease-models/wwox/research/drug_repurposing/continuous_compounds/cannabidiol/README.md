# Purified cannabidiol and activity-dependent adenosine in WWOX loss

context_policy: QUESTION_DRIVEN

**Selection.** The clobazam dossier identifies cannabidiol as a co-exposure in WWOX and a major determinant of active clobazam metabolite exposure. A separate hypothesis asks whether purified cannabidiol modifies the adenosine response to energy demand in a WWOX network even when clobazam and its metabolite are absent. The laboratory's prior adenosine audit found lower neuronal Adora1 and Adk RNA together in one pooled hippocampal comparison. The directions are heterogeneous across models. A fall in A1 receptor availability could counteract increased extracellular adenosine. Use that audit as a prior and measure receptor function and substrate handling separately. [WWOX adenosine audit](../../advisor_pilot_20261113/brainstorm_20261116/deepening_he_20261116/I4_METABOLIC_ADENOSINE_DOSSIER.md) · [Clobazam exposure and clinical comparison](../clobazam/exposure_and_subtypes.md)

**Primary comparison.** Carrier et al. reported reduced radiolabelled adenosine uptake in murine microglial and macrophage preparations and a CBD binding Ki below 250 nM at ENT1. Their animal experiment measured an A2A-dependent inflammatory endpoint. Binding affinity, nucleoside influx and an A1-dependent neuronal firing effect each require separate evidence. None of these experiments measured net transport during a WWOX network burst. [Carrier et al., primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16672367/)

**Prediction and opposing outcome.** ENT1 transports down a concentration gradient. If uptake normally terminates extracellular adenosine after a matched burst, reducing uptake should prolong the adenosine transient and delay evoked firing recovery in an A1-dependent manner. If cells instead supply extracellular adenosine by ENT-mediated efflux under energetic stress, blocking transport could diminish the transient and shorten the A1-dependent suppression. A third outcome is an altered transient with little effect on firing if functional A1 receptor reserve is low. These signs are conditional on flux direction, stage and intact receptor function.

**First test.** Compare WWOX-deficient and corrected neural cultures with the same evoked activity, oxygen, glucose, cell composition and measured free cannabidiol exposure, initially without clobazam. Pair time-resolved extracellular adenosine with evoked spike recovery. Compare uptake and release phases, manipulate ENT1 and A1 independently, and account for adenosine generated outside cells by ATP breakdown. Test whether the genotype-by-cannabidiol effect is lost after ENT1 or A1 disruption while normal evoked transmission is preserved. Next retrieve primary neural evidence on the source of the burst-evoked adenosine transient and quantify the gap, if any, between unbound CBD exposure and ENT1 transport inhibition. Keep mixed-therapy seizure outcomes separate.

**First neural comparison.** Wall and Dale's stimulated wild-type mouse hippocampal slices show why both amplitude and timing matter. Blocking ENT1 and ENT2 together decreased the peak adenosine signal by 39.5%, while the latency rose from 12.6 to 26.3 seconds and the decay time constant rose from 171 to 241 seconds. The latter is a 41% increase, calculated as 100 × (241/171 − 1). The Results count eight slices for the amplitude change and the Fig. 7A caption counts seven. The experiments used an A1 antagonist to suppress feedback, and used two transport blockers. They establish that a smaller peak can coexist with a longer decay; the effects of cannabidiol alone, the contribution specific to ENT1 and the effect on A1-dependent firing remain open. Next compare transport inhibition by free cannabidiol at the relevant exposure with a post-burst exposure measurement and a functional A1 readout. [Wall & Dale, Fig. 7A–E and adjoining Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3764633/)
