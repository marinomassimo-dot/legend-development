# Cannabidiol, extracellular purines and evoked inhibition in WWOX loss

context_policy: QUESTION_DRIVEN

**Decision.** Defer WWOX pediatric trial prioritization for the proposed adenosine mechanism. Retain one testable preclinical question. Does cannabidiol alter the early purine transient and its A1-dependent effect on transmission at measured free exposure? Direct CBD experiments weaken the hypothesis that it simply prolongs the extracellular adenosine signal. In untreated WWOX data, the most consistent transporter RNA change concerns ENT2. The best resolved submicromolar CBD influx assay concerns ENT1 in a murine microglial line. These observations lead to different experimental controls.

## Plasma exposure versus two experimental concentrations

Carrier et al. measured inhibition of one-minute radiolabelled adenosine influx after CBD preincubation. The nominal IC50 was 0.124 µM in EOC-20 microglia and 0.190 µM in RAW264.7 macrophages. The microglial competition binding Ki at 37 °C was 0.237 µM. Binding and transport used different endpoints. The transport medium was balanced salt solution. Free concentration at the membrane was not measured. [Carrier et al., Results, Figs. 3–4, 6 and uptake methods](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16672367/)

The estimates below use molecular weight 314.46 and assume that 5% of total parent CBD in plasma was free. A human plasma-spiking study found 5% under its interaction assay conditions, higher free fractions at high concentrations, and loss of CBD to some container materials. The FDA label reports greater than 94% in vitro protein binding. Neither source measures the unbound fraction in the children below. The [full sensitivity table](free_exposure.tsv) includes assumed fractions of 1%, 5% and 6%; 6% is a bounding scenario for the label's conditions. Peak and trough observations come from distinct sampling designs. [Human binding experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38902820/) · [FDA label, §12.3](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/210365s024s029s030lbl.pdf)

Brstilo et al. obtained 25 trough samples in 22 pediatric responders; selection on response precludes estimating a response threshold. Cáceres Guido et al. obtained six serial samples per participant in 12 children. The relationship between individual participants across these reports is unknown. Wheless et al., Table 3, distinguishes geometric means from arithmetic means in a different synthetic formulation. Dose cohorts and their means do not describe an individual concentration ceiling. None of the three PK summaries measures extracellular brain parent or includes its active metabolite in the numbers above. [Brstilo, Table 1 and population](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10459683/) · [Cáceres Guido, primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33280111/) · [Wheless, Table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6534520/)

These ratios express the size of the exposure bridge. They cannot establish brain target occupancy or rule out effects below an IC50. For example, at the serial median peak the assumptions would need a free-brain/free-plasma ratio of 15.7 to equal the **nominal** microglial IC50 if all assay CBD were freely available. Unknown assay loss, tissue partition, exposure time and local cellular sensitivity change that calculation. Developing-rat measurements do not supply this missing ratio. At P12 after intraperitoneal administration, cortex/plasma radioactivity was 44 ± 4% and CSF/plasma 15 ± 2% at 30 minutes (5 pups, 2 litters). Parallel in vitro P12 plasma free fraction was 25.8 ± 7.6%. Radioactivity includes potential metabolites, tissue contains bound material, and CSF is a different compartment from synaptic interstitial fluid. [Rat distribution study, Tables 8–9 and limitations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11055756/)

## A smaller purine peak can accompany a steeper electrophysiological response

Hu's university-deposited thesis reports direct measurements during CA1 stimulation in a rat model of chronic epilepsy. In epileptic slices, nominal 10 µM CBD changed the mean peak purine signal from 0.37 to 0.20 adenosine-equivalent µM (−46% from rounded means). Eight slices came from five animals. The within-group comparison had adjusted P=0.0047; disease-by-CBD interaction P=0.072. The enzymatic sensor also responds to downstream purines. The mean decay constant changed from 11.8 to 10.2 min, with three of eight epileptic slices excluded because a decay could not be fitted; the overall CBD effect on decay had P=0.797. Thus the longer decay produced by dual ENT blockade in the earlier mouse experiment was not established for CBD. [Hu, thesis §§3.5–3.6, Figs. 3-8 and 3-9](https://doi.org/10.48683/1926.00101315) · [Mouse dual-blockade comparator](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3764633/)

In the same thesis, the magnitude of the fitted slope relating normalized field responses to measured purines in epileptic tissue changed from 1.8 to 4.5 (2.5-fold). The disease-by-CBD interaction for slopes had P=0.391. Maximum evoked field inhibition showed no significant overall CBD effect. Time points within a transient are correlated. The regression does not measure A1 receptor affinity, and neither a CBD-specific transporter mechanism nor a WWOX-selective electrical benefit follows from it. Rat striatal terminal experiments also implicate glutamate uptake, and rat hippocampal cultures/slices show other receptor-dependent CBD effects on basal transmission. Both provide alternatives for an electrical change with a different purine trajectory. [Hu, Figs. 3-10 and 3-11](https://doi.org/10.48683/1926.00101315) · [Pandolfo et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21266173/) · [Ledgerwood et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20825410/)

**Discriminating prediction and reopening criterion.** At matched stimulus burden and measured free CBD, a reduction in early purine peak with faster recovery of transmission would support impaired outward transport. A longer decay with delayed recovery would support impaired clearance. A change in the field response at fixed extracellular adenosine would instead prioritize receptor-side sensitivity or another action on firing. Compare isolated ENT1 and ENT2 effects and the response to an A1-selective agonist in corrected and WWOX-deficient neural systems. Control for extracellular ATP breakdown, glutamate clearance, general synaptic suppression and exposure to clobazam. If a genotype difference persists at clinically credible free exposure and follows ENT1 and A1 dependence with preserved evoked transmission, reconsider priority. Until then, a pediatric efficacy study directed specifically at this mechanism has no validated exposure or response endpoint.

## Isoform-specific countercheck and clinical attribution

Wu et al. compared activity-evoked, extracellular adenosine signals in hippocampal neuronal cultures. Each single transporter knockout retained a response; loss of both nearly eliminated it. Fig. 4N–O shows 4–6 coverslips per group and marks a wild-type versus double-loss comparison. It does not establish a significant difference between the two single-loss responses. In this preparation either remaining isoform could support part of outward adenosine transport. The result strengthens the early-efflux alternative and prevents assigning the dual-blocker phenotype specifically to ENT1. A conditional **new WWOX prediction** is that reduced functional ENT2 could increase dependence on residual ENT1 for activity-evoked release. If so, exposure-matched CBD could further attenuate the early A1 brake. The prediction fails if residual efflux is preserved after ENT1 loss, if direct A1 sensitivity accounts for the electrical change, or if attainable CBD levels leave the transient unchanged. RNA abundance, single-knockout compensation, coverslip replication and species differences limit transfer. [Wu et al., Fig. 4N–O and adjoining Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10083574/)

Individual-trial investigators pooled 714 participants in four randomized trials in Lennox–Gastaut and Dravet syndromes. Relative seizure-frequency estimates for add-on CBD versus placebo were 0.85 (95% CI 0.73–0.98) without background clobazam and 0.59 (0.52–0.68) with it. CBD was randomized within trials; pre-existing clobazam treatment was not. This supports a clinical effect outside clobazam co-therapy in these populations, with no CNS target attribution. In the held WWOX series, four of 13 medication histories list CBD, and two list both drugs as current. It does not provide free-exposure-linked seizure responses for the proposed ENT mechanism. [Pooled trial Results, Fig. 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7689899/) · [WWOX table recount](../clobazam/clinical_counts.tsv)

**Final disposition.** Park the CBD–ENT1 hypothesis for WWOX pediatric trial prioritization. The genetic comparison makes outward transport biologically credible; the disease-specific direction and clinical exposure bridge remain open. Reopen for chemically specific extracellular adenosine and evoked recovery in WWOX versus corrected neural preparations, with separated ENT1, ENT2 and A1 controls at measured free parent CBD. Existing antiseizure efficacy in other syndromes has a different endpoint.
