# A measured central magnesium increment and the expected change in NMDA current

**Prediction before calculating the curves.** Compare short and sustained intravenous exposure in adults by chemical species and sampling time. Use the measured change in mean ventricular ionized magnesium after sustained exposure as a numerical scenario for recombinant receptor fits. Test whether any of the magnesium-only subtype/voltage fits (the original table also contained a −100 mV row, so all twelve were retained) predicts an additional loss of at least five percentage points of magnesium-free current. This exploratory threshold helps distinguish a large direct channel effect from one requiring circuit amplification. Report the percentage of residual current separately and check the artificial magnesium-removal comparator. A smaller result cannot exclude seizure effects or establish oral chloride exposure. No WWOX-specific selectivity is assumed.

## Human exposure and formulation

**Oral chloride has a direct comparator.** Slutsky et al., Fig. 1A, included magnesium chloride, magnesium L-threonate, gluconate plus milk, and unsupplemented rats. They sampled cisternal CSF at baseline, day 12 and day 24 and measured total Mg by calmagite. The chloride arm fell at day 12 and was near its own baseline at day 24. The authors report no significant elevation versus control for the non-threonate salts. At day 24 the threonate arm was approximately 7% above its own baseline while repeated sampling in controls was associated with an approximately 9% fall. The often-cited approximately 15% estimate used the controls. Applying it to oral chloride or ionized Mg in a child would change both formulation and analyte. The small groups (6–8) leave room for a smaller effect. [Slutsky et al., Fig. 1A and CSF methods](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20152124/)

## Channel and circuit comparison

Using Yi et al. Table 3, compute current remaining as 100/[1 + (Mg/IC50)^Hill] with both concentrations in µM. The source fits used fixed maximal inhibition, four to eight oocytes per subtype at −100, −80, −60 and −40 mV. The numerical 0.80 → 0.89 scenario treats ventricular ionized values as bath concentrations solely to test scale. Receptor composition, kinetics, agonist conditions, calcium, membrane voltage and synaptic Mg in WWOX may differ. The earlier WWOX analysis found no replicated increase in the relative D/B RNA supply across juvenile cortex and human organoids. Endogenous receptor assembly and cell-specific current remain open. Values are additional percentage points of magnesium-free current lost. At −60 mV, B/B goes from 3.09% to 2.79% current remaining. That is a 9.83% drop relative to its already small residual current. The largest absolute loss across twelve fits is 2.66 points; changing IC50 by one reported SEM still gives less than 2.67 points in all cases. These sensitivities are not confidence limits. With slopes ≤1, the largest possible equilibrium loss at this concentration ratio for any positive IC50 is 100 × (√r − 1)/(√r + 1) = 2.665 points, r = 0.89/0.80. Removing then restoring magnesium from 0 to 0.80 yields 46.06–99.63 points across these same fits. The separate 1.50 → 1.59 nominal scenario gives at most 1.37 points and does not assume the WWOX bath had 1.50 mM ionized Mg. Substantial seizure effects near a circuit threshold remain possible. **Competing functional prediction.** In a separate experiment in the same Slutsky paper, slices from two-month-old rats spent five hours in 0.8 or 1.2 mM Mg. The baseline recording bath then contained 1.2 mM Mg for both. At 1.2 mM calcium, single-pulse AMPA responses were similar, while burst facilitation was greater after the higher-Mg incubation (Fig. 7B,E). The 0.40 mM incubation difference is more than four times the adult ventricular change above. A time-dependent increase in excitatory burst transfer could oppose an immediate reduction in NMDA conductance. This is a discriminant for WWOX rather than evidence for the sign of a seizure effect. Measure single-pulse and burst transmission early and after a matched duration in a common recording bath, at fixed extracellular calcium, alongside evoked inhibition and recurrent discharge. Preservation of the single-pulse response alone would miss this adaptation. [Rat hippocampal comparison](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20152124/)

## WWOX and pediatric controls

**Blood calcium cannot establish magnesium deficiency.** In Aqeilan et al., supplemental Table 3 reports calcium 1.19 mmol/L KO versus 2.02 WT in a representative pooled serum analysis at three weeks. There are no independent animal values or magnesium row. In a separate whole-body Wwox KO, Ludes-Meyers et al. Table 3 shows calcium 10.18 versus 11.13 mg/dL (KO n=4, WT n=3), BUN 37.25 versus 17.67 mg/dL, and total CO₂ 14.50 versus 21.67 mEq/L. The table’s tests combine WT and heterozygotes versus KO; it does not assay Mg. These assays motivate measuring ionized calcium, magnesium, acid–base status and renal handling in the same preparation. A correction of hypocalcemia or a systemic metabolic difference can change excitability independently of a direct neuronal WWOX response. [Aqeilan et al., supplement p. 2](https://ars.els-cdn.com/content/image/1-s2.0-S0021925819546579-mmc1.pdf) · [Ludes-Meyers et al., Table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2777388/)

**Pediatric feasibility.** Five patients with TRPM6-associated magnesium wasting received oral magnesium hydroxide for maintenance. Diarrhoea limited dose increases. In one infant, tetany resolved with parenteral Mg alongside recovery of serum calcium; seizures subsequently recurred on two occasions. In the separate CNNM2 study, two siblings continued to have seizures even when intravenous treatment restored circulating magnesium to normal. One historic within-child comparison used an oral solution containing both chloride and citrate in an eight-year-old with primary hypomagnesemia. It measured systemic magnesium/calcium and endocrine responses. These observations establish a formulation precedent and a compelling deficiency comparator, with no isolated evidence of oral chloride changing ionized CSF or seizures in magnesium-replete WWOX. [TRPM6 cohort](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4566842/) · [CNNM2 families](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3974678/) · [Mixed-salt study, patient and methods](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/107159/files/pdf)

**Disposition and next comparison.** Low priority for an unselected pediatric trial. Retain the mechanistic comparison while testing whether the balance of recurrent excitation and recruited inhibition could magnify a small change or reverse it after adaptation. A selective reduction of recurrent WWOX bursts at a measured attainable Mg increment, with preserved evoked inhibition and no increased delayed burst facilitation, would raise priority. An effect confined to Mg withdrawal or traceable to simultaneous calcium correction or reduced transmission in both genotypes would lower it. The final check below tests whether the published WWOX evoked peaks estimate this amplification. Keep magnesium deficiency as a distinct subgroup hypothesis until a disease-specific assay documents it.

**WWOX endpoint audit and closure.** Repudi et al., mouse neocortical Fig. 2E–F, found larger evoked field responses in neuronal knockouts. Layer V stimulation was a single 0.1-ms pulse every 30 s. The two peaks measured in superficial cortex both followed the *same* 100-µA stimulus. The knockout data comprised eight slices from six animals and controls six slices from three. The second peak divided by the first cannot identify paired-pulse facilitation, burst transfer, inhibitory recruitment or the slope near firing threshold. Their spontaneous bursting is a separate endpoint. A net amplification remains possible, but using either peak ratio as a gain factor for the small modeled Mg shift would generate spurious precision. Park oral magnesium chloride for unselected pediatric WWOX trial priority. Reopen for WWOX-specific ionized deficiency linked to functional correction, or an intact-network genotype-by-exposure response that preserves inhibition at verified oral central exposure. A further literature review would change this decision only if it produced one of these missing measurements. [Repudi et al., electrical stimulation and Fig. 2E–F](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33914858/)
