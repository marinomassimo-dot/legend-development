# Thiamine and pyruvate oxidation in WWOX loss

**Question before analysis.** In WWOX-deficient neural systems, does extra thiamine improve mitochondrial pyruvate oxidation because the active cofactor thiamine diphosphate (ThDP) is limiting, or does phosphorylation of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex or a downstream respiratory constraint leave flux unchanged? Prior laboratory work mapped kinase-directed approaches and listed thiamine without a neural cofactor-response comparison. Consider conventional thiamine as one molecule. Its analogues have different delivery and target profiles. [Earlier cofactor coverage](../../pdh_pdk_sci44_20261110/output/a4/levers.tsv)

**Discriminating prediction.** At matched substrate and neural activity, a larger baseline difference between native pyruvate dehydrogenase activity and activity after adding saturating ThDP favors a cofactor limitation in WWOX cells. Supplementation would need to raise mitochondrial ThDP and glucose-derived acetyl-CoA/citrate labeling with a functional improvement. A rescue confined to a deprived medium or to blood vitamin concentration would downgrade it. An increase in cytosolic transketolase without recovery of mitochondrial substrate flux is a plausible competing outcome. A patient with a PDHA1 variant affecting cofactor affinity or a primary thiamine transporter deficiency is a positive control for a different lesion. Sensitivity to thiamine in that context cannot establish an effect in WWOX.

**First analysis.** Compare thiamine uptake (SLC19A2/3), conversion (TPK1), mitochondrial import (SLC25A19), PDH subunits, kinases and the independent ThDP-dependent enzyme OGDH across WWOX models with signed contrasts, concentration provenance and donor-level limitations. These transcripts can prioritize limiting steps but do not measure intracellular vitamin availability or cofactor occupancy. Look for an existing WWOX functional or metabolite measurement, relevant medium composition, dose-dependent enzymatic results in a neural comparator and attainable pediatric central exposure before disposition.

**Initial RNA screen.** All fourteen targeted genes were mapped in three datasets (42 contrasts). The main neural thiamine transporter SLC19A3 had lower expression in mouse cortex and hippocampus but higher expression in human organoids. Omitting any single library retained each direction. Mitochondrial ThDP carrier SLC25A19 was lower in all three, robust to single-library omission in human organoids and mouse hippocampus only. TPK1 was clearly lower in hippocampus, whereas its small differences in cortex and organoids crossed zero on omission. Independent biological replication is limited, especially two human control libraries. Differences in tissue composition and developmental stage remain. This favors examining mitochondrial cofactor delivery and measuring native versus ThDP-supplemented activity before assuming deficiency from reduced cellular uptake. A transporter limitation could itself constrain the response to added thiamine. **Disposition.** Park ordinary thiamine for a pediatric WWOX trial and retain it as a cofactor-availability probe. A transporter signal across bulk and neuronal nuclei supplies a testable localization, but neither a deficiency nor a selective intact-neural rescue has been measured. Reopen for matrix-specific cofactor depletion with recovery of functional oxidation and a neural endpoint at an exposure demonstrated to reach the compartment. Replicate units must be independent preparations. A separate indication to treat a diagnosed vitamin deficiency has a different evidentiary basis. Pediatric pharmacologic exposure is documented in a transporter disorder, with no formulation-specific chronic safety or antiseizure-combination assessment in WWOX identified here.
