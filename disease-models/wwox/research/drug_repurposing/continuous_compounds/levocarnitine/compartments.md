**Direct human WWOX check.** Two children in Shaukat et al. had acylcarnitine among the investigations reported as normal. One had a deletion affecting exons 3 and 4 and the other a homozygous splice-site variant. The second child received valproate, but the timing of the metabolic sample relative to exposure was unspecified. A third child, with homozygous p.Leu239Arg, had an unremarkable acylcarnitine profile during neonatal assessment, before later valproate for spasms. These three case narratives give neither absolute free carnitine nor individual C2/long-chain values or paired measurements after treatment. They weigh against assuming a conspicuously abnormal routine profile in every child. They neither estimate prevalence nor show whether brain substrate use recovers with levocarnitine. [Shaukat et al., two cases](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30361190/) · [Serce Pehlevan et al., case presentation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13378201/)

**Physiological concentration anchor.** In 15 children aged at most one year, median CSF free carnitine was 4.11 µM (interquartile range 4.05–4.70) and acetylcarnitine 0.19 µM (0.11–0.23). The 57-child parent study used residual diagnostic samples from symptomatic children in whom meningitis was excluded. These quartiles describe this group and cannot diagnose WWOX deficiency, determine a post-supplement concentration, or serve as normative neonatal cutoffs. The study compared no medicine, administered no challenge and does not give a matched CSF/plasma partition estimate. A reported cellular rescue far above this range would require separate evidence of attainable exposure and target compartment.
