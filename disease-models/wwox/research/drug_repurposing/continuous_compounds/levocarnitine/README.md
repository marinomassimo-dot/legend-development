# Levocarnitine and substrate use in WWOX deficiency

**Prediction recorded before measuring the WWOX contrasts.** If the limiting defect is insufficient cellular free L-carnitine, restoration of that pool should preferentially improve long-chain fatty acid oxidation compared with a carnitine-shuttle-independent substrate in the affected cell type. If the limiting defect lies at mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation downstream of the shuttle, or in shared respiration, increasing the free pool may leave oxidation unchanged. Plasma free carnitine and acyl-to-free ratios alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms. Compare age, dietary intake, tissue, baseline free carnitine and valproate exposure. Improvement confined to depleted, valproate-exposed systems would support a separate secondary-deficiency indication and give little support to a WWOX-intrinsic effect. A drug-naive WWOX-by-levocarnitine interaction in matched preparations would change that interpretation.

**Why now.** Valproate appears in the medication history of 8/13 people in the WWOX clinical series. Available metabolic analyses question whether mitochondrial cofactor availability or downstream flux limits recovery. Ask which tissues and cell types actually use the proposed substrate, and whether a blood response tracks cerebral function in early development. Treat oral levocarnitine as its own molecule with distinct disposition, separate from acetyl-L-carnitine. **First check.** Compare transport, biosynthesis, canonical shuttle and downstream oxidation transcripts across the same three WWOX datasets. Use deletion of each library in turn to expose fragile signs. Neuronal CPT1C is atypical and cannot stand in for canonical long-chain flux. Seek direct evidence from matched free-carnitine and flux measurements, ideally in astrocytes and neurons separately. Before any trial prioritization, examine age, enteral formulation, gastrointestinal tolerability, baseline nutrition, concomitant medication and free central exposure.

**Untreated WWOX comparison.** SLC22A5/OCTN2, CPT1A and HADHA/HADHB each fell in mouse cortex and hippocampus but rose in human week-15 organoids. Omitting any one library preserved all four directions within each dataset. CRAT, which supports acetyl-group exchange with carnitine, fell in all three (mean difference of log2[1 + expression] of −0.24 cortex, −1.40 hippocampus, −0.28 organoid). The cross-model convergence points to acetyl-group handling as a competing explanation for a carnitine-associated signal. It does not show impaired enzyme activity. Further discriminate altered C2 acetylcarnitine/acetyl-CoA buffering from limited long-chain entry by retaining C0, C2, free CoA, substrate-specific oxidation and functional energy recovery together. The mouse tissues are from related models; the organoid arm has two control and four knockout libraries. Tissue composition, developmental stage, media and lineage remain alternative explanations. **Pediatric feasibility.** An oral formulation has pediatric precedent for documented carnitine deficiency, including infants in the UK product information. Oral bioavailability is about 16% for a solution in the US label's adult comparison after endogenous correction. Gastrointestinal intolerance may affect exposure. Label reports of seizures lack an incidence denominator. Neither pediatric deficiency use nor circulating carnitine confirms an effect in WWOX brain. Pediatric valproate studies with opposite plasma results are next-source leads, currently checked only at abstract depth. Their baseline nutrition, measurement units, treatment duration and polytherapy require comparison.
