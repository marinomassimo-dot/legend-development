# Pediatric oral exposure and the peripheral endpoint

In the SLC52A2 series of Foley et al., 16 of 18 people received oral riboflavin for 1–20 months. The authors report tolerability and stable or improved function in 15, with one lost to follow-up; they describe two clinically improved patients in detail. This is uncontrolled follow-up for a genetic transporter defect. It does not yield an efficacy estimate for children with WWOX loss, nor does the combined category indicate how many improved. Before treatment, 10/17 tested had abnormal plasma acylcarnitines. Nine of those ten had repeat profiles and all nine normalized. In a separate paired measurement, blood or plasma flavins increased in 9/10; the analytes and blood compartments varied. This supports feasible oral administration and systemic response in the studied condition. Those measurements did not establish mitochondrial FAD repletion in neurons. The product and delivery route for an individual WWOX child with feeding difficulties cannot be derived from the cohort counts. [Foley et al., biochemical and treatment results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3891447/)

A pharmacokinetic crossover in nine healthy adults compared three oral exposures with intravenous administration. The authors estimated saturable absorption. The adult single-dose estimate is inappropriate for setting a pediatric WWOX exposure, and plasma peaks are not a proxy for cerebral mitochondrial FAD. [Zempleni et al., primary study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8604671/)

**Next test.** Check whether a neural model with a primary riboflavin transporter defect separates uptake from downstream conversion and demonstrates functional recovery in a vitamin-replete condition. Compare that with WWOX neural flavin or flavoprotein measurements where available. Prioritize a preparation with a measured extracellular vitamin concentration and one direct neuronal functional outcome.
