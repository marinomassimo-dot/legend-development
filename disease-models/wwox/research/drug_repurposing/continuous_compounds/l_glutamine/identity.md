# Does WWOX deficiency reduce free glutamine?

**Decision.** Park supplemental L-glutamine for pediatric WWOX trial priority. The key disease-associated fly feature cannot establish a glutamine deficit. A quantified human WWOX plasma observation falls inside the reporting laboratory's range. The competing effects on excitatory and inhibitory transmitter replenishment still support retaining glutamine as a mechanistic probe.

**Metabolite identity check.** In 14-day female fly heads with neuronal Wwox knockdown, Supplementary Table 4 reports a decrease in peak 791 (m/z 147.0764, retention time 441.52 s, gene effect −0.706 log fold change, adjusted P = 0.0263). The table lists glutamine, serine and other possible annotations, with the assigned name and reliability fields empty. A cross-check of all 14 records in the deposited metabolite assignment file, using m/z and retention time to the published precision, finds no match for this peak and no named glutamine. The same procedure recovers two identified lysine ions. The authors report 85 standard-mapped compounds out of 2,355 detected peaks in the knockdown experiment. The 14 deposited entries are a narrower list across experiments. Absence from the deposited list alone cannot establish that the laboratory lacked a glutamine standard. Replicates were pools of whole heads. None of these measurements resolves extracellular substrate in a human neuron. [Primary study, methods and supplementary table 4](https://doi.org/10.1093/braincomms/fcag385) · [Public metabolite assignments](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metabolights/ws/studies/MTBLS12344/m_MTBLS12344_LC-MS_alternating_hilic_metabolite_profiling_v2_maf.tsv)

**Human measurements.** In the plasma amino-acid table of one child carrying homozygous WWOX c.406A>G, glutamine was 423 (reported interval 246–1182) and glutamate was 56 (10–133); numerical units were omitted. The case does not establish a functional null or central concentrations. Separate human brain microdialysis data illustrate why timing and compartment matter. In six adults with severe brain trauma, during a short IV alanyl-glutamine infusion the reported pre/during/post cerebral glutamine values were 529/549/532 µM. In the separate prolonged-infusion cohort, Results and the Figure 4 caption place the significant increase after the infusion. A randomized crossover in 15 patients reported a plasma glutamine increase of 30% and no detected cerebral extracellular glutamate increase. Neither trauma study measures oral exposure or inhibitory output in WWOX. [Case, amino acid table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9193507/) · [Microdialysis and timing, Results and Fig. 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4227121/) · [Crossover abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17001467/)

**Transport sensitivity.** Using the adult MELAS lumbar CSF means of 336.31 and 433.66 µM as hypothetical substrate values, constant transporter capacity and simple one-substrate kinetics, the maximum difference in occupied fraction is 6.35 percentage points over all positive Km. The two literature comparators yield 6.25 and 5.22 points. Neither extracellular terminal concentrations nor fluxes were measured by this calculation. No conversion from RNA to transport capacity was applied. [Uptake calculation](uptake_sensitivity.py) · [Numerical scenarios](uptake_sensitivity.tsv) · [Isolated transport](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10660562/) · [Cell current](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19751803/)

**Reopen** for directly measured free substrate and a preferential functional improvement in inhibitory transmitter output after matched stimulation in a WWOX model, with the delayed cortical response and preserved physiological drive measured concurrently. A reduced ambiguous peak, reduced RNA or an increase in lumbar CSF alone would leave this decision unchanged. [Opposing synaptic effects](uptake.md)
