# A second possible target of brivaracetam during network bursts

**Decision.** Retain activity-dependent astrocytic glutamate release as a mechanistic comparator. Rat astrocytes showed different responses under two artificial burst schedules. Neither isolated astrocytes from a WWOX model nor children with WWOX deficiency received brivaracetam in these experiments. Basal release offered no evidence of an activity-independent developmental rescue.

## Question fixed before extracting the results

Compare stimulus response increments to the matched no-stimulus baseline for acute and subchronic BRV in primary astrocytes (PMID 35562864). Ask whether physiological and fast-ripple challenges have the same number of pulses per train and integrated stimulation; frequency and total stimulus dose may be confounded. Check if a drug effect survives at matched basal glutamate and in the stimulation pattern observed for untreated WWOX cortex, with specific attention to whether WWOX data measured the required fast oscillation, driver cell and astrocyte genotype. Classify as conditional glial mechanism only unless relevant WWOX astrocyte evidence and the stimulation match. Treat shared laboratory studies as dependent. An altered membrane-associated protein abundance is not a measure of hemichannel flux or mediation.

## What the comparison can identify

Fukuyama and colleagues used primary cultured rat astrocytes. Each stimulation set contained ten pulses per burst and ten bursts. Pulse counts and nominal energisation were matched. Both the within-burst frequency (200 vs 500 Hz) **and** the spacing between bursts (100 vs 40 ms) changed. At seven days the authors report a BRV-by-stimulation-duration interaction for extracellular glutamate under the 500-Hz schedule (F(4,30)=29.5), while the 200-Hz schedule showed little evidence for one (F(2,15)=0.4). Six observations were reported per condition. The schedules had different sampled durations, and comparing these two p-values is not a test of a difference between schedules. Extracellular glutamate can also change through uptake. The measured membrane fraction and pharmacological inhibitor do not by themselves prove mediation by astrocytic SV2A.

**Exposure uncertainty.** Sections 4.3.4 and 4.3.5 specify chronic brivaracetam concentrations of 0, 1 and 3 μM. The results and captions to Figures 5 and 7 specify 0, 3 and 10 μM. The intermediate group cannot be identified as a known absolute exposure from those labels. Acute exposure in Figure 3 and section 4.3.2 is consistent at 0, 3 and 10 μM: the paper reports suppression at 10 μM after 120 minutes, with no detected suppression at 3 μM. No pediatric brain exposure follows from nominal concentrations in culture. [Fukuyama et al., results 2.1–2.2, methods 4.3 and figures 3, 5, 7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9101419/)

**WWOX bridge.** In neuronal Wwox knockout cortex Breton et al. report coupling between a low-frequency component and 100–400 Hz activity. There is partial frequency overlap with fast ripples, but the experiment did not establish the repeated 500-Hz/40-ms astrocyte stimulus. The knockout was neuron-directed. Astrocytes with the WWOX genotypes found in patients may respond differently. [Breton et al., Results, Figure 2 and its supplement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8609180/)

**Discriminating prediction.** Replaying measured WWOX cortical patterns with neuronal activity and drug exposure matched should suppress *excess extracellular glutamate* through an astrocyte contribution if this mechanism is relevant. Compare neuronal versus astrocyte WWOX loss, baseline glutamate uptake and release, and matched pulse counts with frequency and interburst spacing varied independently. Persistence in a preparation without astrocytes would favor a neuronal mechanism for that component. A response confined to the synthetic 500-Hz/40-ms regimen lowers priority even if reproducible. Sustained changes in maturation after activity matching require an independent endpoint. The drug stays at mechanistic priority pending a WWOX response or a relevant model that separates neuronal inhibition from glial release.
