# Pediatric specificity of the secondary D-biotin hypothesis

In Mock et al. (PMID 9523856, author abstract), seven children taking carbamazepine and/or phenytoin, six taking phenobarbital and sixteen untreated healthy controls gave untimed urine samples. The investigators separated biotin-related species by chromatography and measured urinary organic acid by GC-MS. Both exposed groups had higher bisnorbiotin. The combined carbamazepine/phenytoin group also had higher biotin sulfoxide and 3-hydroxyisovalerate; only one of seven had low urinary parent biotin. One cannot separate carbamazepine from phenytoin or attribute these observations to WWOX. Serum or total avidin binding alone can miss a difference in catabolism. A single urine sample cannot distinguish long-term nutritional intake, timing and tissue depletion.

In the separate serum comparison (PMID 21642615, author abstract), 20 children receiving valproate and ten receiving carbamazepine were compared with 75 age- and sex-matched controls. No significant group difference in serum biotin or biotinidase activity was reported. Neither finding supplies a statistical equivalence margin or a contradicted individual longitudinal observation. The three valproate-associated alopecia cases reported normal biotin tests, with uncontrolled follow-up; they provide no evidence of improved myelin or seizure response.

In the earlier seven-person series (PMID 6500847) *all patients were adults*, ages 22–37 in printed Table 1 p. 218. The three controls were 27, 30 and 34. This source must not enter the pediatric denominator. Five people had phenytoin or other mixed background treatment, two valproate alone; four of five in the former group had plasma biotin below the authors' threshold, and the two valproate-alone subjects had no reported abnormal organic acid profile. Plasma was microbiologically assayed, and 24-hour urine sampling differs from the later pediatric study. Selected patients, long exposures, small denominators and differing nutritional and clinical conditions preclude a pooled prevalence or drug-specific causal estimate.

**Checkpoint for integration.** D-biotin remains deprioritized for an unselected trial. The in-silico result nominates medication and nutrition as confounders in baseline biotin classification. It does not show benefit from correcting an inferred deficit. Check if a longitudinal study measures catabolites and a functional carboxylase marker before and after the same antiepileptic in the same child, with an intake comparison; otherwise disposition can be parked with an explicit re-entry criterion. The longitudinal evidence is audited below.

**Longitudinal check, author abstracts.** In 32 children starting valproate, mean serum biotinidase activity fell by the third month and returned to baseline by month six (PMID 18922714). The study did not report simultaneous carboxylase activity or urine catabolites in its abstract. A separate 404-person cross-sectional series included three incident cases with serial plasma biotin measurements. Values rose during week one then fell below baseline later (PMID 3925859). Age of the three, analyte speciation and any overlap with the four organic-acid cases are unresolved at abstract depth; do not combine the two subsets. Neither study supplies within-child correction of a carboxylase abnormality, nutrition matching and WWOX-specific neural function. The group difference in a spot urine study can identify a confounder for future work but cannot justify treatment selection.

**Disposition.** Park D-biotin for a disease-modification trial. A controlled comparison showing that a biochemical deficiency and a functional outcome both respond selectively in WWOX at a measured neural exposure would reopen the question. A response confined to individuals with medication- or diet-related deficiency would define a different research population. The pediatric studies here measure peripheral biochemistry; no WWOX-specific neural or developmental response was measured.
