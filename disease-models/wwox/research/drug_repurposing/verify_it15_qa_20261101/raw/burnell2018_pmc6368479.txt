{"identifiers": {"pmcid": "PMC6368479", "pmid": "29446949", "doi": "10.1021/acs.jmedchem.7b01640"}, "title": "Positive and Negative Allosteric Modulators of N-Methyl-D-Aspartate\n(NMDA) Receptors; Structure-Activity Relationships and Mechanisms of\nAction", "full_text": "1.\n\nIntroduction\n\n-methyl--aspartate (NMDA) receptors are\nligand-gated ion channels which, along with AMPA and kainate receptors, are\nactivated by the brain\u2019s primary excitatory neurotransmitter,-glutamate. The NMDA receptors (NMDARs) are\nknown for their prominent roles in synaptic plasticity and in a number of\nneurological and psychiatric disorders including pain, stroke, epilepsy,\nschizophrenia, post-traumatic stress disorder, depression and various\nneurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer\u2019s and\nParkinson\u2019s. Thus, NMDARs are a desirable\npharmacological target and yet, few NMDAR-based drugs have been successful in the\nclinic due to adverse side effects from off-target activity or adverse effects from\nexcessive NMDAR inhibition. Despite limited success to date, there remains\nsignificant potential for the development of useful agents that modify NMDAR\nactivity. Since the different NMDAR subtypes have significantly varied physiological\nproperties and anatomical distributions, agents that act at distinct NMDAR subtypes\nare expected to have markedly different therapeutic and adverse effects.\nFurthermore, the relatively recent identification of several distinct families of\npositive and negative allosteric modulators has revealed that there is a great\npotential to develop agents with highly specific activities that should provide\nadvantages over prior agents. These allosteric modulators\ndiffer from each other in several functional properties and in their subtype\nselectivity, thus greatly expanding the repertoire of NMDAR pharmacological\nmodulation. In addition to developing therapeutics for the above-mentioned diseases,\nthese agents are also powerful tools for defining the function of NMDARs, and their\nsubtypes, in CNS function. These newer agents act at sites distinct from where the\nwell characterized NAM ifenprodil is thought to bind. As the pharmacology of ifenprodil-related\nagents has been well described over the years, this review\nwill focus mostly on the recent studies regarding compounds acting at novel\nallosteric sites that are distinct from the ifenprodil site.\n\nThe NMDAR is a heterotetrameric complex composed of subunits arising from\nseven homologous genes: GluN1, GluN2A-GluN2D and GluN3A-GluN3B.\nThese subunits form an ion channel pore through the plasma membrane that is\npermeable to Na, K, and Caand gated by the\nsimultaneous binding of-glutamate to the GluN2 subunit and glycine (or-serine) binding to GluN1 or GluN3 subunits. The majority of NMDARs\nare believed to contain two GluN1 subunits and two GluN2 subunits. NMDAR subunits\nhave a modular structure with distinct domains (): the extracellular amino-terminal domain (ATD) \u2013 a modulatory\ndomain which can bind zinc in the case of GluN2A, the ligand binding domains (LBD)\nwhich bind-glutamate (GluN2A-D) or glycine (GluN1 or GluN3), the\ntransmembrane domains (TMD) which forms the pore, and an intracellular C-terminal\ndomain. The ATD is a bi-lobed, clamshell-like structure that can be either open or\nshut; the shut conformation inhibits receptor function.\nThe LBD forms a similar bi-lobed structure where the two extracellular segments (S1\nand S2) form the two halves of the clamshell structure. S1 is the peptide sequence\nbetween the ATD and the first membrane-associated domain (M1) and S2 is the segment\nbetween the third and fourth membrane-associated domains (M3 and M4). Agonist\nbinding in the center cavity of the LBD stabilizes a closed conformation whereas\ncompetitive antagonist binding prevents this closure. The combined closing of the GluN1 and GluN2\nLBDs allosterically transmits a change in the TMD corresponding to a higher\nprobability of an open channel.\n\nAllosteric modulators have several distinct advantages for the development of\nsuitable therapeutic agents compared to the other classes of NMDAR pharmacological\nagents (agonists, competitive antagonists, and channel blockers). Enhanced\nsubtype-selectivity generally minimizes off-target activity and unwanted side\neffects, and allosteric modulators bind to regions other than the highly conserved\nligand binding sites or the channel pore. Thus, allosteric agents have greater\npotential for subtype-selectivity as has been found already. NAMs also have the\npotential of maximally inhibiting less than 100% of the agonist response, thus\npreserving some function and avoiding excessive blockade. Therefore, such partial\nNAMs should have a better safety profile than competitive antagonists and channel\nblockers that can potentially eliminate all activity. A partial agonist at the\nligand binding site also promotes intermediate activity levels, but unlike a NAM, a\npartial agonist could activate otherwise inactive receptors. Positive allosteric\nmodulators (PAMs) also offer distinct advantages. For the treatment of NMDAR\nhypofunction as in schizophrenia, or possibly in other cases of cognitive\ndysfunction, augmenting NMDAR activity would be expected to restore proper function\nby increasing the activity of weakly-activated NMDAR-mediated signals. In contrast\nto PAMs, an NMDAR agonist would activate both appropriate and inappropriate\nreceptors and thus increase system noise and potentially cause excitotoxicity.\n\nGiven these advantages of NMDAR allosteric agents, and the recent\ndemonstration of multiple new classes of allosteric agents, there has been a\nresurgence in NMDAR drug development targeting PAMs and NAMs. This review will focus\nmostly on the recent studies regarding compounds acting at novel allosteric sites\nlocated outside of the ATD on the NMDAR. Structure-activity relationship (SAR)\nstudies used in the development of these allosteric modulators and mechanistic\nstudies aimed at identifying their binding sites and mode of action will be\ndiscussed.\n\n2.\n\nSteroidal Based NMDAR NAMs and PAMs: Neurosteroids and Cholesterols\n\n2.1\n\nNeurosteroids\n\nNeurosteroids and related cholesterols were among the first agents to be\nidentified that can allosterically modulate ionotropic glutamate and GABA\nreceptors. Interestingly, some of these compounds\nare endogenous to neural tissue and they can display both PAM and NAM activity\nat NMDARs. Thus, they may represent natural ligands used to modulate NMDAR\nfunction. Using these steroid structures as starting points, medicinal chemistry\nefforts have gone on to identify synthetic agents which are now lead compounds\nbeing studied for different therapeutic applications. Of the various\nneurosteroids, two of the most extensively studied for their activity at NMDARs\nare pregnenolone sulfate(20-oxo-5-pregnen-3\u03b2-yl sulfate,\ncommonly abbreviated to PS), which displays both PAM and NAM activity, and\npregnanolone sulfate(20-oxo-5\u03b2-pregnan-3\u03b1-yl\nsulfate, commonly abbreviated to 3\u03b15\u03b2S or PAS), which is a NAM\n().\n\n2.1.1\n\nNeurosteroid PAM & NAM SAR\n\nPS is a potentiator of NMDARs and an inhibitor of AMPA (AMPAR), kainate\n(KAR) and GABA-A receptors.\nPAS, on the other hand, displays inhibitory activity at NMDARs, KARs, and\nAMPARs. This was\nfound to be as a result of their respective geometry. PS has a more planar\ngeometry than PAS as a result of the double bond in the second ring (). Investigations into the effect of\nthe geometry were carried out with a number of derivatives with and without a\ndouble bond. It was found that more planar compounds tended to have PAM activity\nand those with a more \u2018bent\u2019 structure were generally\ninhibitors. A\nsummary of the most important SAR observations regarding PAS and PS derivatives\ncan be found in. In general, some\nmodifications of PAS did result in increased NAM potency, whereas PS PAM\nactivity was not significantly improved. Both PAS and PS require a charged group\nat C3; PS PAM activity is dependent on a negatively charged substituent, but in\nthe case of PAS, the group can be positively or negatively charged. Replacement\nof the sulfate group with uncharged groups such as hydrogen or formate\neliminates PAM activity in PS derivatives and NAM activity in PAS derivatives,\nwhile substitution with dicarboxylic acid esters of varied length, from\nhemioxylate to hemiglutarate, generally maintains activity. Replacement of the C3 sulfate of PAS\nwith positively charged-argininyl or 4-(trimethylammonio)butanoyl\nincreases inhibitory activity. Although additions to ringof PS reduced\nactivity, some additions to ringof PAS increased activity\nsignificantly. In particular, compound, with an isobutyl chain instead of the acetyl group of PAS,\nhas an ICof 90 nM at GluN1/GluN2B receptors. It was concluded that the inhibitory\npotency was directly related the lipophilicity of the compounds. Some of this\nrequirement for lipophilicity may be needed for access to the PAS binding site;\nthere is evidence that PAS can enter the membrane to get to its binding\nsite.\n\n2.1.2\n\nNeurosteroid PAM activity and mechanism of action\n\nPS inhibits GABA-A, glycine, AMPA and kainate receptor responses as well\nas those of NMDARs containing GluN2C and GluN2D subunits. In addition to this general inhibitory activity, PS\npotentiates agonist responses at neuronal NMDARsand NMDARs that have GluN2A or GluN2B subunits. Further studies demonstrated that PS has both PAM and\nNAM activity at each of the four GluN1/GluN2A-D receptors with PAM activity\nbeing dominant at GluN2A and GluN2B-containing receptors and NAM activity\npredominating at receptors containing GluN2C or GluN2D. When co-applied with\nagonist, PS potentiates steady-state responses of GluN1/GluN2A and GluN1/GluN2B\nreceptors while inhibiting activity at GluN1/GluN2C and GluN2D\nreceptors. However,\nwhen PS is pre-applied and rapidly replaced by agonist (sequential application),\nPS displays potentiating activity of the initial agonist response at GluN2C and\nGluN2D-containing receptors and enhanced potentiating activity at GluN2A- and\nGluN2B-containing receptors. This is interpreted as reflecting the rapid loss of\ninhibitory activity and the persistence of PS potentiating activity when\nswitching from PS to agonist at each of the four GluN1/GluN2 receptors. Taking\nadvantage of these different drug application paradigms to distinguish NAM and\nPAM activity, PS was found to potentiate the different NMDARs with similar\nECs (~80 \u00b5M) but with greater maximal\npotentiation at receptors containing GluN2A or GluN2B subunits. In contrast, PS\ninhibitory activity was estimated to be several fold more potent at GluN2C and\nGluN2D than at GluN2A and GluN2B.\n\nUnder some conditions, PS appears as a weakly effective PAM. PS\npotentiation of GluN1/GluN2A and GluN1/GluN2B receptor responses declines during-glutamate application, a response described as \u201cdis-use\ndependent\u201d potentiation. This may reflect a higher affinity of PS for the resting\nreceptor state than the activated state and may also contribute to the greater\npotentiation seen with sequential PAM/agonist application mentioned above. Such\ndis-use dependency should result in greater potentiation of phasic, synaptic\nglutamate-mediated NMDAR responses over tonically-activated extrasynaptic NMDAR\nresponses. However, this effect may be offset by reduced ability of PS to\npotentiate GluN2B-mediated responses to high agonist concentrations. PS causes a\nsmall increase in agonist potency at GluN2B-mediated responses. Accordingly, at high-glutamate\nconcentrations, which would obscure a potentiating effect due to increased\nagonist potency, there is relatively little PS potentiation of GluN2B-mediated\nresponses. Reduced PS potentiation in the presence of high-glutamate\nconcentrations is also seen for neuronal NMDAR responses. Since synaptic responses see saturating\nagonist concentrations, while extrasynaptic receptors experience lower agonist\nconcentrations, one might expect PS to preferentially potentiate extrasynaptic\nover synaptic GluN2B-containing receptors. Other than the small effect on-glutamate potency at GluN2B-containing receptors, PS generally\nhas little effect on agonist ECand can potentiate responses due to\nsaturating concentrations of agonist. Thus, PS potentiation must have\nadditional mechanisms to increase NMDAR responses other than by increasing\nagonist potency.\n\nPS slows the rates of both NMDAR desensitization and deactivation. In\nwhole cell recordings of recombinant GluN1/GluN2A and GluN1/GluN2B receptors, PS\nslowed the macroscopic desensitization rate but did not change the extent of\ndesensitization. PS\nalso slowed the deactivation time that is related to-glutamate\nunbinding. Consistent with these findings, PS also\nprolongs NMDAR synaptic currents, which are determined by\nNMDAR deactivation time.\nSeveral of the physiological properties of PS along with other allosteric\nmodulators are summarized in Table.\nPotencies of representative NAMs and PAMs are summarized in Tablesand, respectively.\n\nNMDAR channel properties, as expected, are also changed by\nneurosteroids. Using the rate of MK-801 channel blockade as an index of open\nchannels, PS increases open channel probability of GluN1/GluN2B\nreceptors.\nConsistent with this finding, at single channels in isolated patches, PS\nincreases the frequency of channel openings, has a small, variable effect on\nneuronal NMDAR mean channel open time, and has no effect on single channel\nconductance. Similar results are found\nusing cell-attached patches in which the receptor sees a relatively intact\nintracellular environment, except that in this condition, PS produces a more\nrobust increase in the mean open time. Thus, the\neffect of PS on mean open time depends upon an undiluted intracellular\nenvironment, but the effect on channel opening frequency does not. These results\nare consistent with the observation that the rapid loss of PS potentiation of\nNMDAR responses in outside-out patches is slowed by protein phosphatase\ninhibitors.\n\nIn addition to the NMDAR PAM activity of PS, there is also evidence for\ndirect receptor activation when measuring calcium mobilization or ligand-induced\nreceptor trafficking. In this case, PS could\npossibly be acting as an agonist for mGluR-like activity of NMDARs. Further SAR characterization of this agonist activity\nof PS would be interesting as it may be possible to have biased signaling of\nmGluR-like activity. Since the structurally related compound PAS can form\nnanoparticles, the\nrole of possible PS nanoparticles in this additional activity may need to be\nevaluated.\n\n2.1.3\n\nNeurosteroid NAM activity and mechanism of action\n\nPAS, like PS, has a general inhibitory action at ionotropic glutamate\nreceptors - AMPARs, KARs, and NMDARs, but without\nthe additional NMDAR PAM activity. The inhibitory activity of PAS is\nvoltage-independent and non-competitive with glutamate and glycine; it reduces\nthe maximal agonist responses at each of the four GluN1a/GluN2 subtypes while\nminimally changing agonist potency. PAS inhibition\nis also use-dependent with no evidence of binding to the resting state of the\nreceptor in the absence of agonist. Accordingly, PAS displays less inhibition of synaptic\nNMDAR responses than of steady-state NMDAR responses. This property could be\nuseful in that PAS may weakly inhibit synaptic NMDAR currents while providing\ngreater inhibition of tonically-activated extrasynaptic NMDAR currents that are\nthought to occur in pathological conditions. Among structural variations of the\nPAS structure, some agents were found to display use-independent inhibition in\ncontrast to the use-dependent actions of PAS. Thus, these agents would be\nexpected to differ in their actions on NMDAR responses due to phasic/synaptic or\ntonic/extrasynaptic activation.\n\nIn contrast to PS potentiation, PAS inhibition of GluN1/GluN2A receptors\nin cell-attached patches has no effect on mean open time but instead decreases\nopen probability by increasing mean closed time by specifically increasing the\nduration of long-lived closed states related to desensitization. Interestingly, under some\nrecording conditions, the PAM PS can instead inhibit GluN1a/GluN2A responses. In\nthis case, inhibition is associated with a reduction in mean open time and no\nchange in mean closed time.\nThus, the inhibitory actions of PS and PAS at GluN1a/GluN2A receptors appear to\nhave distinct mechanisms.\n\n2.1.4\n\nNeurosteroid NAM and PAM Binding Sites\n\nThe neurosteroid NAM and PAM binding sites are thought to be located\nextracellularly and to be distinct from each other; neurosteroid NAMs do not display competitive interactions\nwith neurosteroid PAMs.\nNeurosteroids are able to access their modulatory binding site via the membrane.\nIn cell-attached patch recordings, PS administration to the outside of the cell,\nand outside of the patch pipette, can potentiate NMDAR responses under the\npipette. PS is not\nlikely to be acting at an intracellular site since intracellular injections of\nPS do not reduce potentiation to externally applied PS. Thus, PS may be accessing the receptor through a membrane\nroute as has been suggested for inhibitory neurosteroids. From this work, the neurosteroid\nbinding sites are thought to be extracellular and may involve the transmembrane\nregion of the receptor as has been suggested for GABA-A receptors.\n\nThe precise identification of the binding site responsible for PS PAM\nactivity remains unknown but is thought to involve S2 (extracellular loop\nbetween M3 and M4) and M4. PS displays GluN2C-like inhibitory activity at\nchimeras wherein the GluN2A sequence has the S2 domain sequence (plus most of M3\nand M4) replaced by GluN2C's corresponding sequence. Similarly, it was found that a GluN2D\nconstruct in which the 84 amino acids containing the C-terminal third of S2 and\nM4 is replaced with the corresponding sequence from GluN2B displays GluN2B-like\nPS PAM activity. Replacing\nboth of the S2 and M4 portions in GluN2D with the corresponding sequence from\nGluN2B is necessary for PAM activity in the chimeric construct. Conversely,\nreplacing this segment in GluN2B with that from GluN2D eliminated PAM activity\n(but did not generate GluN2D-like NAM activity of PS or PAS). Thus,\nsubunit-specific PS PAM activity appears to be defined by the C-terminal third\nof the S2 domain and M4 while PS/PAS NAM activity may involve additional\nupstream regions. This result is consistent with point mutation results;\nmutating D813A/D815A in GluN2A, which immediately precedes M4, prevents PS\npotentiation, but not PAS inhibition. Conversely, GluN2A-A651T (the homologous site of the\nLurcher mutation at the end of the highly conserved\nSYTANLAF sequence in the M3/S2 linker region)\nreduces PAS inhibition but has no effect on PS potentiation. Likewise, mutations\nin this region of GluN1 and GluN2B, alter PAS potency. Thus, the PAS NAM binding site may be\nat the extracellular / membrane interface of M3/S2 or require allosteric\ninteractions at this location.\n\n2.2\n\nCholesterol derivatives\n\n2.2.1\n\nCholesterol derivative PAM and NAM SAR\n\nThe endogenous compound 24(S)-hydroxycholesterol(24(S)-HC) has a structure and PAM activity similar to that of PS but is more\npotent (EC~ 1 \u00b5M) and thought to have a different\nbinding site (). Also, like PS, SGE201 can access its binding site\nfrom the outside through the plasma membrane but not from the intracellular\nside. The onset and\noffset of PAM activity is very slow, which is consistent with SGE201 accessing\nits binding site through the membrane. These\nnew binding sites have already shown great potential as targets for both therapy and\ntool development; the apparent presence of a diverse range of binding pockets has\nmeant that selective ligands for several of the subtypes have been identified, with\nboth potentiating and inhibitory activity. There are now NAMs selective for GluN2A,\nGluN2B and GluN2C/D and PAMs selective for GluN2A, GluN2C, and GluN2D. Although\nthere are a number of compounds that are of adequate potency for pharmacological\ncharacterization and potential starting points for therapeutic investigations, there\nis still a need for new compound development. Future studies should focus on 1)\nincreasing the structural diversity of lead compounds, 2) improving pharmacodynamic\nproperties and optimizing the balance between water solubility and lipophilicity\n(this has been challenging in some of the SAR studies described herein) in order to\nimprove bioavailability, 3) improving selectivity for individual GluN2 subunits, 4)\ndeveloping compounds that are selective for triheteromeric NMDARs (i.e. those\ncontaining two GluN1 and two different GluN2 subunits), and 5) developing related\nagents to distinguish NMDARs containing GluN3 subunits. The finding that PYD106 is\nineffective on heterotrimeric GluN2C-containing NMDARs, suggests that it may be possible to develop\nother agents that can further distinguish triheteromeric from diheteromeric\nreceptors.\n\nThese endeavors would be aided by structural information obtained from\nprotein-ligand complexes. For agents that bind in the LBD interface, recent\ncrystallography studies have characterized the binding sites in detail. Other agents\nthat bind near or in the membrane or between the ATD and LBD await such detailed\nstructural information, but recent AMPAR structures with homologous binding sites\ncan already provide some insights. As we determine the exact mechanisms of action and establish\nthe structures of the binding pockets, it should become possible to tap into the\npotential of these new NMDAR binding sites even further, with compounds of increased\npotency and selectivity.\n\nThe new classes of drugs represented by these varied NMDAR NAMs and PAMs\nshould offer significantly greater pharmacological control over NMDAR modulation\nthan the previously available competitive antagonists and channel blockers. In\naddition to the improved targeting of specific subtypes, these agents have a\nsurprising diversity in physiological properties at the receptor level that lead to\ndistinct effects at the level of synaptic transmission and neuronal network\nfunction.
