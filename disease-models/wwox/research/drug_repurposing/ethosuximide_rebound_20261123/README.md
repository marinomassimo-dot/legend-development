# Ethosuximide, cortical rebound and sustained firing in WWOX loss

**Decision.** Add separate, voltage-matched measurements of post-inhibitory bursts and sustained firing to a direct Wwox–ethosuximide comparison. Suppressing rebound could reduce synchrony even if the same intervention increases tonic spiking. The measured mean cortical rebound itself was far too small to bridge the estimated distance from rest to spike threshold. A direct acute PTZ-challenge result in six Wwox-null mice per group remains the principal drug signal. The experiment giving that result did not measure any of these currents. [Earlier PTZ reanalysis](../ethosuximide_severity_bounds_20261123/README.md)

## Quantitative Wwox constraint

In P13–17 layer II/III pyramidal neurons, the mean post-inhibitory rebound at release from −200 pA was 0.96 mV in WT, 1.24 in heterozygotes and 1.86 in knockout. Applying those group means to the group's mean resting voltage and spike threshold leaves **34.52, 33.97 and 30.44 mV**, respectively, to threshold. Removing the entire knockout rebound in this arithmetic leaves 32.30 mV, still 2.22 mV closer to threshold than WT with its rebound. The excess rebound accounts for 0.90 of the 4.08 mV difference between these reconstructed margins. Population means and pulse protocols cannot establish individual-cell thresholds, the influence of dendritic input or bursts near threshold. This arithmetic does not assign the rebound to any channel. [Breton et al., Fig. 6 and Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8609180/) · [Cell parameters and conditional margins](threshold_margins.tsv) · [Earlier HCN/sag audit](../hcn_20261107/output/README.md)

We independently read the pyramidal-cell firing curves in the original Supplementary Fig. S4A. Interpolating between adjacent **group means** gives the injected currents at 10 and 15 Hz. On the second axis the authors aligned each cell so that zero was 20 pA below its own rheobase. These are exploratory graphical estimates and do not estimate individual rheobases or treatment effects.

| Target | WT / KO current on original axis | WT minus KO | WT minus KO after rheobase alignment |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 Hz | 121.4 / 78.1 pA | 43.2 pA | 6.3 pA |
| 15 Hz | 149.9 / 105.9 pA | 43.9 pA | 19.9 pA |

At 10 Hz a ±0.5 Hz vertical reading uncertainty gives 38.1–48.5 pA on the original axis and 3.0–13.0 on the aligned axis. At 15 Hz the respective ranges are 38.4–49.5 and 14.9–24.8. The authors report no statistically significant slope difference after alignment; their WT–KO comparison gave p = 0.367. The curves are averages from 13 WT cells/5 animals and 40 KO cells/15 animals. Their early separation is compatible with a substantial threshold-position contribution. A passive resistance calculation gives much higher nominal currents than the actual firing curves and should not be used to predict rheobase or drug efficacy. ## An independent native-channel counterexample

Leresche et al. found that ethosuximide decreased evoked burst spike count by one or two and delayed the first burst spike from 29 to 53 ms at the smallest preceding hyperpolarizations (three thalamocortical neurons). In the tonic protocol at −60 mV, mean current needed to reach firing fell from 250 to 160 pA (three neurons). Fig. 5A/C illustrates increased spike count near threshold at 0.5 mM in a cat lateral geniculate neuron. The main text and caption disagree on the direction of a narrower interspike-frequency comparison in panel B, so the present conclusion uses total spike count and threshold current. In the native rat preparations the same paper found no detectable T-current block at up to 1 mM and reported effects on persistent sodium and sustained, calcium-dependent outward current. Recombinant channel experiments elsewhere gave different results. This is a physiological precedent for divergent rates of tonic and burst firing; it does not demonstrate either drug effect in Wwox-deficient cortex. [Original native study, Results and Figs. 3, 5–6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6792570/) · [Earlier laboratory pharmacology review](../advisor_pilot_20261113/brainstorm_20261116/adv052_ttype_he_20261118/TABLE_PRODUCER_BCD_20261118.md)

The native rat thalamic inactivation curve in Fig. 3 had midpoint −84.5 mV and slope magnitude 4.0 mV (six cells). **Assuming the same curve and channel density**, the Wwox group-mean resting voltages predict 21.2% availability in WT versus 6.2% in KO. A linear voltage estimate at the end of the −200 pA pulse gives −99.8 versus −102.2 mV and 97.9% versus 98.8%. At a matched −80 mV both would have 24.5%. Varying the foreign midpoint by ±10 mV changes the predicted availability markedly. Actual end voltages, channel species and density, junction potentials and finite-duration recovery were not matched. Inactivation availability is distinct from drug sensitivity and from rebound current. This counterexample shows why the larger KO rebound at fixed *current* cannot by itself identify a higher T-channel contribution. [All gating assumptions](availability.tsv)

**Discriminating prediction.** Compare a hyperpolarizing prepulse matched for actual voltage trajectory, with and without concurrent subthreshold excitatory input, against a separate tonic input–output curve measured both before and after rheobase alignment. If ethosuximide decreases rebound spikes and delays their onset while tonic spikes at matched absolute input persist or increase, a change in firing pattern is a better explanation than uniform cortical suppression. Isolate T-current and the persistent inward and sustained outward currents in the same model to assign mechanism. An effect limited to unequal conditioning voltages would favor altered recruitment due to the baseline electrical state. None of these alternatives establishes developmental repair.
