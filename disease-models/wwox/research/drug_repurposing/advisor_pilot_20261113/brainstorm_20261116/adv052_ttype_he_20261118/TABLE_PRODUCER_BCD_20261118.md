## What the evidence does and does not say, by endpoint (task 1)

Acute seizure control. For no agent in this set was an infant or under-3-year acute-control (rescue or single-dose) endpoint found. The only acute-dosing data are adult-mouse EEG results for ESX and ulixacaltamide in a Gabrb3 knock-in line (T1-08, rodent, transfer to infants not made) and rodent data for apinocaltamide (T1-21).

Chronic seizure control. Zonisamide infantile-spasm series disagree: 4/11 stopped spasms and 2 of those relapsed (T1-15), 7/11 responders kept response (T1-16, selected responders), against 2% electroclinical response in 87 largely refractory children (T1-01); in 127 children with DEE/EE at least 50% reduction at 12 months in 53.7%, but with an unadjusted OR of 0.46 for epileptic spasms (T1-02). ESX data are in D/EE-SWAS at age 4-18 years or onset 2-12 years (T1-07, T1-12 to T1-14, abstract level): 43.5% to 57.1% spike-wave-index responders and 71.8% seizure freedom in one prospective cohort. No ESX datum under 3 years and no ulixacaltamide or apinocaltamide patient efficacy datum in infants was found. The only trial pointer is a phase 2 T-type blocker study in D/EE-SWAS named by a review (T1-07).

Development. None found as an endpoint for any T-type-active agent in under-3-year patients. The 127-child study states cognition was not assessable in its window (T1-02). The ESX D/EE-SWAS cohort reports parent-reported language and cognition improvement at age 4-18 (abstract, T1-12). The infantile-spasm series record outcomes only anecdotally (T1-01).

Safety in infants and young children. ESX label: effectiveness and safety below 3 years not established (T1-10). ZNS label: not established under 16 and a paediatric oligohydrosis/hyperthermia warning (T1-11); paediatric rates: adverse events 14.2% and metabolic acidosis in 13 of 27 tested children (T1-01, T1-02), reporting-rate analysis (T1-19). ESX tolerability data in this set are from adults (T1-09). No infant safety data for ulixacaltamide or apinocaltamide (T1-20 is healthy adults).

Aggravation and paradoxical effects. Labelled for ESX alone in mixed epilepsy: more tonic-clonic seizures in some patients (T1-10). D/EE-SWAS: sodium channel blockers may worsen the electrical status; the review ranks steroids first (T1-07). Rats under about 25 days: ESX ineffective, with modified or new seizure patterns and a higher clonic-seizure incidence at 12 days (T2-16, T2-18, T2-19), which is the nearest datum to a paradoxical effect, rodent only. Channel-variant logic: a review suggests T-type blockers for gain-of-function and avoidance in loss-of-function variants, graded as theoretical for CACNA1G (T1-06); the CACNA1G variants read here are gain-of-function or mixed (T1-03, T1-05, T1-22); mixed variants undercut a uniform rule. Seizure-type breakdown for ZNS: absence 8/10 and myoclonic 8/16 responders at 12 months, spasms lower (T1-02); the 80% Dravet figure is of n = 5. No seizure-type breakdown for ESX under 3 years was found.

## T-type dependent versus other targets (task 2)

Cell-level pharmacology. Two preparations disagree. Cloned human Cav3.1, Cav3.2 and Cav3.3: block, state-dependent, higher affinity for inactivated channels, and a persistent-current IC50 of 0.6 mM for ESX (abstract-level, T2-02; per-channel figures not seen). Native rat thalamic neurons: no effect on low-threshold calcium current up to 1 mM in slices, dissociated neurons, reticular neurons and GAERS-rat neurons, but 60% reduction of persistent sodium current and reduced Ca-activated K current (full text, T2-01), versus the earlier reduction up to about 40% reported by others (T2-01 background, T2-03 abstract). The authors of T2-01 could not explain the discrepancy.

Other targets. Persistent sodium current and Ca-activated K current (T2-01); GIRK channels (T2-04, abstract); a commentary summarises the three as contributing alongside T-type (T2-05, abstract; full text closed). A channel-block assignment in vivo is therefore not unique to Cav3.

Knockout literature. Cav3.1 null: resistant to GABA(B)-agonist-induced or GBL/baclofen spike-and-wave, not to bicuculline or 4-aminopyridine seizures (T2-07, T2-08); Cav3.1 null on tottering, lethargic or stargazer backgrounds suppresses or strongly reduces spike-wave (T2-09, T2-10, secondary); Cav3.1 null protected in maximal electroshock tonic seizures but Cav3.2 null not (T2-11, abstract); Cav3.2 null: largely prevented epileptogenesis after status (T2-08, T2-12 quoting it) and no change in an Scn1a-null background (T2-08); Cav3.3 null: raised GBL/GHB-induced susceptibility, and the Cav3.2/3.3 double null stayed GHB-susceptible despite no nRT bursting (T2-10, secondary). Whether any study gave ESX (or another T-type blocker) to a Cav3 knockout and compared with wild type: not found by these queries (this is not an absence claim; queries 3, 4, 7, 9 and 11 and the full texts T2-08 to T2-12 gave no such experiment). Related but different: ESX responds in a Cav3.1-overexpressing mouse (T2-10, secondary) and in an alpha2delta-1 overexpressing mouse (T2-15).

Ontogeny. All age-by-ESX data found are in rats (T2-16 to T2-21): ESX reliably effective against PTZ only in adults or 25-day-olds, ineffective or pattern-modifying at younger ages, with a clonic-seizure increase at 12 days in the picrotoxin model; a separate PTZ model still showed suppression of spike-and-wave rhythm at 18 days. No mouse age-by-ESX datum was found. Provoked-assay dependence on T-type versus other targets therefore cannot be separated from developmental channel expression or other-target effects in these data, and the Leresche slices came from 10-18-day rats where ESX did not change the low-threshold calcium current (T2-01).
