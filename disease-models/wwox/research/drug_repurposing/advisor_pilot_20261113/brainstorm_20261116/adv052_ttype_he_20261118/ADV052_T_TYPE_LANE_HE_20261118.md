## What transcripts cannot show about T-type current or burst firing
Transcript counts do not give channel number at the membrane, glycosylation or splice state, the voltage dependence of inactivation, the share of Cav3.1, Cav3.2 and Cav3.3 in a given neuron, or the other conductances that decide whether a low-threshold calcium spike becomes a burst (resting potential, the hyperpolarisation-activated current Ih, potassium and leak channels, persistent sodium). In whole tissue a change of cell mix (reported here and elsewhere for interneurons and oligodendrocytes) moves bulk values without any change per neuron. None of the four datasets sampled thalamic relay or reticular neurons, where Cav3.1 and Cav3.2 carry the burst in rodent absence models. A transcript difference is therefore a reason to measure current, not evidence of an excess or a deficit. The only held functional neuron data in a Wwox-deficient preparation (Breton 2021, PMID 34634460, as recorded in SCI's lane note `ketamine_deep_adv_20261117/KDS_LANE_B1_B4_D_SCI_20261117.md`; not re-read here) report depolarised pyramidal neurons with increased sag current, an Ih footprint, and no T-type measurement.

## (c) T-type dependent versus other targets (kept apart from Cheng 2020)
* **Cell level, two preparations disagree.** Cloned human Cav3.1, 3.2 and 3.3: state-dependent block, persistent-current IC50 0.6 mM (abstract level; per-channel values not seen). Native rat thalamic neurons (full text, 10-18 day slices, dissociated and reticular neurons, GAERS rats): no effect on the low-threshold calcium current up to 1 mM, but a 60% reduction of persistent sodium current and a reduced calcium-activated potassium current; the authors could not explain the difference from earlier reports.
* **Other targets with data:** persistent sodium and calcium-activated potassium currents (above); GIRK block (abstract; the mouse GNB1 paper).
* **Knockouts.** Cav3.1 null: resistant to GBL and baclofen-induced spike-and-wave, not to bicuculline or 4-aminopyridine seizures; protected in maximal electroshock while Cav3.2 null is not; Cav3.2 null: epileptogenesis after status largely prevented; Cav3.3 null: more susceptible to GBL or GHB. **Whether anyone gave ethosuximide or another T-type blocker to a Cav3 knockout and compared with wild type: not found by these queries (5 query sets plus the reviews); it is the experiment that would separate T-type dependence from the other targets.** Related but different: ethosuximide suppresses seizures in a Cav3.1-overexpressing mouse and in an alpha2delta-1 overexpressing mouse (secondary and abstract).
* **Net:** a T-type account of ethosuximide is contested at the cell level and untested by a knockout-by-drug comparison; a selective Cav3 blocker (ulixacaltamide) acts beside ethosuximide in one adult mouse model, which keeps the T-type circuit relevant to absence-type seizures without showing that ethosuximide acts through it.

## (d) Ontogeny of ethosuximide's anti-PTZ efficacy: rat versus mouse
* **Rat (Prague laboratory; six abstracts, DATO at abstract level):** ethosuximide "reliably protected only adult and 25-day-old rats" against metrazol (Mares 1981); in young animals CD50 was not changed and seizure patterns were modified or new (Mares 1983, 1987: "effective only in rats of 25 or more days of age: in 18 day old rats it modified the PTZ seizure pattern"); at 18 days the spike-and-wave rhythm of a low-PTZ model was still suppressed while minimal seizures were not (Mares 1998); in the picrotoxin model ethosuximide increased clonic seizures at 12 days (Velisek 1993); the PTZ minimal seizure itself exists reliably only from about 18 days (Velisek 1992). So the rat result is **endpoint-bound and tied to the age at which the elicited seizure pattern exists**, not a clean age threshold, and it is not shown to be T-type related.
* **Mouse, PTZ or another provoked assay in pups at P12 to P28: no datum found by two routes** (the delegate's keyword sets and the citing and related articles of the rat papers; my two further queries, 112 and 6 hits screened by title). One line: no mouse ethosuximide-against-PTZ ontogeny datum exists in what these routes reach.
* **One mouse-pup ethosuximide datum in a different assay, found by me and missed by the delegate (abstract level, primary unread):** SSADH-deficient mice (Cortez 2004, PMID 15582027): recordings from P10 to P21, absence-like seizures with 7 Hz spike-and-wave appeared in the second week and evolved into convulsive seizures late in the third week; "the absence seizures in SSADH(-/-) were abolished by ethosuximide (ETX) and the GABA(B)R antagonist CGP 35348". The abstract does not state the age at which ethosuximide was given (inside the P10-P21 window); the model is spontaneous and GHB- and GABA(B)-driven, not PTZ-provoked. A second hit, a freeze-lesion cortical dysplasia mouse (Sun 2016, adult recordings), reports spike-waves "temporarily alleviated by ethosuximide" (adults; not an ontogeny datum).
* **What transfers:** the rat PTZ result cannot be carried to Cheng 2020's mouse assay as an age floor: no mouse maturation map of the PTZ response was found; ethosuximide can abolish absence-type activity in mouse pups of about two to three weeks in at least one model; the PTZ endpoint, the dose and the age matter and none of the three is stated for the Cheng 2020 test (ADV's check: age not stated, wild-type PTZ stages peak at about 1).

## What this lane adds to IT's comparative table (decisive differences only)
1. **The ethosuximide arm rests on a descriptive pattern, not on a T-type rationale in WWOX deficiency**: (a) no source links WWOX loss to Cav3 by two routes and the four held transcript sets disagree; (c) ethosuximide's T-type action is contested in native thalamic neurons and no Cav3 knockout was given the drug. Both are needed for a T-type rationale; neither exists.
2. **"Knockout-selective pattern = young-age plus wild-type floor" is a rat inference** (endpoint- and pattern-bound, with a PTZ seizure type that is not the one used by Cheng) and the mouse datum that exists (SSADH pups) points the other way for absence-type activity. It should be worded as unresolved, not as the leading explanation.
3. **For infants no ethosuximide datum under 3 years exists**; the label says not established, and warns of more tonic-clonic seizures in mixed epilepsy, which matters when several seizure types coexist. A transfer of an effect from a PTZ-provoked mouse assay to an infant population has no bridge in this evidence.
4. **A decisive design feature for a first in vivo experiment** (not a recommendation to use any agent): a selective Cav3 blocker arm and the ethosuximide arm in the same age-matched, genotype-crossed design with a vehicle arm per genotype and endpoints by seizure type; otherwise a positive ethosuximide result cannot be attributed to Cav3.

## Does the T-type axis deserve its own next step?
**Not as another literature step.** What remains zero-spend is small (the primary texts behind the knockout reviews, Cortez 2004 and the Prague papers, which are closed or abstract-only); none of it can supply the missing observations, which are laboratory measurements (Cav3 transcript or current by cell type in Wwox-null thalamic neurons; ethosuximide and a selective blocker in a Cav3 knockout or a Wwox-null design with age and endpoint fixed). I would park the axis as a design requirement of the first in vivo experiment (item 4 above) and give it no place of its own in the loop until a laboratory exists; the one cheap reopen is the SSADH primary text if a human visit list is made.
