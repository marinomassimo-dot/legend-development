All ten items come from memory only. No tool was used and nothing was checked against a source. Wherever I am unsure of a finding I say so. "Usual reading" means the standard amyloid-cascade framing: Aβ is made by APP processing, and tau, synaptic and mitochondrial injury follow downstream.

H1. Statement: Loss of WWOX removes its WW1-domain brake on tau-directed kinases (GSK3β, JNK1), so tau is hyperphosphorylated and mislocalised and Aβ-driven synaptotoxicity increases. · Evidence: Supporting: WWOX binds tau and GSK3β in neurons, and reduced WWOX has been reported in AD hippocampal neurons (Sze, Chang and colleagues, roughly 2004 to 2014). Aβ fragments reportedly induce WWOX Y33 phosphorylation and JNK-associated WWOX redistribution, which suggests WWOX is a responder. Against: this evidence is largely from cell culture and human tissue immunostaining. I know of no WWOX-deficient AD mouse showing the effect, and I do not recall the data as causal. · Usual reading assumes: Aβ-induced tau pathology is driven by a gain of kinase activity. This hypothesis makes it a loss of scaffold-mediated restraint. · Rejecting observation: in WWOX-deficient neurons exposed to oligomeric Aβ, measure phospho-tau (for example S202/T205 and S396) at matched total tau and GSK3β activity. No increase over WWOX-intact neurons rejects it. · Discriminator vs H2 (nearest rival, a non-tau excitability route): block GSK3β or knock down tau in WWOX-null neurons. If the added Aβ toxicity disappears, H1 is favoured. If it persists, H2 is favoured.

H2. Statement: WWOX loss causes neuronal hyperexcitability and network hypersynchrony, which raises activity-dependent Aβ release and amplifies Aβ-induced excitotoxicity. · Evidence: Supporting: WWOX deficiency causes epilepsy in humans (WWOX-related DEE) and in mice, and activity-dependent Aβ generation is well established. Against: I know of no direct measurement of Aβ in WWOX-deficient brain. Seizure-related changes can also be secondary to the hypoglycaemia and early lethality of full KO mice. · Usual reading assumes: Aβ causes hyperexcitability. This hypothesis reverses the direction, with WWOX-driven hyperexcitability upstream of Aβ. · Rejecting observation: in a neuron-restricted, adult-induced WWOX deletion with EEG-silent animals or silenced slices, measure interstitial or secreted Aβ40/42. No rise rejects it. · Discriminator vs H1: suppress activity pharmacologically or chemogenetically in WWOX-deficient neurons. If Aβ levels and toxicity normalise, H2 is favoured. If they remain high, an activity-independent route (H1, H3, H4) is favoured.

H3. Statement: WWOX loss shifts APP trafficking or processing toward the amyloidogenic route, by impairing Golgi/TRAPP-complex function (WWOX–TRAPPC6A) or endosomal sorting, so more BACE1/γ-secretase cleavage occurs. · Evidence: none known for WWOX and APP processing directly. A WWOX–TRAPPC6A link and WWOX Golgi localisation have been reported, but the connection to APP is my extrapolation. This is an IPOTESI. · Usual reading assumes: APP processing is controlled by secretase expression and genetics (APP, PSEN). This hypothesis makes a trafficking scaffold the lever. · Rejecting observation: compare the Aβ42/40 ratio, sAPPβ and CTFβ in WWOX-null versus control neurons. No difference rejects it. · Discriminator vs H2: measure Aβ production in a quiet, activity-blocked preparation. Increased production would favour H3, and no change would favour H2.

H4. Statement: WWOX loss impairs mitochondrial and redox homeostasis (increased ROS, altered metabolic programming), which sensitises neurons to Aβ-induced mitochondrial failure. · Evidence: Supporting: WWOX is an SDR-family protein. It has been reported to localise to mitochondria. Its loss is linked to ROS, a glycolytic or HIF1α shift and altered metabolism in non-neuronal cells, and WWOX KO mice are hypoglycaemic. Against: the neuronal data are sparse, and the AD-specific link is not shown. · Usual reading assumes: mitochondrial dysfunction is a consequence of Aβ. This hypothesis makes it a pre-existing vulnerability. · Rejecting observation: in WWOX-deficient neurons before any Aβ exposure, measure mitochondrial membrane potential, oxygen consumption and ROS. A normal baseline rejects it. · Discriminator vs H5: add an antioxidant or restore the mitochondrial defect in WWOX-null cells. If the Aβ sensitivity is rescued, H4 is favoured. If a pro-death signalling state remains, H5 is favoured.

H5. Statement: Without WWOX, Aβ-triggered stress signalling (JNK, p53/p73-linked) is uncontrolled, so a given Aβ load kills more neurons. · Evidence: Supporting: WWOX interacts with JNK1, p53 and p73. WWOX is described as pro-apoptotic and as stress-responsive after Aβ25-35 exposure in some reports. Against: a pro-apoptotic role would predict that loss of WWOX protects against death, which is the opposite direction. This is a real conflict and the evidence is contradictory. · Usual reading assumes: WWOX acts as a pro-apoptotic effector. This hypothesis requires a net protective scaffolding role in neurons. · Rejecting observation: expose WWOX-null and control neurons to a fixed oligomeric Aβ dose. Equal or lower death in the null cells rejects it. · Discriminator vs H4: measure JNK/p53 activation after Aβ in null cells while mitochondrial function is held normal. Elevated signalling alone favours H5, and a mitochondrial defect alone favours H4.

H6. Statement: WWOX loss reduces autophagic and lysosomal clearance, so Aβ and APP fragments accumulate. · Evidence: Supporting: WWOX has been reported to affect autophagy in non-neuronal settings. Impaired autophagy is a recognised amplifier of Aβ burden. Against: the neuronal link is not established, and I am not certain of the autophagy data. · Usual reading assumes: clearance failure is a late consequence of ageing and aggregation. This hypothesis makes it an early consequence of WWOX loss. · Rejecting observation: measure autophagic flux (LC3-II with and without a lysosomal inhibitor) and Aβ half-life in WWOX-null neurons. Normal flux and normal half-life reject it. · Discriminator vs H3: pulse-chase labelled Aβ. Slower clearance with normal production favours H6, and faster production with normal clearance favours H3.

H7. Statement: WWOX loss disrupts ErbB4/neuregulin signalling and synaptic organisation at interneuron-pyramidal synapses, which lowers synaptic resilience and reduces compensation for Aβ synaptotoxicity. · Evidence: Supporting: WWOX binds ErbB4 through PPxY motifs, ErbB4/NRG1 are implicated in AD and in excitation-inhibition balance, and WWOX-related DEE is a synaptic and network disorder. Against: I know of no direct Aβ test. · Usual reading assumes: Aβ synaptotoxicity acts mainly through NMDA/mGluR5/PrPC receptors. This hypothesis adds an inhibitory-circuit vulnerability. · Rejecting observation: restore ErbB4 signalling in WWOX-null neurons and measure Aβ-induced spine or synapse loss. No rescue rejects it. · Discriminator vs H2: test whether interneuron-specific WWOX deletion reproduces the Aβ effect. If it does, H7 is favoured. If excitability changes arise equally from pyramidal-cell deletion, H2 is favoured.

H8. Statement: WWOX loss alters cholesterol and lipid handling in neurons, which raises membrane cholesterol and lipid-raft organisation and so promotes amyloidogenic APP cleavage and Aβ aggregation. · Evidence: Supporting: WWOX has been associated with lipid and lipoprotein traits in human genetic studies, and its SDR domain suggests lipid-metabolic activity. Against: this is circumstantial and I do not recall any neuronal lipid or Aβ data. This is an IPOTESI. · Usual reading assumes: lipid effects on Aβ come through APOE and ABCA7-type genes. This hypothesis proposes WWOX as a modifier. · Rejecting observation: lipidomic and cholesterol profiling of WWOX-null neurons. No change in cholesterol or raft markers rejects it. · Discriminator vs H3: deplete cholesterol in null cells. If the amyloidogenic shift is lost, H8 is favoured. If it persists, a trafficking cause (H3) is favoured.

H9. Statement: WWOX loss impairs Wnt/Dishevelled and related signalling, which lowers neuronal survival and synaptic maintenance and removes a protective pathway that normally buffers Aβ toxicity. · Evidence: Supporting: WWOX interacts with Dishevelled and modulates Wnt signalling, and Wnt loss of function is implicated in AD (Dkk1 induction by Aβ, GSK3β). Against: the neuronal WWOX–Wnt evidence is mostly indirect, and the pathway overlaps with H1 through GSK3β. · Usual reading assumes: Wnt dysfunction is induced by Aβ. This hypothesis puts it upstream. · Rejecting observation: activate Wnt/β-catenin in WWOX-null neurons and score Aβ toxicity. No change rejects it. · Discriminator vs H1: check β-catenin stability against tau phosphorylation. A β-catenin defect without a tau change favours H9, and the reverse favours H1.

H10. Statement: Reduced WWOX is a developmental and age-related loss that lowers brain reserve (smaller hippocampal and network capacity), so Aβ produces more clinical and cellular dysfunction at the same plaque load. · Evidence: Supporting: WWOX loss causes neurodevelopmental abnormalities in humans, and WWOX loci have been reported in AD or cognition association studies, but I do not remember the effect sizes. Against: a reserve effect would not be a mechanism specific to Aβ, and it has not been tested in AD models. · Usual reading assumes: toxicity scales with Aβ burden. This hypothesis makes vulnerability independent of burden. · Rejecting observation: compare plaque burden against neuronal or synaptic loss across WWOX genotypes in an AD model. A proportional relationship at a fixed burden, with no genotype effect, rejects it. · Discriminator vs H5: separate load-dependence from threshold. If WWOX-null neurons are already impaired at zero Aβ, H10 is favoured. If they differ only after Aβ exposure, a signalling or sensitisation mechanism (H5) is favoured.

Cross-cutting caveat: the best-supported item is the tau and GSK3β/JNK interaction (H1), and it is correlative. The WWOX-DEE phenotype supports the excitability and circuit items (H2, H7, H10) only indirectly. H3, H6 and H8 have no direct neuronal evidence that I know of. H5 conflicts with the usual pro-apoptotic description of WWOX.
