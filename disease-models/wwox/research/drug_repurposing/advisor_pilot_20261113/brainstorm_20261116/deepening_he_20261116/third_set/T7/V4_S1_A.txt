ENUMERATION

Defect in physical-chemical terms (DATO from the question, INFERENZA for the mechanism). A receptor guanylyl cyclase is a single-pass membrane protein. It has an extracellular ligand-binding domain, a transmembrane helix, an intracellular kinase-like homology domain (KHD) that binds ATP and acts as an allosteric regulator, a hinge or coiled-coil dimerisation region, and a C-terminal cyclase domain. Its cycle is: ligand binding, then a conformational switch relayed through the KHD, then dimeric catalytic-domain closure, then Mg2+ or Mn2+-dependent conversion of GTP to cGMP plus pyrophosphate, then desensitisation by phosphorylation and by loss of ligand. The defect is a gain of function. The switch is released too easily (reduced autoinhibition, a lower activation barrier, or hypersensitivity to ligand), so the net rate of GTP to cGMP is too high. The steady-state cGMP level is the balance of synthesis against PDE hydrolysis and ABCC4/ABCC5 efflux, and the effectors are PKG2 (PRKG2), cGMP-gated or cGMP-inhibited PDEs, and CFTR through PKG2. The step that is impaired is therefore the off-state of the activity cycle.

Axis (a), catalytic or activity cycle:
- ATP occupancy of the KHD (allosteric).
- Mg2+/Mn2+ metal site and metal identity.
- GTP substrate concentration and the Km-dependence of the cyclase.
- Product pyrophosphate (product inhibition; pyrophosphatase).
- Redox state, because cysteines in the extracellular domain and ROS influence ligand binding.
- Luminal and cytosolic pH, which tunes uroguanylin against guanylin potency.
- Temperature.

Axis (b), folding and stability:
- ER chaperones: Hsp90, Hsp70, calnexin.
- Chemical or osmolyte chaperones.
- Pharmacological ligands that stabilise the KHD or the ligand-binding domain.
- Temperature-sensitive folding.
- The GOF allele may be over-stable, and destabilisation could be the lever.

Axis (c), assembly, trafficking, localisation:
- N-glycosylation and ER export, including COPII adaptors.
- Dimerisation, with a dominant-negative partner as a lever.
- Apical sorting and lipid-raft or palmitoylation targeting.
- Ligand-site binders: decoy receptors or ligand-sequestering agents, and the ligands themselves (guanylin, uroguanylin; STa-like enterotoxin as the extreme).

Axis (d), substrate and metabolite supply:
- Guanine nucleotide pool: IMPDH, GMP synthase, de novo against salvage, and dietary purine or guanosine.
- cGMP export by ABCC4/ABCC5, which is a transport lever.
- Ion and fluid supply: Cl-, HCO3-, Na+ and water transport and their effect on luminal pH and ligand potency.
- Dietary nitrate, NO and L-arginine, which feed the soluble-GC parallel pool.
- Dietary fibre and short-chain fatty acids, which feed epithelial energetics.

Axis (e), degradation and turnover:
- cGMP hydrolysis by PDE5, PDE9, PDE10, PDE1 and PDE2.
- Receptor desensitisation by KHD phosphorylation and by protein phosphatases.
- Receptor ubiquitination, endocytosis and lysosomal degradation.
- Proteasome and ERAD.
- Ligand turnover: proguanylin processing by proteases.

Axis (f), downstream effector or parallel bypass:
- PKG2 (PRKG2) and CFTR phosphorylation.
- cGMP inhibition of PDE3, which raises cAMP and PKA.
- NHE3 inhibition and the resulting fluid secretion.
- Soluble GC and the NO pool as a competing cGMP source.
- Notch/Atoh1 and Wnt as the secretory-fate machinery.
- NF-kB/TNF/IL-22 cytokine loops.
- Tight-junction machinery (claudins, ZO-1, MLCK).

Axis (g), cell type, timing, dosage:
- Epithelium-specific against global effect.
- Stem-cell compartment (Lgr5) against differentiated cells.
- Developmental against adult onset.
- Heterozygous against homozygous dosage.
- Trans-heterodimer with a null allele.
- Reversibility after inducible removal of the GOF allele.
- Compensation by uroguanylin from the proximal gut.

H1. Statement: Excess ATP occupancy, or loss of an ATP-dependent autoinhibitory constraint in the KHD, lets the cyclase domain close more readily, so reducing KHD-ATP coupling lowers cGMP and restores the epithelium. · Evidence: ATP binding to the KHD is known to potentiate GC-C and other receptor GCs, and GOF human variants sit in or near the KHD (I recall GUCY2C GOF alleles in this region, but the precise residues should be checked). Whether the mouse allele acts this way is none known. · Usual reading assumes the GOF is a ligand-sensitivity problem. This reading changes it to a constitutive ATP-allosteric problem. · Rejection: an ATP-binding-null KHD introduced in cis in the GOF allele leaves cGMP and barrier defects unchanged. · Discriminating manipulation: a cis ATP-pocket-dead KHD mutation in the GOF allele, against Gucy2c ligand knockout (H4). Predicts that cGMP falls at saturating ligand and barrier normalises; ligand knockout would instead leave basal ATP-driven activity.

H2. Statement: The mutant cyclase is hyper-responsive to Mn2+ over Mg2+ (or catalytically more metal-promiscuous), so the intracellular free divalent-metal composition sets the pathologically high rate. · Evidence: Mn2+ is known to stimulate membrane GCs in vitro, and Mn2+ over Mg2+ metal preference is a known property of cyclase domains. In the intestine none known. · Usual reading assumes metal is saturating and irrelevant. This reading changes it to metal composition as a rate-limiting variable. · Rejection: a metal-site point mutation that shifts Mn2+/Mg2+ selectivity leaves cGMP unchanged. · Discriminating manipulation: epithelial manipulation of the Mn2+ or Mg2+ transport route (class: Slc39a8 or Slc30a10 gene dosage, or Mg2+ channel TRPM6/7 haploinsufficiency) against H3 GTP pool. Predicts that cGMP is metal-sensitive with the allele but not with wild-type, and barrier follows cGMP; H3's nucleotide manipulation changes cGMP independent of metal.

H3. Statement: Substrate GTP supply is rate-limiting at the high catalytic capacity of the GOF cyclase, so restricting guanine-nucleotide synthesis lowers cGMP and, with it, the epithelial damage. · Evidence: IMPDH inhibition is known to lower cellular GTP pools, and GTP is not saturating for all cyclases. In this model none known. · Usual reading assumes GTP is in excess. This reading changes it to substrate-limited flux. · Rejection: partial IMPDH2 or GMPS reduction lowers GTP without any drop in cGMP. · Discriminating manipulation: epithelial Impdh2 or Gmps haploinsufficiency (or a purine-salvage knockout such as Hprt1) against metal change (H2). Predicts that cGMP falls and barrier improves, but proliferation and differentiation may also change, which is a confound; the metal manipulation would not move GTP.

H4. Statement: The GOF receptor is ligand-hypersensitive, so the pathological output is driven by normal levels of endogenous guanylin or uroguanylin (modulated by luminal pH), and removing ligand normalises cGMP. · Evidence: GC-C is activated by guanylin and uroguanylin and is pH-sensitive for these ligands; STa is the extreme case. Guanylin/uroguanylin knockout mice have been reported with barrier and proliferation phenotypes, though I am not certain of the details. For GOF alleles none known. · Usual reading assumes the GOF is constitutive. This reading changes it to a ligand-gain problem. · Rejection: Guca2a/Guca2b double knockout does not lower cGMP in GOF mice. · Discriminating manipulation: Guca2a/Guca2b (guanylin/uroguanylin) deletion in the GOF background, against the ATP-pocket-dead mutation (H1). Predicts cGMP falls to near basal and barrier improves if ligand-dependent; if cGMP stays high the GOF is constitutive.

H5. Statement: The GOF variant lowers folding quality control (or over-stabilises the receptor), so the mutant accumulates; reducing chaperone support or lowering steady-state protein level lowers cGMP. · Evidence: Hsp90 dependence is known for several receptor GCs and kinases. Specific to GC-C GOF none known. · Usual reading assumes the same protein level and a faster enzyme. This reading changes it to a higher level of active receptor. · Rejection: GOF and wild-type receptor have the same steady-state protein level and half-life. · Discriminating manipulation: Hsp90-axis reduction (class: HSP90 inhibitor at mechanism level, or Hsp90 co-chaperone Cdc37 heterozygosity) against reducing gene dosage (H15). Predicts that receptor protein falls proportionally with cGMP and barrier improves; gene dosage reduces mRNA and protein and cannot be told apart by that alone, but a proteasome block would reveal the chaperone route.

H6. Statement: Excess apical surface delivery or glycosylation of the receptor, rather than its specific activity, sets cGMP, and ER-export or glycosylation limitation lowers the signal. · Evidence: N-glycosylation is required for GC-C surface expression, and apical sorting is a property of intestinal epithelium. For the GOF model none known. · Usual reading assumes the same surface density. This reading changes it to a surface-density problem. · Rejection: a surface-biotinylation comparison shows equal apical receptor and normal glycoform patterns in GOF mice. · Discriminating manipulation: partial loss of ER export or N-glycan processing in epithelium (class: Sec24 isoform deletion or Mgat gene dosage) against H5. Predicts that surface receptor and cGMP fall together; total protein stays unchanged for export limitation but falls for chaperone limitation.

H7. Statement: The GOF variant escapes ligand-induced desensitisation (loss of phosphorylation-dependent inactivation of the KHD), so enhancing desensitisation or removing phosphatase action restores the off-state. · Evidence: GC-C phosphorylation of the KHD is known to be needed for ligand activation, and dephosphorylation is a classic desensitisation route in receptor GCs. Quantitative role in the GOF model none known. · Usual reading assumes that the GOF is a stronger on-switch. This reading changes it to a weaker off-switch. · Rejection: phospho-null and phospho-mimic KHD variants in cis do not alter cGMP in GOF mice. · Discriminating manipulation: phosphosite-null/mimic knock-in in the GOF allele, against H1. Predicts that a phospho-mimic that locks desensitisation lowers cGMP over time after ligand; ATP-pocket mutation changes basal activity instead. Barrier follows cGMP.

H8. Statement: Insufficient cGMP-PDE capacity (PDE5, PDE9, PDE10, PDE1) fails to buffer the high synthesis rate, so raising PDE activity or removing PKG-dependent feedback restores steady-state cGMP. · Evidence: PDE5 is activated by PKG phosphorylation, which gives negative feedback, and PDE inhibitors raise cGMP in intestinal epithelium (known in principle). For this model none known. · Usual reading assumes synthesis is the sole variable. This reading changes it to a synthesis/hydrolysis imbalance. · Rejection: epithelial PDE5/PDE9 loss leaves cGMP in GOF mice unchanged. · Discriminating manipulation: epithelial PDE5A or PDE9A overexpression or knockout, against ABCC4 efflux knockout (H9). Predicts that PDE gain lowers cGMP and improves barrier, while PDE loss raises cGMP and worsens it; ABCC4 loss changes intracellular cGMP with a rise in extracellular cGMP in the opposite direction.

H9. Statement: cGMP efflux through ABCC4 (MRP4) or ABCC5 is the limiting route for removing cGMP, so increased export would lower intracellular cGMP while extracellular cGMP rises. · Evidence: ABCC4 and ABCC5 are known cGMP and cAMP exporters and are expressed in intestinal epithelium; extracellular cGMP has been reported in GC-C activation. For this GOF model none known. · Usual reading assumes degradation is the clearance route. This reading changes it to export as the clearance route. · Rejection: Abcc4/Abcc5 deletion does not raise intracellular cGMP. · Discriminating manipulation: Abcc4/Abcc5 knockout in the epithelium, against PDE5/9 manipulation (H8). Predicts that knockout raises intracellular cGMP and lowers luminal/extracellular cGMP, and barrier worsens; PDE manipulation changes both pools in the same direction.

H10. Statement: Barrier damage in this model is mediated by PKG2 (PRKG2) rather than by cGMP itself, so blocking PKG2 normalises the epithelium without lowering cGMP. · Evidence: PKG2 is the principal cGMP effector in intestinal epithelium and phosphorylates CFTR; PKG2-null mice are protected from STa-induced secretion (known). For barrier or differentiation in the GOF model none known. · Usual reading assumes that restoring cGMP is the way to restore the epithelium. This reading changes it to a downstream effector lever with cGMP left high. · Rejection: epithelium-specific Prkg2 deletion does not improve barrier in GOF mice. · Discriminating manipulation: epithelium-specific Prkg2 deletion, against Pde3 manipulation (H11). Predicts that cGMP is unchanged or slightly higher (loss of PKG feedback on PDE5) and barrier improves; H11 manipulation also leaves cGMP unchanged but acts through cAMP.

H11. Statement: The damage runs through cGMP inhibition of PDE3, which raises cAMP and PKA activity, so reducing cAMP signalling rescues the barrier with cGMP left high. · Evidence: cGMP-inhibited PDE3 and cGMP/cAMP cross-talk are well known in other tissues; in intestinal epithelium none known. · Usual reading assumes that cGMP effects are cGMP-dependent kinase effects. This reading changes it to cAMP cross-talk. · Rejection: cAMP in GOF epithelium is not raised relative to wild-type. · Discriminating manipulation: epithelium-specific PDE3 gain (or adenylyl cyclase reduction, class: Adcy dosage) against Prkg2 deletion (H10). Predicts that cGMP is unchanged, cAMP falls, barrier improves; a PKA-inhibitory manipulation would also help, which Prkg2 deletion would not predict.

H12. Statement: Hypersecretion of Cl- and fluid (CFTR with NKCC1 supply, reduced NHE3) imposes mechanical and ionic stress on the epithelium, so reducing ion or fluid flux relieves the barrier defect while cGMP stays high. · Evidence: cGMP drives CFTR opening and NHE3 inhibition (known); that fluid flux itself damages the barrier in this model is none known. · Usual reading assumes the defect is intrinsic signalling. This reading changes it to a secondary, flux-driven injury. · Rejection: CFTR or NKCC1 loss does not change barrier in GOF mice. · Discriminating manipulation: epithelium-specific Slc12a2 (NKCC1) or Cftr deletion, against Prkg2 deletion (H10). Predicts that cGMP is unchanged, luminal fluid falls, and barrier improves; the Prkg2 deletion would also remove non-ion effects, so a differentiation rescue would distinguish them.

H13. Statement: The secretory-lineage defect is a Notch/Atoh1 imbalance caused by cGMP/PKG signalling, so forcing secretory fate (Notch reduction or Atoh1 gain) restores goblet and Paneth cells and barrier even with cGMP high. · Evidence: Notch inhibition is known to drive secretory differentiation; a link from cGMP to this switch is none known. · Usual reading assumes that restoring cGMP restores differentiation. This reading changes it to a bypass of the fate switch. · Rejection: secretory cell number is restored by Notch blockade but the barrier is not. · Discriminating manipulation: epithelial Atoh1 gain or Notch pathway reduction (class: gamma-secretase inhibition at mechanism level), against Prkg2 deletion (H10). Predicts that cGMP is unchanged, secretory cells are restored, and barrier improves because mucus returns; PKG2 deletion would restore cells through the pathway itself, so Atoh1 would be bypassing it.

H14. Statement: The barrier phenotype is partly a consequence of the inflammatory cytokine loop (TNF, IL-1beta, IL-22) that the high cGMP triggers, so cytokine neutralisation breaks the loop and restores barrier, with cGMP unchanged initially. · Evidence: cytokines such as TNF are known to downregulate guanylin and uroguanylin and to open tight junctions; whether they drive barrier loss in the GOF mouse is none known. · Usual reading assumes the cytokines are a downstream marker only. This reading changes it to a causal amplifier. · Rejection: epithelium-intrinsic GOF in a cytokine-null background (Tnf or Il1r1 knockout) shows the same barrier leak. · Discriminating manipulation: Tnf or Tnfr1 deletion in the GOF background, against inducible removal of the GOF allele in the adult epithelium (H15's timing arm). Predicts that cGMP is unchanged, barrier is partly rescued; the inducible removal lowers cGMP and rescues barrier only if the damage is reversible.

H15. Statement: The pathology depends on gene dose and the timing or cell compartment of expression, so a single wild-type or null partner (heterodimer dilution or a catalytically dead partner) and adult-onset removal of the GOF allele will lower cGMP and reverse the phenotype in proportion. · Evidence: receptor GCs signal as dimers, so a dominant-negative partner is expected to dilute activity (INFERENZA). GOF-specific data none known. · Usual reading assumes a dominant GOF acts at full strength regardless of partner. This reading changes it to dose and partner dependence. · Rejection: GOF/null and GOF/wild-type mice show the same cGMP and barrier as GOF/GOF, or adult removal of the GOF allele does not reverse barrier leak. · Discriminating manipulation: GOF/catalytically-dead heterodimer, or tamoxifen-inducible adult deletion in Lgr5 stem cells against villin-driven differentiated cells, against Hsp90 or gene-dosage reduction (H5). Predicts that cGMP falls roughly with the fraction of active catalytic subunits and barrier follows; stem-cell deletion reverses secretory differentiation, differentiated-cell deletion reverses only acute cGMP effects.
