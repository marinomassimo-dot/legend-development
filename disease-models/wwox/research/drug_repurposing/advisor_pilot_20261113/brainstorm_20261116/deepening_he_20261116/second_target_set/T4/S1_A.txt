Framing: the exemplar is GNAO1 (Gαo), with the usual caveats. Reported missense mutants (e.g. G203R, R209C/H, E246K) behave as loss-of-function, gain-of-function or dominant-negative depending on the assay, so the levers below split by mutant class. The gene-level knockout phenotype (tremor, seizures, hyperalgesia, motor deficit) is not the same as the missense phenotype. All statements come from memory, with no sources checked.

H1. Statement: Selectively removing the mutant transcript (allele-specific knockdown or editing) restores function because the mutant protein acts dominantly (gain-of-function or dominant-negative) over a still-sufficient wild-type allele. · Evidence: heterozygous Gnao1 knockout mice are milder than missense knock-ins, and some mutants look gain-of-function in cAMP and BRET readouts. Allele-specific silencing is a general strategy, with no GNAO1-specific proof known to me. · Usual reading vs this one: the usual reading is haploinsufficiency, to be treated by adding gene. This reading says the wild-type allele is adequate and the mutant is the problem. · Rejection: in cells or mice expressing mutant and wild type, mutant-specific knockdown leaves the signalling readout (GIRK current or cAMP inhibition) unchanged, or worsens it. · Discriminating manipulation vs H2 (wild-type augmentation): remove the mutant allele without adding wild type. Predicts motor improvement and fewer seizures if the mutant is dominant. If the mutant is pure loss-of-function, it predicts no change or a worse motor and seizure phenotype, which is the knockout-like state.

H2. Statement: Raising total wild-type Gαo (gene addition or transcriptional upregulation) restores function when the mutant is mostly hypomorphic (haploinsufficiency-like). · Evidence: Gnao1 heterozygous knockouts show seizure susceptibility and motor deficits, which supports a dose effect. Gene addition is untested for the missense forms. · Usual reading vs this one: this reading treats the mutant as inert rather than interfering. · Rejection: wild-type overexpression in the mutant background does not restore the readout in cells that carry the mutant (a dominant-negative signature). · Discriminating manipulation vs H1: add wild-type Gαo and leave the mutant in place. Predicts a motor and seizure benefit only for loss-of-function mutants, with no benefit and possible worsening for gain-of-function or dominant-negative mutants.

H3. Statement: A pharmacological chaperone, or the cell's folding and proteostasis capacity, rescues mutants that are unstable or misfolded and degraded, raising functional mutant protein. · Evidence: some mutants show reduced steady-state protein or altered localisation in cell assays; this is partial and mutant-dependent. · Usual reading vs this one: the usual reading treats the mutant as a stable but wrong protein. · Rejection: mutant protein level and nucleotide-binding competence are normal in neurons, so stabilising it adds nothing. · Discriminating manipulation vs H4: a small-molecule folding-stabiliser class acting directly on Gαo, with no change in chaperone proteins. Predicts partial motor rescue and seizure reduction only for low-expression mutants.

H4. Statement: Increasing the guanine-nucleotide-exchange chaperone Ric-8 (Ric-8A or Ric-8B) restores folding and Gβγ assembly of the mutant. · Evidence: Ric-8 is the established chaperone for Gα biogenesis in neurons; no mutant-rescue data known. · Usual reading vs this one: it assumes the lesion is catalytic rather than a biogenesis defect. · Rejection: Ric-8 overexpression or loss does not alter mutant Gαo level or membrane abundance. · Discriminating manipulation vs H3: Ric-8 gain in neurons, which acts through the cell's own biogenesis route instead of through a ligand. Predicts the same direction as H3, but with an effect that depends on Ric-8 expression, and a mild effect on seizures.

H5. Statement: Restoring the lipid-modification and membrane-targeting cycle (N-myristoylation and palmitate turnover, with depalmitoylase inhibition) relocalises mutant Gαo to the membrane and restores coupling. · Evidence: Gαo is lipid-anchored, and some mutants show mislocalisation. No rescue data known. · Usual reading vs this one: the usual reading blames the nucleotide cycle alone. · Rejection: mutant Gαo is already membrane-localised at a normal fraction in neurons. · Discriminating manipulation vs H3: a depalmitoylase-inhibitor class, which acts on localisation rather than folding. Predicts modest motor improvement and a smaller effect on seizures.

H6. Statement: For mutants with slow GTP hydrolysis (a locked active state), boosting RGS-mediated GAP activity (RGS4, or the R7 family with Gβ5) restores timely deactivation. · Evidence: some G203/R209-type mutants alter GTP handling; RGS responsiveness of these mutants is unknown to me. · Usual reading vs this one: the usual reading assumes receptor input is the limiting step. · Rejection: the mutant's hydrolysis rate and RGS-stimulated rate are normal. · Discriminating manipulation vs H7: RGS overexpression, with no change in Gβγ. Predicts less seizure and less tonic hyperkinesia if the mutant is over-active, and worse behaviour if the mutant is under-active.

H7. Statement: The mutant traps Gβγ (or fails to release it), so Gβγ-dependent signalling falls, and raising free Gβγ (via GNB1/GNG2 availability) compensates. · Evidence: Gαo carries most brain Gβγ pools, and GNB1 variants give a similar phenotype, which suggests a shared Gβγ route. · Usual reading vs this one: the usual reading looks at Gα effector output only. · Rejection: Gβγ-gated GIRK current is normal in mutant neurons. · Discriminating manipulation vs H6: Gβγ gain or Gβγ-release enhancement. Predicts a seizure benefit with less motor benefit.

H8. Statement: The mutant sequesters GPCRs in a non-productive complex (a receptor-level dominant-negative), and raising receptor density or using biased agonists compensates. · Evidence: none known. · Usual reading vs this one: it moves the defect from the G protein to the receptor interface. · Rejection: receptor-stimulated nucleotide exchange in the mutant is normal. · Discriminating manipulation vs H7: receptor-biased agonism on Gαo-coupled receptors (α2A, D2, M4, GABA-B, μ-opioid). Predicts a partial motor change and modest seizure effect, mirroring partial loss of agonist response.

H9. Statement: The motor phenotype comes from striatal medium spiny neurons, especially the indirect (D2) pathway, and restoring Gαo there corrects movement. · Evidence: Gαo is abundant in striatum, and the dyskinesia resembles ADCY5 and GNAL disease. Cell-type-specific proof is unknown to me. · Usual reading vs this one: the usual reading treats the motor and seizure phenotypes as one global defect. · Rejection: cell-type-restricted rescue in D2 neurons does not change the motor score. · Discriminating manipulation vs the D1 pathway (nearest rival): D2-MSN-restricted Gαo restoration. Predicts motor improvement with little change in seizures.

H10. Statement: The seizures come from GABAergic interneurons (PV or SST), and restoring Gαo there controls seizures but leaves the motor phenotype. · Evidence: interneuron roles in other DEEs; Gαo-specific data unknown. · Usual reading vs this one: it assumes cortical excitatory neurons drive seizures. · Rejection: interneuron-restricted rescue does not change seizure threshold. · Discriminating manipulation vs excitatory-neuron rescue: Dlx-driven interneuron restoration. Predicts fewer seizures with unchanged motor function.

H11. Statement: Loss of Gαo inhibition of adenylyl cyclase 5 and PKA is the effector bottleneck, so inhibiting AC5 or PKA bypasses the mutant. · Evidence: ADCY5 gain-of-function causes a similar dyskinesia, and Gαo is an AC inhibitor. · Usual reading vs this one: the usual reading looks for ion-channel effectors. · Rejection: cAMP is normal in the mutant striatum. · Discriminating manipulation vs H12: an AC5-inhibitor class. Predicts motor improvement and a variable effect on seizures.

H12. Statement: Opening GIRK channels (a Gβγ effector) bypasses the mutant by restoring inhibitory tone. · Evidence: Gαo-βγ gates GIRK, and GIRK modulators are known in epilepsy models. · Usual reading vs this one: it targets the effector rather than the G protein. · Rejection: GIRK current in mutant neurons is already normal. · Discriminating manipulation vs H11: a GIRK-opener class. Predicts a seizure benefit and mild motor change, with risk of sedation.

H13. Statement: The mutant fails to inhibit presynaptic Cav2.2 (N-type) calcium channels through Gβγ, so release is excessive, and blocking Cav2 or α2δ restores balance. · Evidence: Gαo-dependent modulation of Cav2.2 is classic; no mutant-specific data known. · Usual reading vs this one: it assumes postsynaptic effects. · Rejection: presynaptic calcium and release probability are normal in mutant neurons. · Discriminating manipulation vs H12: a Cav2/α2δ blocker class. Predicts a seizure benefit and modest motor effect.

H14. Statement: Paralog compensation by Gαi/Gαz (GNAI1-3, GNAZ) can substitute for the dysfunctional Gαo. · Evidence: knockout mice retain partial Gi/Go-like signalling. · Usual reading vs this one: the usual reading assumes Gαo is irreplaceable. · Rejection: expressing a paralog in Go-lineage neurons does not restore any readout. · Discriminating manipulation vs H2: paralog expression in place of wild-type Gαo. Predicts partial motor and seizure benefit that is class-dependent.

H15. Statement: Striatal cholinergic interneurons lose Gαo-coupled M2/M4 autoreceptor braking, so acetylcholine is in excess, and restoring or substituting that braking corrects dystonia. · Evidence: M4 and D2 couple to Gαo/Gαi, and cholinergic excess is a known dystonia mechanism; GNAO1-specific data unknown. · Usual reading vs this one: it assumes MSN dysfunction is the cause. · Rejection: ChAT-interneuron-restricted rescue changes nothing, or acetylcholine release is normal. · Discriminating manipulation vs H9: cholinergic-interneuron-restricted rescue, or an M4-selective positive modulator class. Predicts motor improvement and no seizure change.
