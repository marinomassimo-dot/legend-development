NOTE ON THE FOLDER: I searched disease-models/ for phage, restriction-modification, CRISPR-Cas, anti-phage, Ku, LigD, RecBCD, microhomology and related terms. The only hits for "phage" are the substring in "esophageal" and "macrophage". The folder holds no statement about phage biology, so none of the ten hypotheses is supported or contradicted by held phage data. What it does hold is eukaryotic DSB-repair material, used below only as methodological analogy:
- (a) DL-MECH-024 in disease-models/wwox/research/discovery_ledger_current.md. It says WWOX loss raises HDR/SSA and RPA32/RAD51 and lowers NHEJ/alt-NHEJ. The general lesson is that which repair route is used is set by end-resection and by the proteins that control it.
- (b) disease-models/wwox/research/fulltext_dossiers/PMID38499540.md. Its section 3.1 says the pathway-outcome reporter sat at the floor for HDR (0.03–0.83% of events). Its section 2 says foci counts are surrogates, and section 6 says HDR is cell-cycle gated (needs S/G2).
- (c) DL-METH-084 in the same ledger. Two studies of the same pathway reported opposite directions, which shows that a surrogate readout cannot settle a repair-route question.
The consequence for every hypothesis below is that the discriminating readout is sequencing of the repaired cleavage site plus a survival count. Foci or reporter surrogates are not enough. All ten hypotheses are IPOTESI from general knowledge, marked as such. The one common premise is that the phage genome suffers a real DSB at a target site (a restriction site or a protospacer) at some point in infection.

H1. Statement: The phage tolerates nuclease cuts by direct, scarless re-ligation of the compatible ends by a phage-encoded or host DNA ligase, so cutting and sealing cycle futilely and a fraction of genomes escapes · Held evidence: none on phage; nothing in the folder contradicts it. Closest analogue is the NHEJ/alt-NHEJ readout in disease-models/wwox/research/fulltext_dossiers/PMID38499540.md, and that dossier shows why a surrogate is not an outcome · What the usual reading assumes and this one changes: the usual reading treats a DSB as lethal unless it is repaired by a mutagenic or templated route. Here the break is reversible by simple end-sealing, so the repaired site is the original, still-cleavable sequence and survival is only partial and stochastic · Rejection: sequence the cleavage site in surviving phage. If more than a trace fraction of survivors carry indels or altered sites rather than the wild-type sequence, and survival does not fall in a ligase-deficient infection, H1 is rejected · Discriminating manipulation (nearest rival H2, mutagenic NHEJ): delete or inactivate the phage ligase in a host whose own ligase cannot seal these ends (or use a ligase-dead allele). H1 predicts survival falls and the surviving repaired sites remain 100% wild-type sequence, with no indels. H2 predicts that survivors carry indels and that survival falls as well, so the deciding readout is the sequence class (wild-type versus indel) in the survivors that remain.

H2. Statement: The phage tolerates cuts through a Ku/LigD-like end-joining system that ligates ends after limited end processing, so survivors carry small indels at the cut · Held evidence: no phage data; the only analogue is that NHEJ and alt-NHEJ fall when resection control changes (DL-MECH-024, disease-models/wwox/research/discovery_ledger_current.md), which says route use is not fixed and is itself set by resection factors · What the usual reading assumes and this one changes: the usual reading assumes a phage needs one dedicated anti-nuclease protein per system. Here a single generic repair module covers all systems that make blunt or sticky DSBs, which is why no per-system inhibitor is needed · Rejection: delete the ku/ligD-type genes. If survival under attack is unchanged, and survivors' sites are unchanged or templated, H2 is rejected · Discriminating manipulation (nearest rival H3, alt-EJ using host factors): delete the phage ku (and ligD) alone, in a host that has no Ku/LigD of its own. H2 predicts survival falls and the survivors that remain are templated or wild-type with no short indels. H3 predicts survival and the indel/microhomology spectrum are unchanged, because the host supplies the end-joining.

H3. Statement: The phage tolerates cuts through host-driven microhomology-mediated end joining (host polymerase and ligase), giving deletions flanked by short microhomologies · Held evidence: no phage data; DL-MECH-024 lists "alt-NHEJ" as a distinct branch that moves with resection, so the analogy supports treating it as separate from classical NHEJ · What the usual reading assumes and this one changes: the usual reading assumes end-joining in bacteria requires an NHEJ module. This reading says it does not, and that repeats already present in the phage genome set where the deletions fall · Rejection: if survivors' junctions show no microhomology (blunt joins or insertions only) and no deletion centred on the cut, H3 is rejected · Discriminating manipulation (nearest rival H2): recode the 2–10 bp microhomologies flanking the cut site synonymously while leaving all repair genes in place. H3 predicts survival falls and the survivor spectrum shifts to other junctions, or collapses to only rare events. H2 predicts survival is unaffected and the indel spectrum is only mildly altered.

H4. Statement: The phage tolerates cuts by single-strand annealing mediated by its own RecT/Redβ-type annealase, which joins resected ends across flanking repeats and so deletes one repeat copy and the sequence between · Held evidence: no phage data; DL-MECH-024 reports that WWOX loss raises SSA together with HDR, so SSA is a distinct, resection-dependent route in the one system the folder describes · What the usual reading assumes and this one changes: the usual reading treats the phage recombinase as a genome-mixing or replication tool. Here it is the specific anti-DSB device, and the cost is a predictable loss of sequence · Rejection: if survivors that lost the site show deletions not bounded by direct repeats, or survival is unchanged after annealase removal, H4 is rejected · Discriminating manipulation (nearest rival H5, RecA-dependent homologous recombination with a co-infecting genome): delete the phage annealase and infect at one high multiplicity in a RecA-proficient host. H4 predicts survival falls (SSA is gone) while a single-copy cut still kills. H5 predicts survival is retained at high multiplicity because RecA uses a sister genome. The repaired site under H4 is a repeat-flanked deletion; under H5 it is a copy of the sister allele.

H5. Statement: The phage tolerates cuts by RecA-dependent recombination using an uncut co-infecting sister genome as template, so survival rises with multiplicity of infection and the repaired site copies the donor allele · Held evidence: no phage data; the nearest held concept is the cell-cycle gating of templated repair (HDR needs a sister template, PMID38499540 dossier, section 6), i.e. templated repair is gated by template availability · What the usual reading assumes and this one changes: the usual reading assumes that a cut genome is lost. Here repair is a population-level event, so tolerance is a property of how many genomes are in the cell, and the lethal event is a single cut genome in a cell with no donor · Rejection: if survival at low versus high multiplicity is the same, and survival is unchanged in a recA-null host, H5 is rejected · Discriminating manipulation (nearest rival H8, replication outrunning the nuclease): infect at multiplicity well below 1 and compare with a high-multiplicity infection, with a silent marker in the sister genome that lies 200 bp from the cut. H5 predicts survival at low multiplicity collapses and the marker is co-converted into the repaired site. H8 predicts survival at low multiplicity is unchanged and no marker conversion occurs. The caution from the held reporter dossier applies: measure the repaired sequence itself, not a surrogate.

H6. Statement: The phage genome carries Chi-like sites so that the host RecBCD, once it meets them, switches from degrading the broken end to loading RecA and recombining, so tolerance depends on Chi position relative to the cut · Held evidence: no phage data; nothing contradicts it. The only held parallel is DL-MECH-024, in which an end-resection-controlling factor decides between repair routes · What the usual reading assumes and this one changes: the usual reading treats RecBCD as a host defence that destroys phage DNA. Here the same enzyme is converted into a repair enzyme by phage sequence, with no phage protein at all · Rejection: if removing all Chi-like sites near the cut changes neither survival nor the repair product, H6 is rejected · Discriminating manipulation (nearest rival H5): ablate by synonymous recoding the Chi-like sites on the side of the cut from which RecBCD enters, leaving RecA and the genome copy number untouched. H6 predicts survival falls even at high multiplicity, and a repaired-site product copied from a sister drops sharply. H5 predicts survival at high multiplicity is unchanged by the ablation.

H7. Statement: The phage tolerates cuts by break-induced replication, where the broken end invades a homologous region and primes DNA synthesis (recombination-dependent replication), giving a long conversion tract and genome copy number gain · Held evidence: no phage data; none contradicts it · What the usual reading assumes and this one changes: the usual reading assumes that repair and replication are separate steps. Here repair is a form of replication, so the repaired region can be far longer than the cut and can even gain copies · Rejection: if survival stays high when the end-invasion-primed replication factors are removed, or the conversion tract is confined to under 50 bp, H7 is rejected · Discriminating manipulation (nearest rival H5, short-tract homologous recombination): inactivate the phage helicase loader or primosome factor that is needed only for recombination-primed replication, while leaving origin-driven replication intact. H7 predicts survival falls and the surviving repaired sites show long (kb-scale) conversion tracts only in the remaining survivors. H5 predicts survival and the short conversion tract (hundreds of bp) are unchanged.

H8. Statement: The phage tolerates cuts by amplification, since replication produces genome copies faster than the nuclease can cut them, so the population of intact copies exceeds the cut-site burden without any repair · Held evidence: no phage data; none contradicts it. The folder's own caution (PMID38499540 dossier, section 3.1) that a null at the floor is not a demonstration applies: a low-cut-rate observation does not distinguish repair from dilution · What the usual reading assumes and this one changes: the usual reading assumes a DSB must be repaired for the genome to survive. Here most cut copies are simply never repaired and are outnumbered, so the cleavage sites in survivors are unchanged · Rejection: if survivors show repaired or altered sites at the target in a substantial fraction, H8 is rejected (it predicts the survivors are uncut genomes with intact sites) · Discriminating manipulation (nearest rival H5): delay the onset of phage DNA replication with a conditional (temperature-sensitive or inducible) initiator, keeping the nuclease active. H8 predicts survival falls sharply with the delay, with no change in the repaired-site sequence class (wild-type, uncut). H5 predicts that survival is less sensitive to replication delay and that the repaired site is templated.

H9. Statement: The phage tolerates cuts because its genome is a concatemer packaged by a headful mechanism, so a cut simply creates a new packaging start and pieces are packaged as permuted, terminally redundant genomes · Held evidence: no phage data; none contradicts it · What the usual reading assumes and this one changes: the usual reading assumes a cut inside the genome destroys it. In a concatemer a cut inside one unit leaves the neighbouring units intact, so no repair is required and the junction left at the cut site is a terminus, not a repaired scar · Rejection: if survivors are present in a phage whose genome is a unit-length cos-type or fixed-end molecule, or the packaged survivors have fixed rather than permuted ends, H9 is rejected · Discriminating manipulation (nearest rival H8): replace the headful packaging signal and terminase with a fixed-end (cos-type) system and keep replication unchanged. H9 predicts survival falls and the permuted-end packaging at the cut vanishes. H8 predicts survival is unchanged. The cleavage site itself is not repaired in either; the sequence readout is the end position of packaged genomes.

H10. Statement: The phage tolerates multiple nuclease systems by physically excluding them, via a protein shell or nucleus-like compartment around its replicating DNA, so most genomes never receive a DSB and the few that do are repaired by routes H1–H7 · Held evidence: no phage data; nothing in the folder contradicts it · What the usual reading assumes and this one changes: the usual reading assumes tolerance is a response to a break. Here the break rate is lowered by a barrier that works against any DNA-targeting nuclease regardless of mechanism, which explains resistance to several unrelated systems at once. Boundary note: this is partly a rival to DSB tolerance and is retained as the exclusion-of-cutting alternative · Rejection: if cleavage products (cut genome ends) are found inside the compartment at the same density as in a compartment-defective phage, H10 is rejected · Discriminating manipulation (nearest rival H8): delete or inactivate the shell protein gene while keeping replication and repair genes intact. H10 predicts survival falls to the level of the most sensitive nuclease system and the cut-site fraction in the genome rises, with survivors showing the same repaired-site classes as the intact phage, only fewer. H8 predicts that the shell deletion does not change the cut fraction, only the survival.
