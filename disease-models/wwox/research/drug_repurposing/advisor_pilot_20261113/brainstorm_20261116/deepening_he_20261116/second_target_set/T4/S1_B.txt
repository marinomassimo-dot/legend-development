**H1.** Statement: Silencing only the mutant allele lowers the dysfunctional protein burden and restores signalling, whereas silencing both alleles does not. · Evidence: Allele-selective ASO/siRNA is established for other dominant disorders. Gnao1 knockout and heterozygous mice show seizures, tremor and hyperactivity, so total knockdown is expected to be harmful. No GNAO1 allele-specific data that I know of. · Usual reading assumes the defect is simply a lack of function that needs more Gαo. This one changes that: the mutant protein is an active poison, and removing it matters more than adding protein. · Reject if: allele-selective knockdown, with wild-type transcript and protein unchanged, fails to improve rotarod or gait in a heterozygous knock-in. A second rejection would be that it lowers the seizure threshold as much as total knockdown does. · Discriminator: allele-specific versus non-selective knockdown (rival H2, wild-type augmentation). Prediction: motor improves and seizures improve or stay unchanged; non-selective knockdown worsens seizures with little motor gain.

**H2.** Statement: Raising wild-type GNAO1 dosage dilutes or outcompetes the mutant and restores total functional Gαo. · Evidence: Haploinsufficiency phenotypes in null mice support a dose effect. Dominant-negative behaviour of some mutants (sequestering Gβγ, receptors or Ric-8B) argues that dilution alone may be partial. · Usual reading assumes a mutation is rescued by removing it. This one changes that: the wild-type supply is the limiting step, so more of it works even with the mutant present. · Reject if: wild-type overexpression in a heterozygous knock-in fails to improve the phenotype, or worsens it. Overexpression toxicity would also count against it. · Discriminator: AAV wild-type GNAO1 versus allele-specific knockdown (H1). Prediction: motor improves modestly, and seizures improve in loss-of-function models. Little effect in gain-of-function models.

**H3.** Statement: Raising GTPase-accelerating activity (RGS4, RGS7/9 with Gβ5) shortens GTP-bound lifetime and normalises mutants that hydrolyse GTP slowly. · Evidence: Some variants (the 203–209 region, gain-of-function-like) have impaired GTP hydrolysis in vitro, as I recall. RGS-insensitive Gαi G184S mutants give exaggerated signalling. Not tested in GNAO1 disease models to my knowledge. · Usual reading assumes the problem sits at the receptor or at the effector. This one changes that: the timing of the GTP-bound state is the lesion. · Reject if: RGS overexpression leaves the cAMP-inhibition and GIRK readouts of a slow-hydrolysis mutant unchanged. · Discriminator: striatal-neuron RGS9-2 or RGS7 overexpression versus Gαi compensation (H7). Prediction: reduced hyperkinesia and chorea; seizures unchanged unless forebrain RGS is also raised.

**H4.** Statement: Enhancing Ric-8B-mediated folding and stabilisation rescues unstable but intrinsically active mutants. · Evidence: Ric-8B is a chaperone and GEF for Gαolf and Gαs-type subunits. Several GNAO1 variants show reduced expression, which fits a folding defect. Direct evidence for Ric-8B in GNAO1 disease: none known. · Usual reading assumes the mutants are catalytically dead. This one changes that: a part of the mutant is functional but lost to misfolding. · Reject if: a mutant with normal expression and impaired activity is not improved by Ric-8B, or an unstable mutant is not rescued by any stabilisation. · Discriminator: Ric-8B overexpression versus proteasome or autophagy modulation (H5). Prediction: steady-state Gαo rises and motor improves for unstable variants; seizures improve in loss-of-function-type variants only.

**H5.** Statement: Enhanced clearance of misfolded mutant Gαo, through proteasomal or autophagic routes, reduces its dominant-negative sequestration of partners. · Evidence: Misfolded G-protein mutants form aggregates and are degraded in other contexts. In GNAO1 models, none known. · Usual reading assumes that more stability is better. This one changes that: for toxic mutants, faster degradation is the benefit. · Reject if: mutant half-life falls but the Gβγ-dependent readout (GIRK current) does not recover. · Discriminator: a proteostasis or degrader-class manipulation versus stabilising chaperone Ric-8B (H4). Prediction: motor improves; seizures improve only where a dominant-negative mutant is present.

**H6.** Statement: Mutant Gαo traps free Gβγ or heterotrimer, and relieving that by adding Gβ1/Gγ2 or by displacing the mutant restores effector signalling. · Evidence: Some mutants, such as the G203R-type, appear to behave as dominant negatives in heterologous assays. GNB1-related encephalopathy shows overlapping features. Not tested as a rescue. · Usual reading assumes the lesion is confined to Gαo itself. This one changes that: the pathology is a heterotrimer-level partner shortage. · Reject if: Gβγ overexpression does not restore GIRK or Cav2.2 modulation in cells expressing the mutant. · Discriminator: neuronal Gβ1γ2 overexpression versus wild-type Gαo augmentation (H2). Prediction: seizures improve (effector-driven); motor effect is smaller.

**H7.** Statement: Raising Gαi1/2 or Gαi3 expression compensates for lost Gαo coupling to Gi/o-coupled receptors. · Evidence: Gαi and Gαo share many receptors and effectors. Gαo is nonetheless the dominant brain isoform and not simply redundant. Not tested here. · Usual reading assumes Gαo has unique, irreplaceable roles. This one changes that: the receptor-to-effector link is rescuable by a sister subunit. · Reject if: Gαi overexpression leaves Gαo-specific readouts (for example specific GABAB or D2 responses) unrescued. · Discriminator: GNAI overexpression in D2-MSNs versus RGS manipulation (H3). Prediction: modest motor benefit; seizures mostly unchanged.

**H8.** Statement: Directly activating GIRK channels bypasses the faulty Gβγ and Gαo signal and restores hyperpolarisation. · Evidence: GIRK activation is a known Gβγ effector route. Gαo deficiency lowers inhibitory GPCR-GIRK coupling. GIRK-activator effects in GNAO1 models: none known. · Usual reading assumes the G protein must be repaired. This one changes that: the effector can be driven downstream. · Reject if: the GIRK current in mutant neurons is already normal, or a GIRK activator fails to raise the seizure threshold. · Discriminator: GIRK-activator class versus Cav2.2 block (H9). Prediction: seizures decrease; motor effect is modest, and sedation or bradycardia are possible.

**H9.** Statement: Restoring Gαo-Gβγ inhibition of presynaptic Cav2.2 (N-type) reduces excess release. This could be done by blocking Cav2.2 or reducing its surface expression. · Evidence: Gαo-Gβγ inhibition of N-type channels is a classic pathway. GNAO1 variants alter it in heterologous systems, as I recall. A specific rescue: none known. · Usual reading assumes the pathway runs through cAMP. This one changes that: a direct membrane-delimited channel route matters. · Reject if: Cav2.2 block or deletion leaves release probability and the phenotype unchanged in the mutant. · Discriminator: Cav2.2 conditional reduction versus a GIRK activator (H8). Prediction: seizures improve if excitatory terminals are the driver; the motor effect depends on the striatal or cerebellar release site.

**H10.** Statement: Correcting the cAMP/PKA set-point with phosphodiesterase or adenylyl-cyclase-axis modulators offsets over- or under-inhibition of cAMP in striatal neurons. The direction depends on the allele (gain-of-function-like or loss-of-function-like). · Evidence: Caffeine has been reported to help in some variants in worm models and in patient reports, mechanism unclear. Variants differ in direction of cAMP effect. · Usual reading assumes one mechanism fits all GNAO1 alleles. This one changes that: the direction depends on the allele. · Reject if: PDE or AC modulation fails to shift striatal cAMP in the expected direction in a variant-matched knock-in. A rescue that is the same for gain- and loss-of-function alleles would also reject it. · Discriminator: PDE10A or PDE4 inhibitor class versus adenosine A2A antagonist class. Prediction: motor changes with the allele; seizures are unchanged or worse with cAMP elevation.

**H11.** Statement: The movement phenotype is driven by indirect-pathway (D2) MSN Gαo dysfunction, and restoring Gαo only there is sufficient. · Evidence: Gαo is highly expressed in striatal MSNs. D2 signalling is Gi/o-dependent. Cell-type-selective rescue: none known. · Usual reading assumes widespread brain dysfunction. This one changes that: the motor phenotype is localised. · Reject if: D2-MSN-selective rescue fails to improve motor scores, or D1-MSN-selective rescue works equally. · Discriminator: D2-MSN versus cholinergic-interneuron rescue (H12). Prediction: motor improves; seizures unchanged.

**H12.** Statement: Cholinergic interneuron or M4-receptor signalling, which is Gαo-dependent, is the key node, and modulating M4 restores striatal balance. · Evidence: M4 couples to Gi/o and is relevant to D1-MSN control. This is a theoretical inference. · Usual reading assumes dopamine is the main modulator. This one changes that: acetylcholine is. · Reject if: an M4 positive modulator leaves motor behaviour unchanged, or its effect is the same in Gαo-null cells. · Discriminator: ChAT-selective rescue versus D2-MSN rescue (H11). Prediction: motor changes; seizures are unaffected.

**H13.** Statement: The seizures come from GABAergic interneuron Gαo dysfunction, and rescue there is sufficient for seizures but not for motor function. · Evidence: Gαo regulates GABAergic transmission, as I recall. Interneuron dysfunction is common in DEE. Not tested here. · Usual reading assumes the same cells cause both seizures and movement disorder. This one changes that: the two phenotypes have separate cellular origins. · Reject if: interneuron-selective rescue fails to raise the seizure threshold. · Discriminator: PV/SST-selective rescue versus pan-neuronal rescue. Prediction: seizures decrease; motor unchanged.

**H14.** Statement: A critical developmental window exists, so early restoration rescues both phenotypes, and later restoration rescues only motor function. · Evidence: GNAO1 loss alters neurite outgrowth and synapse formation in some systems. DEE onset is early. Not tested. · Usual reading assumes the disease is an acute signalling defect that can be repaired at any age. This one changes that: part of it is a lasting developmental effect. · Reject if: adult-onset induction or rescue is as good as early rescue for both seizures and motor function. · Discriminator: tamoxifen-timed rescue at P0 versus adulthood. Prediction: early rescue improves both; late rescue improves motor function only.

**H15.** Statement: Mutant Gαo is mislocalised because of lipidation defects (myristoylation and palmitoylation), and restoring membrane targeting rescues function. · Evidence: Gαo needs both lipid modifications to localise to the membrane. A few variants sit near these sites. Not tested. · Usual reading assumes the defect is in nucleotide binding or catalysis. This one changes that: the issue is where the protein sits. · Reject if: forced membrane anchoring of the mutant, or modulating palmitoylation, does not change function. · Discriminator: a ZDHHC/APT modulator versus a membrane-anchored mutant construct. Prediction: motor improves; seizures improve only if the effect is neuronal-wide.
