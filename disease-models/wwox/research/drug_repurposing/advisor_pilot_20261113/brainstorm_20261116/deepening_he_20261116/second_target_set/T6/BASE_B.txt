H1. Statement: Cleaved genomes are repaired by RecA/UvsX-type homologous recombination, using an intact sister genome (or concatemer copy) from the same infected cell as the template, so survival rises with the number of co-replicating genome copies. · Evidence: T4 and lambda are strongly recombinogenic. Recombination is known to raise phage survival under partial restriction and after Cas cleavage. I do not know of a clean test of this in the multi-system setting. · Usual reading assumes: resistance to each system needs its own anti-defence protein, and a cut genome is a dead genome. This reading changes that to a general repair capacity with no specificity for the nuclease. · Rejection: at fixed MOI, survivors of nuclease attack show no co-conversion of a neutral marker near the cut site from a co-infecting marked genome. Absence of marker transfer rejects it. · Discriminating manipulation (nearest rival H2, single-strand annealing): delete the phage strand-exchange recombinase (uvsX-like) while keeping the annealing protein. Prediction: survival falls strongly, and the effect is greater at low MOI. Survivors that remain carry the intact, re-cleavable wild-type site, with gene-conversion tracts copying the sister's sequence.

H2. Statement: Cleaved genomes are repaired by single-strand annealing between direct repeats flanking the cut, through a Redbeta/RecT-type annealing protein after end resection, so the repair removes the intervening sequence. · Evidence: lambda Red and RecT-family annealing proteins are well characterised for joining ends via homology. Their role in tolerating nuclease cuts is not known to me. · Usual reading assumes: repair must restore the original sequence. This reading says repair is deletional and costs the site. · Rejection: survivors repaired at a site flanked by long direct repeats show no deletion products. Intact-site recovery only, rejects it. · Discriminating manipulation (rival H1): place the cleavage site between engineered direct repeats, or remove the existing ones. Prediction: with repeats, survival rises independently of MOI. Without repeats, survival falls to the H1 level. The repaired site is deleted along with one repeat copy and is no longer cleavable.

H3. Statement: Phage-encoded Ku and ligase (LigD-like) join the ends by non-homologous end joining, which tolerates the break at the price of small indels. · Evidence: mycobacteriophage Omega encodes Ku and LigD-like end-joining functions. Whether they contribute to defence evasion is unproven, and most phages lack them. · Usual reading assumes: bacteria and phages repair with homologous recombination or not at all. This reading says the repair is direct and template-free. · Rejection: a phage with Ku/ligase deleted shows the same survival and the same repaired-site spectrum. No change rejects it. · Discriminating manipulation (rival H1): delete the phage Ku gene in a phage that is otherwise recombination-proficient. Prediction: survival falls even at high MOI. Survivors under H3 carry short deletions or microhomology-flanked indels at the cut. H1 survivors would be wild type.

H4. Statement: A phage-encoded end-binding protein (Gam- or gp2-like) shields the broken ends from RecBCD and other exonucleases, so the ends stay intact long enough for other repair to act. · Evidence: lambda Gam inhibits RecBCD and T4 gp2 caps DNA ends. This is established for end protection but is not shown for tolerance of restriction or CRISPR cuts. · Usual reading assumes: end-protectors are specific anti-RecBCD tools. This reading says they are a general buffer for any double-strand break. · Rejection: deleting the protector gives the same survival in a recBCD-null host. No dependence on host exonuclease rejects it. · Discriminating manipulation (rival H1): delete the end-protector alone while leaving the recombinase intact. Prediction: survival falls in a recBCD+ host and recovers in a recBCD-null host. The repaired site sequence is unchanged in kind, with wild-type or converted sequence and fewer survivors. Under H1 the same deletion would not matter.

H5. Statement: A phage or host ligase directly re-seals cleaved ends, and where ends are cohesive this restores the identical sequence, creating a cut and re-seal cycle that lets a fraction of genomes get through. · Evidence: T4 ligase is known to seal restriction-generated sticky ends in vitro. Its in vivo tolerance role is untested to my knowledge. · Usual reading assumes: after the cut, the genome is irreversibly damaged. This reading says the cut is partly reversible. · Rejection: survival is the same for nucleases that leave blunt ends and those that leave cohesive ends, and the same without the phage ligase. · Discriminating manipulation (rival H3, non-homologous end joining): delete the phage ligase gene and test an enzyme-matched pair of cohesive-end and blunt-end sites. Prediction: survival falls with the deletion, and more for cohesive-end sites. The repaired site is the exact wild-type sequence, with no indels, and so stays re-cleavable. Under H3 the repaired sites would carry indels.

H6. Statement: Error-prone repair or replication through the break creates point or indel mutations at the cleavage site, so tolerance is the heritable escape of a mutant subpopulation. · Evidence: CRISPR target escape mutants are well documented, including protospacer and PAM mutations. Their origin in repair, as against pre-existing variants, is debated. · Usual reading assumes: escape mutants are pre-existing variants selected by the defence. This reading says the cut itself generates them. · Rejection: the escape frequency under nuclease attack does not exceed the pre-existing mutant frequency, and it does not change after a bottleneck that removes pre-existing variants. · Discriminating manipulation (rival H1): swap in an antimutator, high-fidelity polymerase allele, or a mutator allele. Prediction: survival tracks mutation rate, falling with the antimutator and rising with the mutator. Survivors carry site mutations and resist re-challenge. H1 survivors would be wild type and stay sensitive.

H7. Statement: The infected cell is flooded with genome copies faster than the nuclease can cut them, so survival is a titration or kinetic race that needs no repair. · Evidence: restriction is known to be saturable at high MOI and with fast replication. This is established for restriction in general, not for this phage. · Usual reading assumes: the resistant phage has an active mechanism. This reading says it only outpaces the enzyme. · Rejection: survival is unchanged when replication onset is delayed. · Discriminating manipulation (rival H1): delay early replication, for example with a temperature-sensitive or weakened replication initiator, with recombinase intact. Prediction: survival falls sharply. Survivors have a site sequence unchanged from wild type, because uncleaved genomes were never repaired, and no conversion markers are seen. Under H1 survival would fall only if recombination were also hit.

H8. Statement: The genome is physically sequestered from the nuclease, for example by a protein shell or nucleus-like compartment, so no double-strand breaks form (not tolerance, but an alternative to it). · Evidence: nucleus-forming jumbo phages such as phiKZ resist several DNA-targeting systems, including restriction and Cas9, because the shell excludes them. · Usual reading assumes: the phage is hit and repairs the damage. This reading says it is never hit. · Rejection: cleavage intermediates are readily detected in infected cells with a shell that is intact. · Discriminating manipulation (rival H7): delete or truncate the shell protein gene (for example chmA). Prediction: survival falls and cleaved genome fragments are seen. A truncated mutant should be rescued by high MOI, with the cleavage site sequence unchanged in survivors, since no repair is involved. H7 would not predict loss from the deletion.

H9. Statement: A broken end invades a homologous genome copy and primes replication (break-induced or recombination-dependent replication), so the break is bypassed by copying rather than patched. · Evidence: T4 uses recombination-dependent replication as a main mode, with UvsX, UvsY and gp59 loading the helicase. Its role in tolerating nuclease cuts is not known to me. · Usual reading assumes: repair is local patching. This reading says the whole downstream segment is copied from the template. · Rejection: markers that are distal to the cut on the template are not co-copied with it. · Discriminating manipulation (rival H1): inactivate the helicase loader (gp59-like) while leaving strand exchange intact. Prediction: survival falls, with local patching still possible. Survivors under H9 show long conversion tracts, with distal markers from the template copied along. H1 survivors would show short tracts.

H10. Statement: The phage repairs by homologous recombination with host chromosomal or prophage sequences that share homology with it, so the template comes from the host. · Evidence: prophage and phage recombination is widespread, and lambda-Red-type recombination uses host homologies. Use against nuclease cuts is only an expectation. · Usual reading assumes: the only template is a sister phage genome. This reading says that at low MOI the host supplies it. · Rejection: at low MOI, survival does not depend on host homology, and no host-derived sequence is found in repaired sites. · Discriminating manipulation (rival H1): move the cleavage site into a segment that has no homology to the host, and in a second construct add a marked homologous cassette to the host or prophage. Prediction: at MOI of about 1, survival falls for the non-homologous site and rises again with the cassette. The repaired site carries the cassette's marker. Under H1 the host cassette would not matter and the marker would never transfer.
