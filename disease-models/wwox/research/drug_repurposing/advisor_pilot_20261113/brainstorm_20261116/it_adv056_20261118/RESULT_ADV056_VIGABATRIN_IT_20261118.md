## Answer first
1. **Case-level data can bear only "reported".** Vigabatrin exposure is documented in 22 children in 10 reports (21 after the held de-duplication rule); at least 21 and at most 24 distinct children can be counted. **No child received vigabatrin alone.** A response is stated for 11 of the 22 (8 responses, 3 non-responses) and every response is qualified (partial, transient, or defined clinically while EEG still recorded attacks). No sustained, instrumented response exists. The four classes of the card (vigabatrin, hormonal, ketogenic diet, cannabidiol) overlap in the same children: 38 children (36 after possible identities) received at least one of them, so the class counts are not additive.
2. **The harm side is better characterised than the benefit side, and it transfers.** The label's visual-field warning is irreversible-loss language, with onset from weeks to years and no exposure known to be free of risk; in infants "vision loss may not be detected until it is severe". A vigabatrin-associated MRI change in infants (VABAM) is reported in about 22 to 45 percent of scanned infants across reports (different designs), mostly asymptomatic and reversible on stopping in the reports that rescanned. The WWOX-class MRI report is two children, abstract only: the abstract does not say whether they were symptomatic or whether the change resolved.
3. **The efficacy evidence is spasm-defined and does not transfer cleanly** to a class child with other seizure types: every randomised infantile-spasms outcome is spasm cessation within two to six weeks in a syndrome-defined population; the one placebo-controlled trial in another seizure type (Lennox-Gastaut, single centre, abstract) reports benefit for drop, focal, myoclonic and atypical-absence seizures and not for generalised tonic-clonic seizures.
4. **Vigabatrin has no measurement in any Wwox-null animal and none on inhibition-restoration in a WWOX system.** The mechanism fit is a hypothesis with two named dependencies that are not measured (section 3).
5. **Reasoned stop for the literature loop on this unit** (section 6): the missing items are not literature items.

## 2. The harm signal and the class warnings
* **The signal I recorded** (new symmetrical pallidal and thalamic signal change during vigabatrin in a WWOX-class report: 2 children, abstract only, a report selected for the finding): the authors call it VABAM, the same entity as the cohort paper's prespecified imaging criteria; no source read describes it as anything else. Not stated in either abstract: symptoms, resolution, reversibility, hormonal co-therapy at the time. In the same centre's 64-child vigabatrin cohort (mixed aetiology, not a WWOX rate), concomitant prednisolone was associated with VABAM in univariable analysis (odds ratio 4.07, 95% CI 1.22 to 17.3) and a higher peak dose too; the two WWOX children are probably inside that cohort and must not be added to its 18 of 64. The authors raise whether children with a GABAergic-pathway genetic epilepsy or delayed myelination are more susceptible; the baseline MRI of the two children showed white-matter volume loss and callosal atrophy.
* **WWOX context:** whether pallidal and thalamic involvement under vigabatrin in WWOX-DEE is drug effect, disease trajectory or both is unresolved; the repository's background shows brainstem and other signal in WWOX children with and without vigabatrin in the drug column, so a cross-sectional attribution is not possible, while the report's comparison is within-child ("new during treatment"). The neonatal parkinsonism audit's thalamic lesion is attributed by its authors to birth history and the audit says not to read it as a WWOX imaging feature.
* **VABAM, the class (abstract level):** 22% against 4% in 205 infants with a blinded read; 30.9% of 81 scanned; 22.1% of 172; 44.7% of 76 scanned; 28.1% of 64. Reversible on stopping in reports that rescanned (6 of 9 in the first); the label adds that in a few patients the lesion resolved despite continued use; mostly asymptomatic (a symptomatic form in 4 of 104 children with exposure data in one report, possibly linked to concomitant hormonal therapy). Risk factors as stated: higher peak dose (sensitive to model specification in one report), younger age at start, cryptogenic aetiology in one report, lower rate in tuberous sclerosis (10% against 32.6%); sources disagree on duration and aetiology. The specific pattern was not seen in children older than 6 years and in adults (label).
* **Visual-field toxicity (label, read in full for the boxed warning):** onset unpredictable, from weeks to months or years; loss not reversible once detected; risk rises with dose and cumulative exposure, no exposure known to be free of risk; assessment at baseline (no later than 4 weeks), at least every 3 months, and 3 to 6 months after stopping; perimetry for adults and cooperative paediatric patients; a more common or more severe effect in infants and children "cannot be excluded". **Estimates in children differ with the method:** ERG-defined damage in 21% of 146 treated children with no recovery after stopping; perimetry at school age after infant exposure 34% of 32, from 9% to 63% with duration; a population cohort could test only 28 of 88; a record review without perimetry found no plausibly linked case among 143 (upper bound 3.2%); a cohort of 284 found toxicity hard to identify because of comorbidities. No estimate is unbiased for a child who cannot be tested.
* **Is the retinal or optic phenotype a distinct risk or only a monitored one?** On the label's own wording it is a **labelled caution, not only a monitored risk**: the drug should not be used in patients with, or at high risk of, other irreversible vision loss unless the benefit clearly outweighs the risk, and the label calls the interaction with other damage "likely adverse"; a paediatric Goldmann series states "great caution where there is pre-existing visual pathway damage" (14 children able to perform fields, 4 with pre-existing damage); and the monitoring tests depend on cooperation, which a child with severe impairment may not give. **A study with pre-existing optic atrophy or visual impairment as the exposed group: not found in these queries (two routes; partial screen at title and abstract level).** Optic atrophy in some WWOX-class children is taken as a stated premise and was not searched or verified here. No source gives the size of the extra risk.

## 3. Mechanism fit (mine; IPOTESI throughout; the label and rodent sources are in section 4)
Vigabatrin irreversibly inhibits GABA-transaminase and so raises brain GABA (ambient and synaptic). Held WWOX-system observations on inhibition: spontaneous inhibitory currents smaller in amplitude and frequency in neuron-targeted knockout neocortex at P13 to P17, recorded with a chloride-loaded pipette and with no PV identity (Breton 2021; RP-019); a lower number of PV-immunopositive cells in the hippocampus of a global knockout at two weeks, a marker count, one laboratory, with GAD65/67 protein lower in homogenate (Aldaz 2019, n = 5 v 5 for the blot); the heightened PTZ sensitivity of the null pup (a GABA-A antagonist; Cheng 2020); a chloride gradient that is undetermined (map and NEED-GABA-CHLORIDE-GRADIENT; SCI42). **What the fit predicts:** if the inhibition deficit is presynaptic, a reduced GABA supply (fewer or immature PV cells, lower GAD), raising ambient GABA could offset it, and the PTZ pattern is compatible with a reduced GABAergic reserve (INFERENZA, compatible with many other readings). **What it does not predict:** (a) a benefit if the deficit is postsynaptic (receptor subunits; ADV-004 concerns the subtype dispute for benzodiazepines, not GABA elevation) or if the chloride gradient is depolarising at the age tested (extra GABA could then be less effective or paradoxical; no E_GABA measurement exists); (b) a spasm-specific effect (the clinical effect of vigabatrin in infantile spasms is not explained by this fit); (c) safety in a developing brain with white-matter loss. **Two unmeasured dependencies decide the fit:** whether GABA release or the GABA-A response is the limiting step in the burst preparation (RP-019: no PV-identified recording, no mIPSC), and the chloride gradient (NP3A4). The fit is one of several (NMDAR gain, intrinsic excitability, gap-junction) and none is excluded.

## 4. Young rodents, about P10 to P28 (abstract level; TRANSFER)
Model and age dependent, not uniform: NMDA-induced flexion seizures (the spasm model) suppressed at P12 while clonic-tonic seizures were not suppressed at P12 or P25; PTZ: mainly a change of latency at P18 and P25 and suppression of the tonic phase in immature groups; flurothyl: no anticonvulsant effect at the ages tested; hypoxia then PTZ at P25: pro-convulsant in non-hypoxic controls and less severe after hypoxia; spasm models (TTX, betamethasone-primed, a Gabrb3 knock-in at P13 to P15): spasms suppressed; a multiple-hit model: daily dosing acted for one day and was not well tolerated. Brain GABA rose transiently in young mice. **Harms in the same window:** reduced myelin staining, axonal degeneration and glial death after treatment from P12; MR white-matter signal and microvacuolation partly reversed two weeks after stopping while behaviour stayed abnormal; intramyelinic oedema and hypomyelination; cone and ganglion cell loss in neonatal rats treated from P4; behavioural and learning deficits in mice; apoptosis; juvenile dog neuropil vacuolation (transfer). Kainate, pilocarpine or kindling in young rodents with vigabatrin: not found in these queries (Europe PMC 5 off-topic hits, PubMed 0). **Relevance:** a PTZ arm with vigabatrin in the Cheng assay would mostly report a latency and tonic-phase effect, so a Racine-stage readout may not be sensitive to it; and the myelin and retinal findings in young animals fall on the Wwox-null phenotype's own weak points (hypomyelination, optic atrophy in some children).

## 5. Randomised evidence and what transfers (abstract level)
| Report | Design | Vigabatrin n | Primary outcome as the abstract defines it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1999 placebo-controlled | double-blind placebo 5 days, then open | 40 | spasm reduction against baseline at the end of the double-blind phase; assessor not stated |
| UKISS 2004 | randomised, masking not stated | 52 of 107 | no spasms on days 13 and 14: 54% against 73% on hormonal treatment |
| UKISS 2005 | same trial | 52 | Vineland score at 14 months; the subgroup without an identified aetiology differed |
| ICISS 2017 | open label, assessors masked | 186 of 377 (with hormonal therapy) | no witnessed spasms from day 14 to 42 (parent diary): 72% against 57% |
| ICISS 2018 | same trial | 181 of 362 | Vineland composite at 18 months: no difference |
| smaller randomised comparisons, TSC-only studies | various | 9 to 24 per arm | spasm-free at one month; remission days 14 to 42; one underpowered three-arm trial |
| EPISTOP 2021; PREVeNT 2024 | TSC, preventive against conventional or placebo | 94; 29 of 56 | time to first seizure (positive); Bayley-III cognitive at 24 months (negative) |
| Lennox-Gastaut 2026 | double-blind placebo, ages 2 to 18 | 50 per arm | at least 50% fewer drop attacks at 12 weeks (51.7% against 8.9%) |
The label also cites three double-blind placebo-controlled studies in focal seizures (ages 3 to 16), not read. **INFERENZA:** the harm side transfers best (exposure effects across aetiologies); the efficacy side transfers poorly (spasm-cessation outcomes within weeks in a syndrome-defined population; none is a seizure-burden outcome in mixed seizure types; the developmental outcome of the spasm trials was null at 14 and 18 months); the preventive trials ask a different question; hormonal co-therapy confounds the MRI estimate (association in two reports). The largest gap is exposed-group evidence in children with baseline visual impairment. Other GABA-transaminase inactivators surfaced: CPP-115 (a vigabatrin analogue: spasms reduced in the multiple-hit rat model, lethal at the top dose, smaller ERG effects than vigabatrin in rats, a healthy-adult study and one single-patient report), OV329 (adult rat and mouse data only); not candidates for the class on this evidence.
