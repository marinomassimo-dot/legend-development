# A. Decision evidence: does the Wwox-deletion slice finding transfer to ketamine?

AI-curated research brief for discussion with a clinical team. Hypothesis level. Reference genotype class only (WWOX-related developmental and epileptic encephalopathy); no individual-level information. This page contains no dose, no schedule and no recommendation for or against any use of any drug in any person. Prepared on 2026-11-23; every value in the tables was re-read on that date at the depth shown in its Status column (full text, figure or table; or PubMed abstract), except where a row says it is carried from an internal record (see the section Methods and coverage).

## What this means for a clinician (no recommendation)
1. The published finding is a mouse brain-slice result: when the Wwox gene is deleted in neurons, cortical slices burst, and d-APV, a drug that blocks the NMDA receptor at its agonist site, stopped the bursts in five slices from three animals.
2. Ketamine blocks the same receptor by a different mechanism and also acts on other targets. It has not been tested as an intervention in any WWOX model found, and no child with WWOX-related disease is reported as treated with it.
3. Ketamine studies in children with other conditions exist, but the settings differ and they cannot be pooled or carried over; reports of electrographic seizures after exposure in people with epilepsy are a caution, not a rate.
4. What would change the picture is one laboratory experiment (section The deciding measurement). Until then this brief supports discussion of a hypothesis and says nothing about whether any drug should be used in any person.

**The strongest single point against a simple transfer** is the competing explanation through the HCN1 channel (the Ih current): ketamine lowers Ih in neurons of an overlapping age window with the slices (14 to 22 days for the Ih data, postnatal day 13 to 17 for the slices), and the one source that discusses an Ih blocker in this setting calls its use "controversial" because it made seizure-like events worse in a cited report, so even a proven Ih route would have an open direction (row I3 here and row D1.5 of the negative-results table). A related unresolved disagreement concerns concentration: the authors of the HCN1 paper call ketamine concentrations of about 8 to 16 uM "clinically relevant", citing a free anaesthetic plasma level of about 5 to 10 uM (Chen 2009 [4], Results, Fig 1C paragraph; the same paper's Discussion says these concentrations are "somewhat higher than" those for NMDA-receptor inhibition), while the plasma values in the reports opened for this brief span a wide range: the adult volunteer median peak (112.87 ng/mL, 0.47 uM) is far below 8 to 16 uM, whereas the highest group-mean peak found (2104 ng/mL, 8.85 uM, anaesthetised children, high-dose nasal arm) lies inside the range of the authors' effective concentrations (see the quantitative table, Q9 to Q13). This brief does not settle it.

## Terms used
* **WOREE:** WWOX-related epileptic encephalopathy, the severe early-onset end of WWOX-related disease. **DEE:** developmental and epileptic encephalopathy.
* **S-KO, S-HT, S-WT, S-Control:** mice with neuron-specific Wwox deletion (S-KO), one copy deleted (S-HT), wild type (S-WT), and control littermates (S-Control).
* **sIPSC:** spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic current, a measure of inhibitory input to a recorded neuron. **Sag:** the slow voltage relaxation during a hyperpolarising current step, a readout of the Ih current.
* **d-APV:** a competitive NMDA-receptor antagonist (blocks the agonist site). **Pore blocker:** a drug, such as ketamine or memantine, that enters and blocks the open channel; **use-dependent** means it needs the channel open, and **trapping** means it can stay inside after the channel closes.
* **Ih / HCN1:** a hyperpolarisation-activated current carried by HCN channels. **PV:** parvalbumin-expressing inhibitory interneuron.
* **Cmax, Tmax, AUC:** peak concentration, time of the peak, and area under the concentration-time curve. **CSF:** cerebrospinal fluid.
* **Not currently scheduled:** a research item that no one has been asked to do now; it is not a negative result.

**How to read the tables.** Stage: **D** Data (read in a source), **I** Inference (reasoned from data), **H** Hypothesis (to be tested), **E** Experiment (a proposed measurement). Status: **VERIFIED** (reproduced from the original full text, figure or table, locator given), **ABSTRACT-ONLY** (reproduced from the PubMed abstract opened that day), **UNVERIFIED** (carried from team notes, not reproduced). Numbers in square brackets are entries of the reference list. ## The decision this page serves
Whether a research group should spend a slice experiment on a use-dependent NMDA-receptor pore blocker such as ketamine in the published Wwox-deletion preparation. The authors write that NMDAR-associated glutamatergic currents "initiate" the network bursts and that NMDA signaling "contributes" to the epileptic activity (Breton 2021 [1], Discussion 3.4); this brief reads the d-APV result as showing that NMDA-receptor activation contributes to the bursts. The result was obtained with d-APV, a competitive antagonist that blocks the receptor at a different site from ketamine. Ketamine was not tested as an intervention in any WWOX system found (the only exposure is incidental anaesthesia in vivo, W7).

## What the evidence supports, in plain words
* **Data:** neuronal Wwox deletion makes neocortical slices burst; d-APV stops the bursts in the five slices tested; inhibitory currents are smaller and sag is larger.
* **Inference, narrow:** NMDA-receptor activation contributes to the bursts in that preparation.
* **Hypothesis, untested:** a pore blocker would reproduce the effect. Four alternative readings (Ih, disinhibition, rebound, sedation or co-medication context) make a ketamine result in this slice hard to read; the experiment in the section The deciding measurement separates the first three (matched block depth, sag in the same cells, time after washout); the fourth is outside the reach of any slice.
* **Not established:** any benefit of ketamine in a WWOX system (no dedicated ketamine arm exists; the d-APV arm and the incidental anaesthesia are not that), in any person, acutely or chronically; developmental benefit; safety in infants of the class. The clinical data are from other settings and are not pooled.
