## (a) A data-collection frame for a registry or a clinical team (retrospective; nobody is exposed to anything for it)
**Design in one line.** The informative comparisons are *within the same person across episodes* and *across people with the same condition*, both ordered in time; a single episode cannot separate factors that all occurred in it.

**What the record needs, for every episode that is analysed.**
1. **A time-stamped event line at day resolution (hour for exposures):** admission, each sedative or anaesthetic agent with start and stop, each other drug started, stopped or changed, antibiotic start and stop, fever days, culture results, respiratory or feeding support, discharge. Amounts are whatever the clinical record holds; the frame does not ask anyone to extract or compare doses.
2. **A baseline long enough to know the person's own variation:** at least three pre-event measurements of the same outcome with the same instrument over at least three months (a caregiver diary with fixed anchors, seizure or event counts, a validated scale if one exists for the condition, sleep log, video rated by someone who does not know the exposure). Without it, "improved" cannot be told from the person's usual range.
3. **Outcome measures at fixed times after the event** (for example at 1, 2, 4, 8 and 12 weeks), by the same method as the baseline, with the rater's knowledge of the exposure recorded.
4. **The illness course:** temperature, inflammatory markers if taken, cultures, imaging, the day the clinical team judged the illness to have resolved.
5. **Antiseizure and other chronic medication changes** around the admission, including anything stopped or reduced (deprescribing).
6. **Other episodes of the same person** (earlier or later admissions, procedures, sedations) with the same fields, with and without the agent of interest and with and without an illness: this is where within-person separation comes from.
7. **A comparison group** with the same condition and a comparable admission, exposed and not exposed, for a self-controlled series (each person is their own control) and for the effect size.

| Rival (leading ones) | Its signature in such a record | What separates it from the others | What the record could NOT separate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resolution of an intercurrent illness | improvement begins after fever or markers turn and tracks them; returns to the documented pre-illness range | the same recovery after illnesses without the agent; whether the improvement exceeds the pre-illness baseline | a lasting agent effect that coincides with illness resolution in every episode |
| Sleep and seizure burden | improvement parallels the sleep log or the seizure and EEG record; antiseizure changes explain a drop | improvement that tracks sleep or burden within days rather than the exposure; later episodes with the same sleep gain | burden and sleep that are themselves moved by the agent (a mediated path) |
| Co-administered sedatives (GABAergic and others) | effect during and just after the infusion; fades in days unless metabolites accumulate | the same sedatives without the agent in other episodes; stop and start times | any sedative that was always co-given with the agent |
| Antibiotics | improvement starts days after the first dose and follows the infection | culture-proven infection; improvement before or after the first dose; courses in other episodes | antibiotics that are inseparable from illness resolution |
| Anticholinergic co-medication | onset within hours; fades in days; relevant only if the agent crosses into the brain (agents that do not cross have no brain effect by design: the class and agent must be recorded) | agent and class by name; episodes with and without it | a central anticholinergic given always with the agent |
| Reporting and regression to the mean | improvement follows an unusually low point; pre-event trend is downward; rater knows the exposure | blinded or anchored measures; comparison with the person's year of data | observer expectation in a record that was never blinded |
| An NMDAR-pore effect with a slow ratchet | onset within a day or two of the exposure, independent of the illness course; gradual decay over weeks; recurs after a natural re-exposure | people or episodes with the agent and no illness and no other change; a dose-timing relation across repeated exposures | a combination with any factor that always travels with it |
**What no record can do:** separate two components that co-occur in every episode; recover a baseline that was never measured; remove confounding by indication (people receiving the agent are often sicker); settle a mechanism. It can show that a pattern is or is not compatible with each rival, and it can say which missing field would change the answer.
**Harms travel with it:** the frame also collects the adverse events recorded in each episode (emergence reactions, secretion and airway events, cardiovascular and urinary events, behavioural change, seizure change), so that the registry can show benefits and harms in the same table. It is a data-collection frame, not a recommendation about using or avoiding any agent.

## (b) The model statement, in plain words (for IT's three-layer statement; layer: hypothesis from a model)
In the computer model of the network that bursts when Wwox is absent, blocking the main NMDA-type glutamate channel makes the bursts shorter and further apart, and the model returns to exactly where it started once the block is lifted: we tested ramps down and back up, and holds of up to half an hour, and found no memory of any kind. Everything in the model that is slow relaxes within about twenty seconds, so by design it cannot carry a benefit for weeks. For an effect to last weeks, something else would have to be added: a slow adjustment of the network's overall gain, a lasting step in how the network matures or wires, a second stable state, or a slow change in the brain's environment, such as inflammation. None of these is in our data, and the first one would tend to push excitability up after the drug, not down. We also expected the model to show a rebound after release, as the real tissue did in a laboratory slice (about 1.85 times the usual bursting; a value read from the published figure image, not a tabulated number), and it did not. That miss is ours, and it means the model is missing a slow ingredient that the slice has. All of this is about a model of one laboratory preparation; it says nothing about any person.

## (c) The next free step in the model, if any
* **Candidate:** add a labelled toy slow homeostatic gain w (time constant hours; scales the NMDAR and AMPA weights; driven by the deviation of average activity from a set point), preregistered, and test whether it reproduces three held features together: bursting abolished by a full block, a washout overshoot near the held 1.85 times baseline (image-read), and a return to baseline over hours to days. Predictions to freeze first: P 0.85 that an overshoot of the right sign appears for some time constant; P 0.35 that one setting reproduces overshoot size and baseline together without moving the fitted baseline by more than 10%; P 0.9 that the overshoot decays and no lasting benefit appears (the sign is wrong for one). Cost: about 20 minutes of my own compute and attention, no tokens beyond mine, no delegate.
* **Is it worth doing?** Modestly, and only for what it can show: it tests whether a homeostatic structure can explain the held overshoot, which is a gap in the model (S1). It cannot confirm that structure (the toy is tuned on one image-read number, the 1.85-fold overshoot), and it cannot show a benefit for any person; it would be labelled a toy in the file name and the first line. I would not do the ratchet (S2): it is unconstrained, any lasting effect can be written in by construction. My recommendation: do the homeostatic toy after the meeting if the operator asks for model work, and not before; it is not needed for the meeting's statement.
