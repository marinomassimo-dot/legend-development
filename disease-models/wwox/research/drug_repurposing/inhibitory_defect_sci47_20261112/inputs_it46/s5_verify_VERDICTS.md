## R1. Wwox-deficient cortical slices (PMID 34634460): inhibition deficit, and what is measured

### R1.1 Does the paper report a deficit of spontaneous or evoked synaptic inhibition?

**Verdict: SUPPORTED for spontaneous inhibition (sIPSC amplitude and frequency, recorded cells). NOT TESTED for evoked inhibition. Depth: FULL (text only).**

Model identity (so the premise is read correctly): the knockout is neuron-specific (Synapsin-Cre, "S-KO"), not global; "S-CTL" pools heterozygotes and wild types. Methods 5.1: "Both male and female mice aged P13 to P17 were humanely sacrificed". Results 2.1: "13 to 17 day old S-KO, S-HT, and S-WT littermate mouse neocortical and hippocampal slices".

Quotes (Abstract, as printed on PubMed): "In layer II/III pyramidal neurons, Wwox knockout mice demonstrated elevated amplitude of excitatory post-synaptic currents, whereas the frequency and amplitude of inhibitory post-synaptic currents were reduced, as compared to heterozygote and wild-type littermate controls."

Results 2.5 ("Unbalanced spontaneous excitatory and inhibitory currents in pyramidal neurons of the superficial neocortex"): "In contrast, the sIPSCs were reduced in both amplitude and frequency (G, H; mean amplitude: S-CTLs 57.3 ± 31.0pA; S-KOs 27.5 ± 19.4 pA; mean frequency S-CTL 10.3 ± 11.5 Hz; S-KOs 8.4 ± 10.0 Hz; for n's seelegend)." ("seelegend" is the text-extraction artefact for "see legend".) Worked from these printed means: amplitude 57.3 to 27.5 pA is a 52% fall; frequency 10.3 to 8.4 Hz is an 18% fall, and the printed SDs are as large as the means (11.5 and 10.0 Hz against means of 10.3 and 8.4), which looks like a pooled-event distribution. The n values and the statistical tests are in the figure legends and Supplement, which I did not receive, so the strength of the frequency effect is not checkable by me. The text then says: "These data indicate that the S-KO neocortex favors excitation over inhibition, primarily through an impairment in the amplitude of the inhibitory currents." The authors themselves put the weight on amplitude.

Conclusions (section 4, item 1): "decreased spontaneous inhibition".

Evoked inhibition: no evoked IPSC experiment is described anywhere in the text. The evoked recordings are field potentials (layer V stimulation, layer II/III LFP; Supplementary Fig. S3) and are NMDA-receptor mediated (Results 2.2). So "evoked synaptic inhibition" is not reported.

### R1.2 Measured in the recorded cells, or inferred?

**Verdict: the sIPSCs are measured directly in the recorded layer II/III putative pyramidal neurons; every statement about the cause (interneuron loss, presynaptic change, reduced interneuron action-potential amplitude) is inferred or untested. Depth: FULL (text only).**

Exactly what was measured:
- Recording configuration (Methods 5.3): "For recordings of sIPSCs, a high-chloride intracellular solution was used, in order to enhance the chemical gradient of chloride. This solution is composed of, in mM: 140 KCl, 1 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 10 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 3 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na2GTP". And: "Spontaneous EPSCs and IPSCs were isolated by voltage-clamping pyramidal neurons at -70 mV in K-Gluconate or KCl intracellular solution respectively."
- Reading of that: with 140 mM chloride in the pipette the recorded cell's chloride is clamped by the pipette, so the sIPSC amplitude is recorded under an artificial chloride gradient. The recording therefore cannot report the native GABA reversal potential, and the paper does not report one for P13-P17 (no gramicidin or cell-attached E_GABA measurement appears in the text). Whether GABA is hyperpolarising or depolarising in the S-KO cortex is therefore not measured.
- Isolation: the text delivered names no GABA-A antagonist (bicuculline, gabazine) and no glutamate-receptor blocker for the sIPSC recordings. Methods 5.4 lists only "TTX, ... d-APV, ... CBX, and ... BB-FCF" (1 uM, 50 uM, 100 uM, 10 uM). With a KCl pipette at -70 mV, EPSCs are also inward. The figure legends were not in the delivered text, so I cannot exclude that a legend states a blocker; as delivered, pharmacological confirmation that the events are GABA-A is not shown.
- No miniature IPSCs (the TTX recordings were for mEPSCs only, Results 2.3).
- Interneuron-related findings (Results 2.6): "the interneurons of S-KOs exhibited no significant difference in the number of action potentials generated as compared to controls"; "For the interneurons, only the action potential amplitude was diminished in the S-KOs compared to the controls, suggesting the impairment of sIPSCs could be related to reduced action-potential amplitude, but not to other specific active or passive interneuron membrane properties." Interneurons were patched and characterised for excitability; they were not counted, typed (no parvalbumin or somatostatin identification in the text), or recorded as presynaptic partners.
- The authors' own statement of what is not known (Discussion 3.3): "As the action potential properties of the interneurons themselves are unchanged in the WWOX S-KO, these findings may indicate a change in pre- or post-synaptic properties unrelated to action potential firing, or reflect a WWOX-dependent decrease in the interneuron population () for which we did not account." Also: "further studies are likely necessary to identify the specific reason why a Wwox knockout would reduce GABAergic currents."
- Internal inconsistency to note: Results 2.6 reports a diminished interneuron action-potential amplitude, while Discussion 3.3 says interneuron "action potential properties ... are unchanged". Discussion 3.6 attributes reduced action potentials "in both pyramidal neurons and interneurons" to hypomyelination, a further inference not measured here.
- A separate observation outside R1 but in the same paragraph (Results 2.5): the text says sEPSC amplitude shows "a leftward shift in their cumulative probability distribution" while describing larger amplitudes (a rightward shift would be larger); mean sEPSC amplitude 23.3 versus 24.7 pA is a 6% difference. I could not see the figure.

### R1.3 Any other WWOX or Wwox source (human or mouse) reporting a GABAergic or parvalbumin-interneuron deficit?

**Verdict: SUPPORTED for mouse (one source, hippocampus, counts, ABSTRACT). Human: none found (NOT_REACHED as a positive; absence claim limited to the routes below).**

- Mouse: Hussain et al. 2018, PMID 30290271, Neurobiol Dis 121:163-176 (ABSTRACT; PMC7104842 exists but I did not open it). Verbatim: "in this study, using a Wwox knockout (Wwox KO) mouse model (2 weeks old, both sexes) and stereological studies we observe that Wwox deletion leads to a significant reduction in the number of hippocampal GABA-ergic (γ-aminobutyric acid) interneurons. Wwox KO mice displayed significantly reduced numbers of calcium-binding protein parvalbumin (PV) and neuropeptide Y (NPY) expressing interneurons in different subfields of the hippocampus". Also: "decreased levels of Glutamic Acid Decarboxylase protein isoforms GAD65/67 expression in Wwox null hippocampi suggesting lower levels of GABA synthesis". Limits: global (ubiquitous) knockout, hippocampus not neocortex, cell counts and protein levels, not synaptic function. This is the study Breton et al. cite for "a loss of GABAergic interneurons in the hippocampus of a ubiquitous ... KO model" (Discussion 3.3).
- Not a GABAergic source (abstract level): Repudi et al. 2021, PMID 33914858 (neuron-specific deletion): abstract reports hyperexcitability, "reduced maturation of oligodendrocytes, reduced myelinated axons and impaired axonal conductivity"; no interneuron or GABA statement in the abstract (full text not reached: no PMCID).
- Not a deficit report: Aldaz and Hussain 2020, PMID 33255508 (review, ABSTRACT): Wwox expression is highest in "GABAergic basket cells and granule cells from cerebellar cortex" in single-cell RNA-seq; this is expression, not a deficit. Choi 2025, PMID 41442931 (case report, ABSTRACT): "Further research is warranted to investigate whether children with genetic epilepsy related to the GABAergic pathway or delayed myelination are more susceptible to VABAM"; this is a speculation about vigabatrin imaging toxicity and does not report a GABAergic deficit in WWOX children. Hsu 2021, PMID 34359949 (review, ABSTRACT) concerns interneuron reduction in Alzheimer disease patients, not WWOX-DEE.
- Human WWOX GABAergic or PV deficit: none found in two PubMed queries (WWOX with GABAergic, parvalbumin, interneuron, epilepsy, knockout, seizure; Wwox in title/abstract with interneurons, GABA, parvalbumin; 3 and 5 hits respectively, all listed above). A negative from abstract-level PubMed queries is not a proof of absence (no postmortem or iPSC literature was searched beyond these queries).

## R2. Benzodiazepine-site positive modulation amplifies existing GABA; and behaviour when GABA is depolarising

### R2(a). Mechanism: amplifies GABA already present, does not substitute

**Verdict: SUPPORTED, with one stated exception. Depth: ABSTRACT.**

- Campo-Soria, Chang, Weiss 2006, PMID 16783415 (ABSTRACT). Verbatim: "Dose-response relationships for GABA were compared in the absence and presence of 1 microM diazepam (DZP) ... The dose-current relationships yielded EC(50)'s ... of 41.0+/-3.0, 21.7+/-2.7, and 118.3+/-6.8 microM for GABA, GABA plus DZP, and GABA plus DMCM". And: "Our results support a mechanism in which DZP increases the apparent affinity of the receptor, not by altering the affinity of the closed state, but rather by shifting the equilibrium towards the high-affinity open state." This is a left-shift of the GABA concentration-response curve (EC50 41.0 to 21.7 microM): amplification of the response to GABA.
- Exception, in the same abstract: "Oocytes expressing ultrahigh levels of wild-type GABA(A) receptors exhibited currents in response to 1 microM DZP alone". So at ultrahigh receptor expression in Xenopus oocytes, diazepam alone can gate the channel (EC50 for DZP-mediated activation 72.0 nM). "Does not substitute" holds for ordinary expression levels; it is not absolute.
- Supporting context (ABSTRACT only, mechanism not stated in detail): Sigel and Steinmann 2012, PMID 23038269: "GABA(A) receptors located in the postsynaptic membrane mediate neuronal inhibition that occurs in the millisecond time range; those located in the extrasynaptic membrane respond to ambient GABA and confer long-term inhibition. GABA(A) receptors are responsive to a wide variety of drugs, e.g. benzodiazepines". Rudolph and Knoflach 2011, PMID 21799515: benzodiazepines "non-selectively target GABA(A) receptors containing the α1, α2, α3 or α5 subunits". Neither abstract states the amplification mechanism; Campo-Soria is the load-bearing source.
- Not read: Study and Barker 1981 and the classic single-channel literature (not retrieved).

### R2(b). When GABA is depolarising or excitatory, benzodiazepine-type modulation may be less effective or may worsen excitation

**Verdict: SUPPORTED as a hedged ("may") statement in animal and in vitro work; PARTLY in the wider picture because counter-evidence exists and the human NKCC1-block trials do not confirm it. Depth: ABSTRACT for every source.**

For (less effective):
- Dzhala et al. 2005, PMID 16227993: "In line with this caudal-rostral developmental pattern, GABAergic anticonvulsant compounds inhibit motor manifestations of neonatal seizures but not cortical seizure activity." and "The NKCC1 blocker bumetanide shifted E(Cl) negative in immature neurons, suppressed epileptiform activity in hippocampal slices in vitro and attenuated electrographic seizures in neonatal rats in vivo. Bumetanide had no effect in the presence of the GABA(A)-R antagonist bicuculline".
- Dzhala, Brumback, Staley 2008, PMID 17918265: "This outward flow of Cl- in neonatal neurons is excitatory and contributes to a greater seizure propensity and poor electroencephalographic response to GABAergic anticonvulsants such as phenobarbital and benzodiazepines." (The statement is the authors' framing; the experiment itself tested phenobarbital: "Phenobarbital failed to abolish or depress recurrent seizures in 70% of hippocampi. In contrast, phenobarbital in combination with bumetanide abolished seizures in 70% of hippocampi".)
- Glykys et al. 2009, PMID 19755108: "Most neocortical neurons maintained higher [Cl(-)](i), and were excited by GABA(A)R activation. Phenobarbital had insignificant anticonvulsant responses in the neocortex until NKCC1 was blocked."
- Dzhala et al. 2010, PMID 20810895: "recurrent seizures progressively increase the intracellular chloride concentration ... and invert the net effect of GABA(A) receptor activation from inhibition to excitation ... These changes correlate with increasing frequency of seizure-like events and reduction in phenobarbital efficacy."

For (may worsen), benzodiazepine-specific:
- Nardou et al. 2011, PMID 21847371 (immature rat hippocampus, in vitro): "in contrast to PB, DZP aggravated propagating ILEs from the start, and did not prevent the formation of MF. PB reduced and DZP increased the network driven giant depolarizing potentials".
- Kasahara et al. 2019, PMID 31058209 (mouse febrile-seizure model): "high-dose administration of diazepam and pentobarbital exacerbated the behavioral and electrophysiological phenotypes of the induction phase of experimental FSs at P11 but not at P14. We further found that the deteriorated phenotypes at P11 were suppressed when NaK2Cl cotransporter isoform 1 (NKCC1) ... was blocked"; the authors add that sedation cannot be excluded as a contributor. Note the age: effect at P11, absent at P14 (relevant to a P13-P17 slice preparation).
- Thompson, Suchomelova, Wasterlain 2026, PMID 41705794 (review, Epilepsia Open): "drugs that activate GABAA receptors, like phenobarbital and midazolam, can make seizure-associated brain damage worse in newborn rats" (plain-language summary).

Against or limiting:
- Isaev et al. 2005, PMID 16000527 (P8-P12 rat in vivo, high K/low Mg): "Co-infusion of the GABA(A) receptor agonist isoguvacine or the GABA(A)-positive allosteric modulator diazepam completely prevented high-K(+)/low Mg(2+)-induced seizures." Diazepam was anticonvulsant in this immature model.
- Tyzio et al. 2009, PMID 19743469 (human infant Sturge-Weber cortex): "Spontaneous epileptiform activity ... was suppressed by glutamate receptor antagonists, the GABA(A) receptor agonist isoguvacine, and the positive allosteric GABA(A) modulator diazepam ... The NKCC1 antagonist bumetanide had little effect". Depolarising GABA is not universal in pathological tissue.
- Clinical NKCC1 block, both with phenobarbital (not a benzodiazepine): NEMO, Pressler 2015, PMID 25765333: "bumetanide as an add-on to phenobarbital does not improve seizure control in newborn infants who have hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy and might increase the risk of hearing loss"; "The trial was stopped early because of serious adverse reactions and limited evidence for seizure reduction" (open-label, n=14, no control). Soul 2021, PMID 33201535 (randomised, double-blind pilot, n=27 treated and 16 control): "Although definitive proof of efficacy awaits an appropriately powered phase 3 trial, this randomized, controlled, multicenter trial demonstrated an additional reduction in seizure burden attributable to bumetanide over phenobarbital"; baseline seizure burden was unbalanced ("much higher seizure burden in treatment versus control groups (median = 3.1 vs 1.2 min/h, p = 0.006)").
- So the neonatal-seizure refractoriness story is chiefly told for phenobarbital; the benzodiazepine-specific inference rests on Dzhala 2008's framing sentence, Nardou 2011, Kasahara 2019, and the 2026 review, and is contradicted in part by Isaev 2005 and Tyzio 2009.
- Not tested by any of these: the chloride state of a WWOX-deficient or any P13-P17 cortex (Breton et al. did not measure it; see R1.2).

## R3. Tonic (extrasynaptic, delta-containing) against synaptic GABA-A receptors; neurosteroid-type against benzodiazepine-site modulators

**Verdict: PARTLY. Receptor-pool preference of neurosteroids is SUPPORTED (ABSTRACT, non-epilepsy neurons). A behavioural difference from benzodiazepines in epilepsy models is shown only indirectly (tolerance in one rat study; synaptic loss with preserved tonic current in status epilepticus). No source compares a tonic-selective with a benzodiazepine-site modulator head to head in a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. Depth: ABSTRACT for all.**

1. Stell, Brickley, Tang, Farrant, Mody 2003, PMID 14623958: "at concentrations known to occur in vivo, neuroactive steroids specifically enhance a tonic inhibitory conductance in central neurons that is mediated by extrasynaptic delta subunit-containing GABAARs. The neurosteroid-induced augmentation of this tonic conductance decreases neuronal excitability." Shows preference of neurosteroids for the tonic delta pool; not an epilepsy model, not a comparison with a benzodiazepine.
2. Naylor, Liu, Wasterlain 2005, PMID 16120773 (lithium-pilocarpine status epilepticus, rat dentate granule cells; age not given in the abstract): "Unlike synaptic mIPSCs, the amplitude of extrasynaptic GABA(A) tonic currents is augmented during SE." and "These results provide a potential mechanism for the inhibitory loss that characterizes initiation of SE and for the pharmacoresistance to benzodiazepines, as a reduction of available functional GABA(A) postsynaptic receptors." This is the best support for "the synaptic pool, which benzodiazepines act on, is lost while the tonic pool is preserved or increased"; it does not test a tonic-selective drug.
3. Reddy and Rogawski 2000, PMID 11082461 (rat, pentylenetetrazol test): ganaxolone, a neuroactive-steroid analogue and "positive allosteric modulator of gamma-aminobutyric acid(A) receptors": "there is no tolerance to the anticonvulsant activity of ganaxolone nor is there cross-tolerance to ganaxolone when tolerance develops to diazepam. However, there is cross-tolerance to diazepam with chronic ganaxolone treatment." So the two classes behave differently (no tolerance to the neurosteroid on its own), but the cross-tolerance to diazepam means they are not independent systems. The abstract does not identify the receptor pool.
4. Knight et al. 2022 (Marigold), PMID 35429480, a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (CDKL5 deficiency disorder, randomised placebo-controlled phase 3, n=101): "median percentage change in 28-day major motor seizure frequency of -30.7% (IQR -49.5 to -1.9) in the ganaxolone group and of -6.9% (-24.1 to 39.7) in the placebo group (p=0.0036)". Somnolence was more frequent on drug. This addresses a neurosteroid in a DEE; it has no benzodiazepine arm and does not isolate the tonic mechanism; the abstract describes ganaxolone only as "an investigational neuroactive steroid". Transfer to WWOX-DEE is by analogy only. (Follow-up PMID 37950390: 13 of 37 discontinuations were for lack of efficacy over two years; abstract only.)

Additional (ABSTRACT): Peng et al. 2004, PMID 15456836 (pilocarpine mouse model of temporal lobe epilepsy): dentate delta-subunit labelling "decreased by 4 d after status epilepticus ... and remained low", and "physiological concentrations of the neurosteroid tetrahydrodeoxycorticosterone were less effective in reducing excitability in the pilocarpine-treated animals than in controls", i.e. the tonic pool and neurosteroid sensitivity can themselves be lost in chronic epilepsy. This limits any assumption that a tonic-selective drug keeps its effect.

Not found: a developmental-age (neonatal) comparison of neurosteroid against benzodiazepine on delta-containing receptors; delta-subunit expression in the P13-P17 cortex; any WWOX-related delta-subunit data.

## R4. Oligodendrocyte-lineage GABA-A receptors; held expression tables

### R4.1 Do OPCs and newly formed oligodendrocytes express functional GABA-A receptors in rodent cortex?

**Verdict: SUPPORTED for OPC (NG2 cells) in mouse somatosensory cortex; UNSUPPORTED (no source found) for newly formed oligodendrocytes. Depth: ABSTRACT.**

- Velez-Fort et al. 2010, PMID 20484634 (barrel cortex of NG2-DsRed mice, patch clamp): "the frequency of spontaneous and miniature GABAergic synaptic activity of cortical NG2 cells dramatically decreases after the second postnatal week ... However, NG2 cells still receive GABAergic inputs from interneurons in the adult cortex. These inputs do not rely on the presence of functional synapses but involve a form of GABA spillover. This GABA volume transmission allows interneurons to induce phasic responses in target NG2 cells through the activation of extrasynaptic GABA(A) receptors."
- Balia et al. 2013, PMID 24217990 (neocortical NG2 cells, single-cell RT-PCR and pharmacology): "NG2 cells ... express γ-aminobutyric acid A (GABA(A)) receptors" and "Single-cell RT-PCR and the effects of zolpidem and α5IA on evoked GABAergic currents reveal the predominance of functional α1- and α5-containing GABA(A)Rs at interneuron-NG2 cell synapses in the second postnatal week, while the α5 expression declines later in development when responses are exclusively extrasynaptic. ... γ2 ... is down-regulated in NG2 cells". This is the key source for a bath-applied benzodiazepine-site drug: zolpidem (a benzodiazepine-site agonist) alters the evoked GABA currents of cortical NG2 cells, so a bath-applied modulator is not neuron-only at about postnatal week 2. Caveat: the γ2 subunit that the benzodiazepine site needs is down-regulated later, so benzodiazepine-site sensitivity of OPCs at P13-P17 and beyond is expected to be reduced (reading of the abstract, not a stated result for P17).
- Orduz et al. 2015, PMID 25902404 (eLife): "NG2 cells of the developing somatosensory cortex form a transient and structured synaptic network with interneurons ... Fast-spiking interneurons, highly connected to NG2 cells, target proximal subcellular domains containing GABAA receptors with γ2 subunits ... connectivity peak at PN10". Trotter, Karram, Nishiyama 2010, PMID 20043946 (review): "NG2 cells express GABAA receptors and the AMPA subtype of glutamate receptors."
- Newly formed oligodendrocytes: I found no primary patch-clamp source showing functional GABA-A currents in newly formed (premyelinating) oligodendrocytes of rodent cortex. Two PubMed queries and one author-targeted query (Berger and colleagues, corpus callosum; PMID 1351952 surfaced but I did not open it) returned no such study. The RNA data below (R4.2) show GABA-A subunit transcripts in that class, which is not function.

## R5. Generic antiseizure efficacy of benzodiazepine-type modulators in DEEs; WWOX records

### R5.1 Evidence that benzodiazepine-type modulators reduce seizures in several DEEs irrespective of cause, with limits

**Verdict: PARTLY. Randomised evidence exists for one DEE syndrome (Lennox-Gastaut syndrome); "several DEEs irrespective of cause" is not shown by any source I read. Tolerance is documented in animals and is not prominent in the human extension data I read; "no developmental gain" is an absence of measurement, not a result. Depth: ABSTRACT.**

- Ng et al. 2011, PMID 21956725 (randomised, double-blind, phase III, Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, ages 2-60, n=238 randomised, 217 analysed): "Average weekly drop seizure rates decreased 12.1% for placebo vs 41.2% (p = 0.0120), 49.4% (p = 0.0015), and 68.3% (p < 0.0001) for the clobazam 0.25-, 0.5-, and 1.0-mg/kg/day groups". Class II evidence (the abstract's own label). Not stratified by cause in the abstract; endpoints are seizure rates and global assessments, with no developmental or cognitive endpoint in the abstract.
- Conry et al. 2014, PMID 24580023 (open-label extension, 267 patients): "86% still had that degree of drop-seizure reduction at Year 3 (and 14% lost their initial responses)"; "The mean modal clobazam dosage was 0.90 mg/kg/day at Year 1 and 0.97 mg/kg/day at Year 5, suggesting that study patients did not need significant increases in dosage over time." Uncontrolled, selected completers (188 of 267 completed). This is evidence for sustained effect, which weakens a strong tolerance premise in LGS.
- Tolerance, animal: De Sarro et al. 1996, PMID 8870036 (genetically epilepsy-prone rats): "Tolerance to the anticonvulsant activity developed most rapidly during clobazam treatment, less rapidly following diazepam treatment, and most slowly during clonazepam treatment." Amano et al. 2001, PMID 11508647 (kindled rat): tolerance to clorazepate and diazepam within days.
- Dravet syndrome: the North American consensus (Wirrell 2017, PMID 28284397) states that "Beyond the initial treatment with benzodiazepines and use of valproate, there was no consensus on the optimal in-hospital management of convulsive status epilepticus" and that strong consensus was reached on "first- and second-line therapies for seizures"; the abstract does not name clobazam or give an efficacy figure, so it does not support a clobazam-specific claim.
- "Irrespective of cause": no cited trial reports efficacy stratified by aetiology; LGS is a syndrome of mixed causes but the abstract gives no subgroup. UNSUPPORTED as stated.
- "No developmental gain": none of the abstracts above measures development as an outcome. This cannot be called a finding; it is an unmeasured endpoint. In the other direction, I did not retrieve a source showing a developmental benefit or harm.
