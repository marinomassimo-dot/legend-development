## Paper 1. Girgis 2024, J Epilepsy Res (PMID 38978529): cerebral folate transport deficiency in two children with intractable myoclonic epilepsy

`context_policy: QUESTION_DRIVEN` (held: the selection file's reason, SUBSTITUTE, folate readout). Surface: Europe PMC JATS, seven figures opened as images. Whole case report read.

**What it measures.** Two clinical cases with an exome-based diagnosis of a folate-receptor transport defect (FOLR1), brain imaging, EEG, and a reported seizure response to folinic acid.

**What it does not support (all about the disease class, not WWOX).**
- *A CSF folate-defined diagnosis.* CSF 5-methyltetrahydrofolate, the defining analyte, was not measured in either child; the diagnosis rests on exome sequencing and imaging.
- *A clean variant description.* The first child's variant is called a "homozygous nonsense pathogenic variant" while the protein change given (p.R128W) is a missense substitution.
- *A normal screen excludes the cause.* The routine metabolic screen in the first child (ammonia, lactate, acylcarnitine profile, urine organic acids, CSF glucose, protein, lactate) was normal while the FOLR1 diagnosis was genetically confirmed; the authors draw no wider inference, so that a routine screen does not exclude the disorder is read from this one child only.
- *A quantified treatment effect.* Seizures are "better controlled" with no counts, no repeat EEG and no CSF folate recheck; complete control was not reached.
- *Folic acid advice.* The statement that folic acid is contraindicated (it competes for the receptor) is a one-citation restatement (reference 6, a diagnosis-and-management paper the source does not label a review); class-level and not advice.

**System gap to the disease model.** A folate-receptor defect and WWOX loss of function are different genes with different tissues; the paper contributes only the screening-logic fact that a normal routine screen and no CSF folate leave cerebral folate transport deficiency unexcluded.

**Reading debts it carries.** None WWOX-direct.

## Paper 2. Papadopoulou 2021, JIMD Reports (PMID 34258135): cerebral folate deficiency in two siblings with FOLR1 variants, the ketogenic diet and early treatment

`context_policy: QUESTION_DRIVEN` (held: the selection file's reason, SUBSTITUTE, folate readout). Surface: Europe PMC JATS, two figures and the one-page supplement (Schema 1) opened as images. Whole report read. Publisher figure numbering is reversed (file g002 is Figure 1, file g001 is Figure 2), verified from the XML `<fig>` references and declared in the manifest.

**What it measures.** Two siblings with biallelic FOLR1 variants (one novel missense, class uncertain significance), CSF 5-MTHF at diagnosis, treatment with B6, folic and folinic acid and a ketogenic diet, and the seizure course of the older sibling in a supplementary schema.

**What it does not support.**
- *A diagnostic CSF cut-off.* The younger sibling's CSF 5-MTHF at diagnosis was 49 nmol/L, far above the under-5 typical of FOLR1 deficiency; the authors take this to mean moderately low CSF folate cannot separate a primary receptor defect from a secondary deficiency.
- *Comparable CSF values.* The older sibling's value (19 nmol/L) was drawn on oral folinic acid; the younger sibling's reference range is quoted two ways (63-122 at diagnosis, 44-122 at repeat).
- *A ketogenic-diet effect.* One child halved daily seizures (about 10 to 5) after the diet was added to two antiseizure drugs, in a course that fluctuated between 10 and 15 per day beforehand, with B6, folic acid and folinic acid changes overlapping in time (Schema 1: 15, 15, 10, 10, 15, 10, 10, 5, 5, 1, 1 per day across nine months).
- *A folinic acid effect.* The authors themselves write the benefit is hard to interpret because it was combined with the diet and drugs.
- *A folic acid effect.* Folic acid 1 mg daily is "beneficial according to the parents", in a child later shown to have a receptor defect. This sits against Paper 1's statement that folic acid is contraindicated: the two statements are not reconciled by either report.

**System gap to the disease model.** CSF 5-MTHF is the readout that defines this class and its reference range is laboratory-specific; a WWOX loss-of-function model has no folate measurement anywhere in the repository.

**Reading debts it carries.** None WWOX-direct.

## Paper 3. Lai 2025, Ann Child Neurol Soc (PMID 42563882): biotinidase deficiency as "a rare and treatable cause for generalized epilepsy"

`context_policy: QUESTION_DRIVEN` (held: the selection file's reason, SUBSTITUTE, biotin axis). Surface: Europe PMC JATS (a short clinical letter), Figure 1 from the Europe PMC supplementaryFiles container, opened as an image. Whole letter read.

**What it measures.** One adolescent with generalised epilepsy and biallelic BTD variants, treated with levetiracetam and biotin, with MRI of a cerebral-peduncle lesion at baseline, 8 and 20 months.

**What it does not support.**
- *The title.* The authors write that the relationship of the deficiency to her epilepsy is unknown; seizure freedom is on levetiracetam plus biotin.
- *A biochemical diagnosis.* The diagnosis of mild deficiency rests on exome sequencing; no enzyme activity or biotin level is given.
- *Attribution of the lesion regression to biotin.* Two treatments were given together and the pre-treatment seizure frequency is not stated; the three coronal T2 slices differ in level and contrast and carry no measurement.
- *Generalisation.* The sentence that treatment "can reverse identified lesions and prevent further complications" rests on one patient and a cited review.

**System gap to the disease model.** The letter is a reminder that biotinidase deficiency is a cofactor cause on a DEE work-up list; it adds no WWOX content.

**Reading debts it carries.** None.

## Paper 4. Ribeiro-Constante 2026, J Inherit Metab Dis (PMID 42660645): treating BCKD-kinase deficiency, a disorder of an overactive enzyme

`context_policy: QUESTION_DRIVEN` (held: the selection file's reason, SUBSTITUTE, BCAA axis, comparator for the WWOX-locus leucine/isoleucine association). Surface: the PMC article page fetched with an empty User-Agent (no Wayback capture, no Europe PMC XML for this 2026 deposit); four figures from the PMC blob store converted to PNG and opened as images; Tables 1 to 3 read. Whole review read. Publisher file numbering is not figure numbering (g003 is Figure 1, g002 is Figure 2, g004 is Figure 3, g001 is Figure 4), verified from the page order and the images. The page carries an all-rights-reserved notice; the local copy is evidence only.

**What it measures.** A narrative review of BCKDK deficiency (31 reported patients, 20 families): the kinase loss leaves BCKDH constitutively active, so branched-chain amino acids are depleted rather than accumulated; the treatment is a high-protein diet (2 to 4 g/kg/day) plus BCAA supplementation (100 to 250 mg/kg/day).

**What it does not support.**
- *Early treatment prevents autism.* The claim that none of three patients treated before age 2 developed autism rests on three patients found through sibling screening, which the authors call subject to ascertainment bias. The Figure 3 legend hedges it ("a small cohort requiring confirmation") but the figure's own box states a prevention rule ("Initiate before age 2 years to prevent autism manifestation").
- *Controlled outcome evidence.* All outcome data come from one retrospective cohort of 21 patients with variable follow-up; the authors grade the evidence "Level 4 (case series) at best"; 13/13 motor and 11/15 head-circumference figures are fractions of treated patients with follow-up.
- *Brain delivery.* The plasma-brain gap is shown only in Bckdk knockout mice and is "undemonstrated in humans".
- *The 31-patient total as an independent count.* It sums three reports (21 + six + four); Table 2's note says the 21 include patients from two earlier reports, and non-overlap was not tested here.
- *Treatments beyond diet.* Partial BCKDH suppression, BDK stabilisers, AAV and mRNA gene therapy, antisense and neuroprotective adjuncts are preclinical or conceptual for this disease; the Figure 4 legend says the adjuncts "have not been tested in BCKDK deficiency".
- Nothing about WWOX: the WWOX-locus leucine/isoleucine association named in the selection file is not discussed (0 occurrences).

**System gap to the disease model.** The comparator the selection file asked for exists in one respect only: an amino-acid panel can be a screening readout for a treatable class (low plasma BCAA, high-protein plus BCAA treatment), so the WWOX-locus leucine/isoleucine association (receipted by scientist-b) is at most a prior for an amino-acid panel in a WWOX biomarker list, not evidence that WWOX loss alters BCAA flux.

**Reading debts it carries.** None WWOX-direct.

## Paper 5. Nassar 2024, Sci Rep (PMID 39543202): L-carnitine and gastric emptying in children on a ketogenic diet, a randomised trial

`context_policy: QUESTION_DRIVEN` (held: the selection file's reason, SUBSTITUTE, carnitine axis). Surface: Europe PMC JATS; five tables, no figures. Whole trial read.

**What it measures.** Thirty children (12 months to 18 years) with drug-resistant epilepsy starting a ketogenic diet, 15 per arm, with or without L-carnitine 50 mg/kg/day for three months; the primary endpoint is antral length by ultrasound 15 minutes after a diet meal.

**What it does not support.**
- *A between-arm antral-length result.* Antral length rose significantly within the carnitine arm (P = 0.039) and showed no significant difference in the diet-only arm (P = 0.925); no between-arm test of antral length is reported, although the abstract frames it as a comparison with the non-supplemented group.
- *Gastric emptying.* The endpoint is one antral-length measurement, not an emptying time.
- *A carnitine-deficiency premise.* Serum carnitine was not measured; the laboratory methods list only a lipid profile.
- *Improved stool consistency.* The conclusion says so; Table 4 shows the Bristol comparison and the GI symptom score comparison as not significant, and only defecation frequency differed (+50 versus -33 percent, P = 0.001). The Discussion states "50% in comparison to 33%", omitting that the diet-only arm fell.
- *Seizure benefit.* Seizure severity fell in all patients and did not differ between arms (P = 0.097).
- *A clean lipid table.* Table 5 prints the pre-intervention HDL range as 32 to 40 mg/dl with a mean of 57.4 (and 38 to 60 with a mean of 63.4 after), which is impossible.
- *Prospective rigour.* The trial ran January to November 2023 and was registered in February 2024; blinding and placebo are absent; the limitations paragraph asserts external validity while naming no placebo and a short follow-up; controls were matched on sex, age and BMI Z-score after card-based randomisation.

**System gap to the disease model.** The ketogenic diet is the one diet widely used in the DEE class; this trial gives a carnitine co-supplement context and nothing about WWOX.

**Reading debts it carries.** None.

## Paper 6. Merlotti 2021, Int J Mol Sci (PMID 33406758): energy metabolism, ketogenic diets and skeletal health, a narrative review

`context_policy: QUESTION_DRIVEN` (held: the selection file's reason, SUBSTITUTE, bone/calcium/vitamin D axis; Wwox-null mice have bone defects). Surface: Europe PMC JATS; three figures from the Internet Archive capture of the PMC page via the PMC blob store, opened as images; Tables 1 to 6 read. Whole review read.

**What it measures.** A narrative synthesis of rodent ketogenic-diet bone studies (12-week diets, 6 or more mouse and rat studies tabulated), paediatric and adult human cohorts, athlete short-term trials, a speculation about GLUT1 deficiency and vitamin C, and a proposed 24-month bone-endpoint protocol.

**What it does not support.**
- *A conclusion about harm or benefit.* The authors conclude no adequately powered human study exists. The search is called PRISMA-guided but gives no search string and no record counts.
- *A tight fracture figure.* "Approximately 20%" of children on the diet for six or more years rests on one citation; the cohorts tabulated report 8.8 percent and about 7 percent fractures, a Dutch cohort with a non-significant BMD decrease (the text says "susceptible to further decreases"), and a Swedish 24-month modified Atkins diet cohort with no negative effect on growth or bone mass.
- *Isolating the diet.* Antiseizure drugs and immobility are named alongside the diet as causes of low bone density in children with intractable epilepsy.
- *Internal consistency.* Table 5 cites the Australian 29-patient cohort as reference 109 (the metformin mouse paper) while the text cites 119; reference 120 is 144 subjects in the text and 57 in Table 5; the text says a repeat DXA is not appropriate before 15 to 18 months while the Table 6 protocol places DXA at 12 months; a sentence attributes blood glucose to "glycerol released from triglycerides of lysine residue".
- *GLUT1 and bone.* The authors label the vitamin C hypothesis speculative; the only inborn-error data are three adult GLUT1-deficiency patients on the diet more than five years with no appreciable change.
- *Figure claims.* Figure 3 draws 3-beta-hydroxybutyrate acting on bone-specific miRNAs with a question mark; Figures 2 and 3 are schematics with no data.
- Nothing about Wwox or WWOX (0 occurrences).

**System gap to the disease model.** The review supports a class-level monitoring-parameter list (BMD and BMC with height adjustment, turnover markers with their least significant change, calcium, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, urinary calcium, kidney stones) for any child on a ketogenic diet; it is the authors' own proposal, not a validated protocol, and it adds no WWOX evidence. The WWOX bone prior (Wwox-null mouse bone defects) comes from other sources and is not touched here.

**Reading debts it carries.** None WWOX-direct.

## Paper 7. Nóbrega 2022, Front Neurol (PMID 36619921): cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis, a practice review

`context_policy: QUESTION_DRIVEN` (held: the selection file's reason, SUBSTITUTE, bile-acid axis). Surface: Europe PMC JATS; six figures from the Internet Archive capture of the PMC page via the PMC blob store, opened as images; Tables 1 to 3 read. Whole review read. The images attached to the Figure 1 and Figure 2 captions on the PMC page are swapped (g0001 shows the timeline, g0002 the bile acid pathway).

**What it measures.** A narrative review of CYP27A1 deficiency, its clinical timeline, Mignarri suspicion index, diagnostic criteria, MRI patterns and chenodeoxycholic acid treatment (retrospective cohorts and case series).

**What it does not support.**
- *Randomised evidence.* There is no placebo-controlled trial of chenodeoxycholic acid; the conclusion calls it safe and apparently effective on retrospective studies. A 56-patient cohort is reported with an absolute outcome (complete resolution in all treated before age 24) beside the review's own statement that imaging features are rarely reversible even with early diagnosis.
- *Consistent frequencies.* Chronic diarrhea is 31 percent in one paragraph, 76 percent in another and 40 to 50 percent in the timeline figure; cataracts 82, 85, 70 percent.
- *A consistent biomarker statement.* Section 5 says plasma cholestanol is normal or low; Section 3 says high cholestanol is the main diagnostic marker; the diagnostic criteria require raised cholestanol while a footnote concedes genetically proven cases with normal values.
- *A genotype-phenotype map.* The text says there is no clear genotype-phenotype correlation, yet Table 2 and the timeline assign c.1214G>A to spinal and to non-neurological CTX and c.1421G>A to classical and non-neurological CTX.
- *Figure-legend fidelity.* Figure 4 legend places cerebellar hyperintensity in FLAIR panels B to E, but panels D and E are slices above the cerebellum with no arrow.
- *Neurotoxicity of cholestanol.* Evidence is rats on a 1 percent cholestanol diet; the gene therapy is one liver-directed AAV in a mouse model.

**System gap to the disease model (screening logic only).** Epilepsy is a moderate (25-point) item in the Mignarri index against a 100-point trigger for cholestanol testing, and the index is described as more indicated for juvenile and adult patients; seizures in early infancy alone would not trigger CTX testing under it. No WWOX bile-acid record exists and this paper adds none.

**Reading debts it carries.** None.

## Paper 8. Agarwala 2023, Brain Commun (PMID 38074073): early-infantile DEE, aetiologies, phenotypic differences and outcomes, a prospective observational study

`context_policy: QUESTION_DRIVEN` (held: the selection file's reason, treatable causes). Surface: Europe PMC JATS; supplementary DOCX read after unpacking; eight figure JPEGs opened as images; Tables 1 to 4 read. Whole article read.

**What it measures.** A prospective cohort of 80 children with seizure onset before 3 months (EIDEE), single private hospital in Jaipur, 2016 to 2021, mean follow-up 30 months, with metabolic screening, MRI and stepwise genetic testing; aetiology groups are genetic (40, 50 percent), structural (15, 19), metabolic (11, 14; all vitamin responsive) and unknown (14, 17); molecular diagnosis in 53 of 77 tested (69 percent).

**WWOX-direct content (class level).** Three cohort members carry biallelic WWOX loss: two with the same homozygous nonsense variant c.790C>T (p.Arg264Ter) (seizure onset at 4 and 21 days; neuroimaging thin corpus callosum and normal) and one with a homozygous deletion within 16q23.1 (onset at 27 days; cerebral atrophy), found by array. That is 3 of 80 recruited (3.75 percent), 3 of 53 diagnoses (5.7 percent) and, with KCNQ2, the second most frequent gene group after STXBP1 (N = 5) in Figure 2. The authors write that WWOX "has been increasingly recognized as an important cause of EIDEE ... as was also seen in some of our patients."

**What it does not support.**
- *WWOX-specific outcomes.* Outcomes are by aetiological group, not gene: no seizure outcome, death, medication count or developmental outcome is reported for the three WWOX cases. Whether the two c.790C>T cases come from related families is not stated.
- *A WWOX incidence.* Three in 80 is a single-centre, referral-biased (private hospital, costs borne by families), male-predominant series that excluded children with acute metabolic crises and with infantile spasms as the first presentation.
- *A pathogenic-variant yield.* The 69 percent counts 10 variants of uncertain significance with a strong clinical match; the Figure 2 genetic group of 40 includes the two CNPY3 variants classed uncertain in Table 3.
- *Sequencing found everything.* Case 50's deletion is listed as found by array, a technique the Methods apply to patients undiagnosed after next-generation sequencing; per-case exome copy-number calling is not stated (an inference, hedged).
- *Group comparisons free of definition effects.* The unknown group requires non-response to a pyridoxine or P5'-P trial and vitamin-responsive status is defined by response, so "only vitamin-responsive aetiology predicts seizure control (P = 0.02)" is partly definitional; severe delay was assigned clinically to children who could not be tested and autism "may have been over-diagnosed".
- *Consistent reporting.* The Figure 5 legend gives the exome versus targeted-panel comparison as P = 0.2, the Results text P = 0.4; Figure 7's bar gives vitamin-responsive seizure control as 91 percent (10 of 11) against 9 of 11 in the text, and the Kaplan-Meier panel's quantity is not defined against the other panels.

**Answer to the work-up question (selection question 4, screening logic only).** The only treatable cofactor causes this work-up found were pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy (7), biotinidase deficiency (3) and pyridoxal phosphate deficiency (1): 11 of 80 (14 percent), a lower bound given the exclusions. The screen was basic (lactate, ammonia, blood tandem mass spectrometry for 49 disorders, 41 of 80 tested; no CSF, folate, creatine or CoQ10 testing described) and found only biotinidase deficiency, which clinical suspicion had already raised. A normal genetic result did not exclude B6 responsiveness: in three pyridoxine-dependent patients with normal genetics, stopping the vitamin led to recurrence. Folinic acid appears only as a named trial in the Introduction. So for a case of this class: a negative genetic result cannot stand in for a pyridoxine trial, and this paper says nothing about what a negative folate, creatine or CoQ10 screen excludes.

**Reading debts it carries.** Piard 2019 (the WOREE series the Table 1 rows cite for c.790C>T) has a manifest in the repository. Surana 2020 (PMID 33196034, an infantile-spasms aetiology cohort from the same setting) is cited in the WWOX deletion row and is proposed in the candidate (no free-standing queue id minted here).

## The plain statement

- **No WWOX evidence exists for any of the seven substitute axes** (folate, biotin, BCAA, carnitine, bone and calcium, bile acids, and the cofactor causes of DEE as a class): the seven substitute papers contain no WWOX occurrence. They state what a work-up for each class looks like; they are never evidence about WWOX.
- **One paper is not a pure substitute.** Agarwala 2023 contains three WWOX case rows in an unselected-by-gene early-infantile cohort: they are class-level WWOX-direct data (case phenotype, zygosity, detection route) with no WWOX-specific outcome.
- **Selection question 4 (cofactor causes a work-up excludes):** B6 (pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy, pyridoxal phosphate), and biotin (biotinidase) are the cofactor causes found by a work-up in this cohort; a normal genetic evaluation does not exclude B6 responsiveness; folate, creatine and CoQ10 were not tested in this cohort, and two folate-transport reports show a normal routine metabolic screen with no CSF folate leaves cerebral folate transport deficiency unexcluded.
- **Selection question 5 (a CSF folate readout):** yes in kind, CSF 5-MTHF, but its reference range is laboratory-specific and the one pair of siblings read here shows a value (49 nmol/L) far above the typical receptor-defect value; no WWOX folate measurement exists, so folate stays a pure substitute.
- **What would change this.** A WWOX-DEE cohort with CSF 5-MTHF, plasma amino acids and carnitine measured would turn any of these axes into evidence; the falsifier for "WWOX loss does not alter these cofactor axes" is a measured abnormal value in WWOX-null patients or models.

## Staged leads (not appended to the discovery ledger)

1. `IPOTESI` WWOX appears in about 4 percent of an unselected-by-gene early-infantile epileptic encephalopathy series (3 of 80; 3 of 53 diagnoses). Falsifier: other prospective, gene-agnostic EIDEE cohorts with a WWOX count well outside 1 to 8 percent. Source: Agarwala 2023.
2. `IPOTESI` A homozygous intragenic WWOX deletion can be missed by exome-based testing and found by array in a WWOX encephalopathy case. Falsifier: the cohort's exome CNV output for that case, or a WWOX series reporting exome detection of intragenic deletions. Source: Agarwala 2023, Table 4 and Methods (inference).
3. `IPOTESI` Neuroimaging at ascertainment can be normal in WWOX encephalopathy with early onset (case 8: onset day 21, normal neuroimaging entry). Falsifier: the primary report of this case or serial MRI. Source: Agarwala 2023, Table 1.
4. `IPOTESI` A normal genetic result must not stand in for a vitamin trial in early-onset epilepsy of unknown cause (three PDE patients relapsed on stopping pyridoxine after normal genetics). Falsifier: a larger series where genetics-negative patients do not relapse. Class-level, not advice.
