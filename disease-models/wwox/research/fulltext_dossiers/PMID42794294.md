## What it is

A PRISMA-style review of 15 human reports on iron status or prophylactic iron supplementation in infants and young children and neurodevelopmental outcomes, registered (per its Methods) as an undergraduate thesis and not in PROSPERO. Its conclusion: benefit in infants at increased biological risk (preterm, low birth weight, iron deficient, anaemic mothers), no benefit in healthy term infants with adequate stores, and possible long-term harm from high-dose iron in iron-replete infants (a "U-shaped" risk). WWOX: zero occurrences.

## WWOX content

None. **No WWOX-deficient evidence.** The population is iron-deficient or iron-replete otherwise typically developing infants; no child with a genetic encephalopathy is studied.

## What it says that bears on the iron-myelination question (DATO about the review)

- Iron is required for oligodendrocyte maturation and myelin formation (cited premise).
- One cited preterm DTI study (Li, n = 63) links iron deficiency to weaker cerebellar-thalamic connectivity and lower motor scores at 3 months; at 6 months there was no motor difference between groups.
- The benefit of supplementation is concentrated in iron-deficient or high-risk infants; effects in iron-replete infants are null, and in one Chilean formula cohort lineage negative.

## What it does NOT support (the negative is the product)

1. **No modifiability of hypomyelination by iron.** The one DTI-type study has no untreated comparison arm (groups defined by baseline anaemia and ferritin, all supplemented), and its motor difference disappears at 6 months. The review's Discussion nevertheless says the alteration "appeared reversible" and that iron deficiency leads to hypomyelination; Table 2 reports connectivity and motor scores, not myelin. Not WWOX evidence, and not even iron-myelin evidence of the kind the H1 question needs.
2. **The sample size is double counted.** The 7840 total equals the arithmetic sum of the fifteen Table 2 sample sizes (recomputed here), and that sum includes the 412-child neurocognitive subsample of the 3300-infant Bangladesh trial that is also a row; three rows come from the Chilean cohort lineage with different stated original cohorts (835, 1657, 1010) and no overlap statement; two Toronto rows are likely related. "15 studies" is fewer independent trials.
3. **The reported harm of iron in replete infants is thin.** One cohort lineage, one borderline coefficient (beta -0.13, p = 0.048, among many outcomes; a companion coefficient has p = 0.076), observational follow-up of a randomised formula comparison, and the review cites two references for the same result.
4. **Methods versus Figure 3 do not agree.** Methods say the search ran December 2025 to April 2026 with a strict 10-year publication window; the PRISMA figure says last search 15 May 2025, no limits on language or status. The exclusion reasons also differ between the Methods text and the figure (both sum to 255). Methods 2.1 and 2.4 disagree on older publications. A citation (SIBDI) is numbered differently in two places. Figures 1 and 2 carry an illustration-tool credit and are not data.
5. **Cerebral iron is asserted, not measured.** The statement that haemoglobin can be normal despite cerebral iron deficiency is supported only by two references that are trials, not brain-iron studies.
6. **Registration and design:** undergraduate-thesis registration, no meta-analysis, heterogeneity acknowledged by the authors.

## Design and limits

Review with PRISMA conduct claimed, six databases, no meta-analysis, risk of bias said to be assessed with RoB 2 and ROBINS-I but no per-study risk-of-bias result appears in Table 2.
