## What the paper did

A de novo SCN2A missense allele in the DIII S4–S5 linker was characterised before dosing: in silico
structural modelling, whole-cell voltage clamp in CHO cells (impaired inactivation, increased
persistent current), and dynamic action-potential clamp in an axon-initial-segment model (increased
firing, reduced rheobase). Elsunersen (PRAX-222), a 20-mer 2'-MOE 5-10-5 gapmer with mixed
phosphorothioate (n = 13) and phosphodiester (n = 6) linkages, allele-**non**selective, was then given
intrathecally. Safety surveillance: ECG, echocardiography, vitals, full blood chemistry and
coagulation, CSF cell count and protein, cranial ultrasound and serial MRI; later restricted to
monthly EEG and bloods. Population PK modelling of lumbar CSF and simulated cortical concentration
was fitted to measured CSF concentrations.

## What it measured — and the three numbers that do not agree

- Abstract: "19 intrathecal administrations over 20 months".
- Main text: seven doses to a cumulative 30.5 mg in the initial period, then "further ten dosages of
  8 mg … on a monthly basis" — seventeen administrations and ~110.5 mg.
- Extended Data Fig. 5 caption: "A total of 94 mg elsunersen (intrathecal) doses were administered …
  until the age of 21 months".

Three mutually inconsistent accountings of the exposure. This matters because the **dose schedule is
precisely the parameter a reader would transfer**, and it cannot be transferred from a record that
does not agree with itself. What is consistent and usable is the **interval**: seizure frequency and
severity rose 4–6 weeks after each administration, PK modelling showed little accumulation and
moderate retention, and the authors conclude redosing is needed every 4–6 weeks.

- Efficacy: status epilepticus of 7 weeks' standing, 20–25 seizures/hour, interrupted 8 days after the
  first dose and ultimately terminated; mean 19.7/hour in the first 12 weeks falling to 6.6/hour
  across weeks 13–35 (67 %); ~5–7/hour at 22 months.
- Safety: no severe or serious adverse events; no hydrocephalus (a named ASO class effect); no
  attributable ECG, echocardiographic or laboratory abnormality.

## What it does **not** say

- It does not claim causality: "Given that the data are based on one single patient, establishment of
  a definitive causal link will require further validation through formal clinical trials."
- It does not separate the ASO from concomitant high-dose sodium-channel blockers; phenytoin was
  reintroduced during the decline, and a later seizure rise was attributed to sepsis plus falling
  phenytoin levels.
- **It reports no developmental benefit.** The infant remained severely impaired at 18 months, and the
  authors underline "the need for future studies to explore whether elsunersen can contribute to
  neurodevelopment improvements beyond seizure control". Progressive cortical atrophy on serial MRI
  was attributed to the disease, not the drug.

## Transfer limit to WWOX-DEE (stated exactly)

**The mechanism does not transfer at all.** SCN2A-DEE here is gain of function and the drug is a
gapmer that degrades mRNA; WWOX-DEE is loss of function and knockdown is the wrong direction. The
paper itself notes SCN2A is intolerant of both haploinsufficiency and dosage increase, "suggesting a
small therapeutic window of medical knockdown strategies" — a constraint specific to a dosage-sensitive
channel gene.
**What transfers** is architectural, and only that: (i) mechanism confirmation *before* dosing, by an
assay that discriminates the variant's biophysics, as the entry condition for an individualised
oligonucleotide; (ii) the intrathecal route with a defined safety panel in an infant, including CSF
cell count and protein and surveillance for hydrocephalus; (iii) a redosing interval derived from
measured CSF concentrations rather than from convention; (iv) the expanded-access / named-patient
regulatory pathway as an existing precedent in a neonate. For a WWOX splice allele the analogue of (i)
is a splicing assay in patient-derived cells, which this repository does not yet have.
**And one honest warning:** seizure control without developmental benefit is the outcome this case
documents. Any WWOX therapeutic argument that treats seizure reduction as a proxy for developmental
outcome is contradicted by the closest existing precedent.
