## What the paper did

A retrospective two-centre cohort (2010-2020) of 36 EIMFS patients (one with onset at 9 months, admitted outside the stated criterion); 26 tested genetically (exome, 265-gene panel, mitochondrial genome, karyotype or CNV array), 17 with a causal call across 11 genes. Outcome tables by gene (Table 3), by MRI (Table 4) and by EEG pattern (Table 5). No statistics are computed.

## What it measured about WWOX

- **One patient (patient 10), male**, carrying **three** variants: WWOX initiator-codon `p.M1?` (classed LP), WWOX `p.H78Y` (classed VUS), and a hemizygous **ATP7A frameshift** `p.G865Dfs*5` (classed P). The authors describe low ceruloplasmin and hair and skin changes compatible with **Menkes disease** and "could not confirm whether it was a copathogenic gene".
- Table 3: the WWOX row and the ATP7A row are **the same patient** (footnote: one patient has two genetic mutations). Onset 3.5 months, focal seizures, abnormal MRI, no hypsarrhythmia or burst suppression, severe retardation, alive at 6 months; seizure control **ineffective** in the outcome columns while oxcarbazepine is listed under "effective drugs".

## What the paper states

- WWOX is among genes "associated with ineffective seizure control and poor prognosis" (Abstract, Conclusions) — a denominator of one patient who also has a pathogenic ATP7A variant.
- "Oxcarbazepine was effective for patients with SCN2A, ATP7A+WWOX, and PRRT2 mutations" — contradicted for this patient by Table 3's own seizure-control columns.

## What the paper does NOT say

- No WWOX RNA or protein test; no phase confirmation beyond parental origin; no separation of the WWOX and ATP7A contributions.
- No dose, timing or magnitude for any drug response.
