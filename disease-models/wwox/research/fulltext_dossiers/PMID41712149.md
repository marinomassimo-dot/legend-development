## What the review states that bears on the assigned question

**Cargo and cassette.** The AAV genome limit is given as ~4.7 kb and the human *SCN1A* open reading
frame as >6.0 kb, so single-vector replacement is impossible. Two structural answers are described:
a **split-intein dual-vector** *SCN1A*, combined with interneuron-specific enhancers, which improved
postnatal survival and reduced febrile and spontaneous seizures in two mouse models; and
**gene-regulation** instead of replacement — dCas9 fused to VP64 or VPR to upregulate *Scn1a* in
GABAergic neurons, and ETX101, an AAV9 delivering an engineered transcription factor under a
GABAergic-neuron-specific enhancer (REGABA) that drives the endogenous wild-type allele.

**The developmental window.** The strongest statement in the review is not from a vector at all. A
conditional knock-in mouse (*Scn1a*^Stop/+) allowing on-demand reactivation of the gene after
symptoms had appeared (P30) gave "complete rescue of both spontaneous and thermally induced
seizures", amelioration of behaviour, normalisation of fast-spiking interneuron firing and reversal
of widespread gene-expression abnormalities including those linked to astrogliosis — and
**reactivation at P90, in adulthood, also restored physiological NaV1.1 levels and rescued seizures
"despite months of prior seizures"**. Against that, the review's clinical argument runs the other
way: natural-history studies (BUTTERFLY, ENVISION) associate higher early seizure burden and delayed
effective treatment with poorer cognitive and adaptive outcomes, and ETX101-treated children dosed
before two years are reported to have accelerated cognitive skill acquisition from 16 weeks.

**Dose and its two-sided risk.** ETX101: 19 children aged 6 months to 7 years, single
intracerebroventricular dose by stereotactic neuronavigation, staggered, four dose levels; no
treatment-related serious adverse events; dose-dependent and sustained seizure reduction at the
first three levels, including a median 78% reduction at the third level over up to 7 months. The
review's own balance sheet for both gene-based routes names "dose-dependent immune responses,
vector-related toxicity, and uncertainties regarding long-term expression and neurodevelopmental
effects following early-life intervention", and for the regulation route a "theoretical risk related
to excessive or off-target transcriptional activation that will require careful dose selection and
prolonged surveillance".

**The immune and CSF cost of intrathecal dosing in children** is quantified for the antisense
programme, which is the only modality here with substantial paediatric exposure. Across MONARCH and
ADMIRAL, 81 children received at least one intrathecal dose of zorevunersen; ~30% had drug-related
treatment-emergent adverse events, most commonly asymptomatic CSF protein elevation and procedural
vomiting; drug-related CSF protein elevation was 11 of 81 (~14%) in MONARCH/ADMIRAL and ~27% (20 of
74) in the open-label extensions; **when all CSF measurements were reviewed irrespective of
attribution, transient elevations above 50 mg/dL occurred in approximately three-quarters of
patients** in the extensions, without clinical symptoms and with one discontinuation. Serious
adverse events occurred in 22% but were largely unrelated; one suspected unexpected serious adverse
reaction was attributed to the drug; three serious events related to the collection or
administration procedure; one death from SUDEP, deemed unrelated.

**Preclinical NHP comparator.** A single intracerebroventricular dose of ETX101 was "well tolerated
over 28 days, with no serious adverse events or notable histopathological changes" — 28 days, in a
field where the cited DRG literature puts peak severity at one to five months.

## What the review does NOT state

- **It does not transfer to a recessive null, and does not say so.** Dravet is >95% *SCN1A*
  haploinsufficiency with an intact wild-type allele. Every modality the review treats as most
  advanced — TANGO antisense upregulation, dCas9 transcriptional activation, ETX101's engineered
  transcription factor, the *Scn1a*^Stop/+ reactivation experiment, and the *Scn8a*-ASO
  rebalancing — **requires an intact allele or an endogenous locus to act on**. For a biallelic null
  there is nothing to upregulate. Only the split-intein dual-vector replacement route is structurally
  available, and that is the one route for which the review reports no human data at all.
- It gives no dose in vg or vg/kg for any gene-therapy programme, and no dose-scaling method.
- It gives no immunogenicity numbers for ETX101 — immunogenicity is listed as something the trials
  monitor, not as a result.
- The ETX101 human results are attributed to a single reference and, by the review's own stated
  search cut-off of 31 March 2025, cannot be a peer-reviewed phase I/II report; the review does not
  say what kind of source they are. The 19-child figure, the 78% median and the cognitive-acquisition
  claim are therefore review-level statements awaiting a primary.
- It reports no cognitive or developmental endpoint that has been placebo-controlled for any
  gene-based therapy: EMPEROR (zorevunersen, sham-controlled phase III) is underway and ETX101's
  phase III studies "will commence in 2026".
- It says nothing about *WWOX*, and nothing about any recessive loss-of-function DEE.

## Caveats the review itself gives

Long-term safety is unresolved; the phenotypic spectrum is broad; advanced therapies have efficacy
and accessibility problems; early antisense benefits "require confirmation in the EMPEROR RCT";
ETX101's disease-modifying effect needs phase III confirmation; the mechanism is contested, with
mouse strain and developmental stage suggesting an excitatory-neuron contribution and human iPSC
studies producing "conflicting data"; the ataluren trial in Dravet (n = 7, 12 weeks) was negative and
the authors attribute that to size and duration rather than to the drug; small case series (n ≤ 4)
reporting benefit from contraindicated sodium-channel blockers "do not justify routine use".

## Caveat found by reading the declarations

Both of the programmes the review presents most favourably are commercially sponsored by companies
with which the authors declare relationships: the senior author lists Encoded Therapeutics (ETX101)
as a scientific-advisory-board, travel-funding, investigator and consulting relationship and Stoke
Therapeutics (zorevunersen) as a speaker-honoraria, travel-funding and consulting relationship,
among a long list; the first author declares advisory-board and honoraria relationships with other
sponsors. The review is open access, declares its funding and conflicts fully, and the disclosure
does not make the statements wrong — but every efficacy figure here is second-hand and sits inside
that disclosure.
