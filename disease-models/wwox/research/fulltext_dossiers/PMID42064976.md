## What it is

A retrospective single-centre chart review of 60 children (2-18 years) with drug-resistant epilepsy who stayed on IVIG for at least a year, autoimmune encephalitis excluded: seizure-frequency category scores at baseline, 3 and 12 months by etiology, seizure type and concurrent medication changes, plus adverse effects. 46.7 percent had a genetic etiology and 16 a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (Lennox-Gastaut, Dravet). WWOX: zero occurrences; no WWOX-related child is identifiable (no gene-level data).

## WWOX content

None. **No WWOX-deficient evidence.** This is the nearest clinical paper to the H2 question for genetic DEE, and it does not name genes.

## What it measures (DATO)

22 of 60 (36.7 percent) had at least a 50 percent reduction by a year and eight were seizure free; the repeated-measures reduction was significant for generalized seizures (p = 0.019) and for the unknown-etiology subgroup (p = 0.03) but not for the genetic subgroup; no effect at 3 months; 88.3 percent reported no adverse effects.

## What it does NOT support (the negative is the product)

1. **No evidence for IVIG in genetic DEE.** The genetic subgroup (28 children) showed 42.9 percent at least 50 percent reduction by count (chi-square versus etiology p = 0.41, no significant etiology effect) yet "no significant seizure frequency reductions ... across time", and the conclusion still advises considering IVIG early "particularly among patients with genetic or unknown etiologies". The abstract's "effective" and the conclusion contradict the paper's own genetic-subgroup result. No gene is named, so nothing can be said for a WWOX class.
2. **No comparator.** Uncontrolled, retrospective, unblinded scoring from charts; spontaneous fluctuation, regression to the mean after a severe baseline, concurrent changes in antiseizure drugs and the placebo-like effect of a monthly infusion are not separated. The authors say causality cannot be established.
3. **Survivorship selection.** Only children who stayed on IVIG at least a year are analysed; nine who stopped early (some for ineffectiveness) are excluded after the fact; 13 more stopped after a year. The reported 36.7 percent therefore overstates the response among all treated children (counting all nine early stoppers as non-responders the figure would be 22 of 74, 29.7 percent; arithmetic from the paper's own counts).
4. **Arithmetic inconsistency in the response breakdown.** The text lists 22 (at least 50 percent), 2 (under 50 percent) and 23 (no change), 47 children; Table 4 puts 38 in the "under 50 percent or no change" group (63.3 percent). Thirteen children are unaccounted for in the text (the same number as those who stopped after a year; the paper does not say; INFERENZA).
5. **Statistics on an ordinal scale.** The 0-8 category scale (each step roughly a three-fold change in frequency) is analysed with repeated-measures ANOVA; many subgroup p-values (generalized 0.019, unknown 0.03, stable ASM 0.045) are uncorrected, and the effect in the genetic group is about 0.6 category steps.
6. **The medication paradox is read generously.** A significant effect only among children whose antiseizure drugs were stable or reduced is offered as indirect support, though escalation in the other group could equally mark a worse course.
7. **Dates and counts do not line up** (74 treated against 75 on the dashboard, Methods endpoint February 2021 against a dashboard end date of 26 May 2022, 3,650 against 3,850 patients in the database, 69 versus 60 analysed in the Results text).
8. **Adverse-effect reporting is chart-based** and lists only mild symptoms; no systematic search for known serious infusion-related events (the paper does not state it looked).

## Design and limits

Single centre, no control, selection by retention, ordinal outcome with ANOVA, dose and interval individualised, one author group's own earlier cohort partly overlapping the setting.
