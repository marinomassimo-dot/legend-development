## 1 · The brief's title for this paper is wrong on the one word that matters

The Orchestrator's selection card titles this *"…clinical implications for **intrathecal**
delivery"*. The article's actual title is:

> "Variability in the circulation of cerebrospinal fluid: causes and clinical implications for
> **intraventricular** drug delivery"

and the abstract confirms the scope: "macromolecular pharmaceuticals designed to be delivered
directly into **the ventricles of the brain**". Intraventricular (ICV, via an Ommaya reservoir),
intrathecal (lumbar puncture) and intracisternal (ICM) are three different routes with different
access procedures, different first-pass tissue and different clearance paths; group C contains one
human ICM case (PMID 41966056), several lumbar IT studies, and this ICV review. **The group's own
framing silently merged them.** This is the second premise correction of the group and the most
consequential, because route is the variable the group was assembled to separate from dose.

The review is still relevant — its physiology governs any CSF-route dose — but every statement in it
is scoped to ICV unless it says otherwise.

## 2 · What the paper is

A narrative review of CSF production, pathway anatomy, flow dynamics and clearance, with emphasis on
human data and comparisons to laboratory species, written for people delivering macromolecules and
AAV capsids into the ventricles. It has 7 tables and 6 schematic figures. It contains **no new
measurement**; every number is attributed to a cited primary.

## 3 · The quantitative core: Table 4, and why it cannot set an infant dose

> **TABLE 4 — Average brain values for four commonly studied species**
>
> | Species | Brain volume (cm³) | External brain surface area (cm²) | CSF volume (cm³) | Ventricular surface area (cm²) | Total surface area*/brain volume (cm⁻¹) |
> |---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
> | Mouse | 0.3 | 3 | 0.04 | 0.5 | 11.7 |
> | Rat | 2.2 | 20 | 0.15 | 1 | 9.5 |
> | Macaque | 90 | 200 | 15 | 8 | 6.8 |
> | Human | 1400 | 1850 | 140 | 32 | 1.3 |
>
> *External brain surface area plus ventricular surface area; values are approximate.

Two things follow, and the second is the finding.

**(a) It corroborates the scaling factor an actual programme used.** PMID 41948127 scaled its mouse
dose to macaques **371-fold** "for the increased CSF volume". Table 4's CSF volumes give
15 / 0.04 ≈ **375** — independent agreement to within ~1%, which is as close as two
order-of-magnitude physiological compilations get. Macaque→human by the same scalar is
140 / 15 ≈ 9.3×. The table is internally coherent: CSF volume rises monotonically across the four
species and the surface-area-to-volume ratio falls monotonically, as it must.

> **Transcription note, recorded because it nearly produced a false finding.** My first pass read
> this table from a flattened text rendering of the JATS in which adjacent numeric cells are
> concatenated without a delimiter — `Rat2.2200.1519.5`. That string admits several readings, and
> the one I first took made the rat's CSF volume exceed the macaque's, which I was about to report
> as an error in the source. Re-extracting the table **by cell boundary** from the XML showed the
> row is `2.2 | 20 | 0.15 | 1 | 9.5` and the source is correct. The defect was in my extraction, not
> in the paper. Numeric tables must be read cell-wise, never from flattened running text.

**(b) There is exactly one "human" row, with no age.** 1400 cm³ of brain and 140 cm³ of CSF are
adult values. A neonate's and an infant's CSF volume and brain mass are substantially smaller, and
the review itself says so in prose — "In newborns and children, the dynamics of CSF stroke volume
change rapidly with age, primarily due to skull growth" — before deferring the detail to
**Supplementary Material 2**, which is not in the body. **So the only cross-species scaling table in
group C cannot be used to convert any NHP dose into an infant dose.** The paediatric numbers that
would be needed are, in this source, outside the body text.

## 4 · What it states that bears on the question

- The factors expected to dominate inter-subject variability of CSF macrocirculation, and therefore
  of intraventricular drug delivery, are — in the authors' synthesis — **overall CSF production,
  anatomical pathway variation, and age**.
- CSF dynamics vary with **circadian cycle, posture and musculoskeletal movement, age and sex**
  (more pronounced in men than women, independent of age or height, per a cited primary), and with
  **anaesthesia**.
- Non-paediatric trials "typically include male and female subjects ranging in age from 18 to
  65 years, **with the same drug dose and schedule being used across patients**", whereas for
  intraventricular therapies "dosing considerations may need to include factors such as the patency
  of the pathways and estimated ventricular volume (at a minimum), much as a pediatric dosing
  schedule is adjusted according to weight". **This is the review stating plainly that fixed dosing
  is the current practice and that it is inadequate for a CSF route.**
- Particle size is not the limiting factor: the foramina of Luschka are "40,000 times larger than an
  AAV capsid"; pial stomata of ~1 μm are "40 times the size of an AAV capsid". What governs
  distribution is flow, surface adhesion and arachnoid membranes, not aperture.
- Human ICV kinetics, cited: a radiolabelled monoclonal antibody given as a single Ommaya injection
  distributed throughout cranial and spinal subarachnoid space **within 4 h**, and over the cerebral
  convexity **within 24 h**.
- Large molecules given into the ventricles "move more slowly and/or incompletely into the SAS".
- Cited claim relevant to the contested question: **"CSF-administered or peripheral-blood-administered
  viral vectors are known to present a risk of inducing DRG toxicity … which tends to be most severe
  and consistent in the lumbosacral DRGs in non-human primate studies."** This matches
  PMID 41210171's representative panel being a **sacral** DRG. Cited to three primaries, none read.
- Cited: AAV particles given intraventricularly are "transported throughout the brain, with an
  expression profile comparable to intracisternal injection".
- **Patient-measurable delivery readouts the review names:** phase-contrast MRI (offered as
  potentially "decisive" for trial eligibility), radionuclide ventriculography/cisternography, and
  **AAV9 capsids labelled with PET isotopes (carbon-11, fluorine-18, zirconium-89) for noninvasive
  quantitative biodistribution in vivo**. The last is the only route in group C to answering "how
  much of the dose reaches the target" in a living patient rather than at necropsy.
- Exclusion logic: "Patients with aqueductal stenosis (as a simple example) would not be candidates
  for intraventricular viral vector therapy for a spinal cord disease."

## 5 · What it does NOT state

- It supplies **no** AAV-specific pharmacokinetics of its own, and no dose recommendation.
- It gives **no** paediatric CSF volumes, stroke volumes or dosing arithmetic in the body.
- It does not address intrathecal lumbar or intracisternal delivery except in passing.
- It does not quantify how much of an administered dose reaches any target; the question is posed,
  not answered.
- It makes no claim about WWOX, epileptic encephalopathy, or any specific disease beyond the
  examples in Table 7 (leptomeningeal disease, Alzheimer's, Huntington's, ALS, spinal cord injury).

## 6 · Caveats and the reading posture this source requires

The brief's own warning was apt — "reviews of physiology are where plausible numbers acquire false
precision". Table 4 is labelled "approximate" and is sourced to three secondary compilations, none
of them a measurement paper; its rows are consistent with each other and with an independent
programme's scaling factor (§3a), but that is coherence, not validation. **No number taken from
Table 4 may be promoted without reading the primaries behind it.** Table 4's entries are recorded
here as the review's rendering, not as measured values.

## 9 · Verdict

**INGEST** — as a framing and method source, not an evidentiary one. Its contribution to the
question is to establish that "dose" at a CSF route is a distribution rather than a number, that
age is among the top three drivers of that distribution, and that PET-labelled capsid is a route to
measuring delivery in a living patient. Its paediatric content is in a supplement that was not
retrieved, and the route in its title is **not** the route the brief ascribed to it.
